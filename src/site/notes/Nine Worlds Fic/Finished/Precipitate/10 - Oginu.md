Travelling for work with Ser Rhodin was proving to be an education in food. Dinner had been a series of elaborate and tiny raw fish dishes, which Oginu had found surprisingly delicious. After they had gelato from a stall that specialised in unusual flavour combinations. Ser Rhodin had saltbush, tamarind, and mandarin; Oginu had pepperberry and guava.

The hotel had been sumptuous, and Ser Rhodin and Oginu had been given adjoining rooms. It was the first time Oginu had ever slept alone, unless you counted camping. The bed was large enough for three, possibly even four. Someone even delivered a hot towel for shaving in the morning!

Breakfast they eat walking through the fruit market. Ser Rhodin walks up to a stallholder, engages in a little talk about their produce, buys two pieces and asks for it to be prepared. Then they eat it off banana leaves with bamboo skewers instead of forks. There are plenty of fruits Oginu has never had fresh, and some he has never heard of at all, including _taun_ which looks like a lychee and tastes more like a mango. 

After they wash their sticky hands and faces, Ser Rhodin says, “Let's see if the elders have decided in our favour.”

They amble back through the city, towards Sayo Mdang's uncle's shop. Some stores are already well open, and some are still shut. It seems that in Gorjo city things happen when they are ready. Perhaps it is because they don't have the Palace bells ordering them about. It seems like it would be more relaxed, but used to everything happening in sync, Oginu finds himself a little tense.

When they arrive, it is just the old man who ogled them, and one other. They are sitting at a backgammon board, but either have already put the pieces away or have yet to get them out. Both of them turn to look at the two guards.

“I'll let Lazo explain,” says the non-ogler. Now looking closely, Oginu can see some similarities between this man, Sayo Lazo, and Sayo Mdang. Presumably also a relative then. “He'll be down soon.”

Ser Rhodin bows respectfully to the two men, Oginu following a moment later. It makes Oginu wonder if one of them was the god Ser Rhodin mentioned. Neither of them look anything like the gods of Oginu's people, though they both have the confident and piercing look of elders. He supposes there's no reason gods have to look the same to everyone, after all the Shaian Sun looks like the Emperor (or does the Emperor look like the Sun?), and the Moon Lady is unearthly pale.

It is indeed not long after that Sayo Lazo arrives and unlocks the door to his shop. He wanders over to them, as the two old men set up another game.

“Yes, with conditions,” says Sayo Lazo, holding up a hand to forestall any questions. “This is not my lore, it belongs to the Nevan,” he says calmly, “The lonà hold it, and it has never before been given to velioi; they will probably at least want one of their own to escort you.”

“Thank you,” says Ser Rhodin, respectfully, “For the news and the advice.”

They watch the sun rise a little higher, as Sayo Lazo sets up his shop and greets his first customer of the day. The backgammon players chortle to each other, but Oginu ignores that. Instead, he admires the view, watching boats come in with catches of fish, or visitors. There is a brief wave of children through the streets, who must be heading to school. From somewhere drifts the sound of a flute and a drum, and he wonders who might be playing. A rehearsal for an evening show? A lesson for a child? Or perhaps just friends jamming together.

Eventually, Oginu spots two old women and a middle-aged man winding their way towards them. When they get close, one of the women calls out, “Tovo! How many fit on your little vaha?”

“Four, if nobody gets into an argument,” says Sayo Tovo, who it turns out is the man who told them to wait for Sayo Lazo. “Are you telling me you don't have a sail to spare, lonà-tai?” he asks, with a laugh.

“I have sails a-plenty but no spare masts. My apprentices are all in Ve'ea, as you well know, Tovo,” she says, but her face is smiling, “Will you take those two strapping young lads and Toucan? The island is in the north-west of the Epalos, Toucan knows it.”

Sayo Tovo stretches, and stands up from the game. “You'll have to finish the game for me then,” he says. Then he gives the other old man a long, lingering, kiss. 

When they break it off, the old woman grunts, “You'd think you two were still teenagers, the way you carry on. It's barely a day's sail.” She takes the chair that Sayo Tovo has vacated.

Ser Rhodin bows to the group, “We have been told there will be conditions, Saya Lorekeeper, would you be able to tell us what they are?”

She gives him a smirk, and waves at the man who came wih her. “Toucan has to go with you. If you're planning on coming back the same way, then he'll come with you on the way back too. There's part of the path you'll have to go blindfolded. You can't tell anyone else about the path, except I suppose your Lord Magus who is probably spying on you anyway.”

Oginu looks at the middle-aged man, Toucan. He's well-dressed, and smiling, giving more of an air of successful businessman or enthusiastic academic than adventurer. At least the old man, Sayo Tovo, with his grass skirt hand hand-made bag, looks like he sprung up out of the islands.

Ser Rhodin turns to Sayo Toucan, “Thank you for agreeing to guide us, sir. I am Ser Rhodin, and this is Oginu. I don't know how long we will be gone, time moves differently between Zunidh and Alinor these days. If it helps smooth things with your family or workplace, I can offer you a letter explaining you are helping the Imperial Guard with an investigation?”

Sayo Toucan grins, “Ghilly wants Kip back safe as much as I do, we've all been friends for years. She'll probably be more angry that there's not enough space on the vaha for her. But a request for indefinite leave of absence from the courthouse *would* go down much smoother with a formal letter.”

With a nod, Ser Rhodin opens his bag, and pulls out an envelope and a pen. Taking a corner of the backgammon table, he unfolds the letter and looks up expectantly. “What name should I make it out to?”

“Toucan Navan is my name, and it would be best addressed to the Chief Justice,” says Sayo Navan.

Ser Rhodin scribbles with the pen for a moment, and then shakes the paper to dry it faster. Oginu can see the full seals of the Imperial Guard at the bottom, and wonders when Ser Rhodin made up the document. Or perhaps he always carries around such things?

After folding it, he hands it to Sayo Navan, with a flourish. The other man laughs, and takes it. “I'll need to fill in some paperwork at the courthouse, and then pack at home. I can meet you back here around noon?”

“Excellent!” says Ser Rhodin, and then turns to Oginu, “I'll wrap up at the hotel, and take the reports to the Spire. Since you claimed some association with sailing, Oginu, why don't you help Honoured Elder Tovo prepare?”

“Yes, sir,” says Oginu, and salutes. The elders seem to find this exchange amusing. Oginu turns to Sayo Tovo, and waits for instructions. 

After a very long inspection by Sayo Tovo, he asks, “You can sail, boy? What kind?”

“We called them 'sampan', but the only word I know in Shaian for it is 'boat', sir. Narrow, long, with a flat bottom for carrying goods, one sail with seven battens. I sailed it along the coast with my brother-in-law, a trader,” said Oginu, nervously, “It's been a long time, sir.”

“Can you swim?” asks Sayo Tovo, one eyebrow raised.

“Yes, sir,” says Oginu. That's a skill he's maintained much more than sailing. He can swim in rivers, or lakes, or even the big public bath in Solaara.

“Well, at least you won't panic if you fall off then,” says Sayo Tovo, “Come on boy, help me make space for the amount of overpacking Toucan and Rhodin will do.”

Oginu does as he is told, lifting and carrying, tying and testing. They have none of the same words for the knots Sayo Tovo wants, but once he points them out, Oginu knows them. Eventually they have repacked efficiently enough that Oginu thinks three of the guard packs would fit. He hopes that Sayo Navan doesn't want to bring too much more.

They are, it turns out, finished with plenty of time before noon. So Sayo Tovo takes the time to grill Oginu on the vaha, or at least what he can while she is moored. Different ways the sail can be set, where you can stand to balance or turn, and what every part of her is made of. This last part he finds particularly interesting, having never much paid attention to boat construction as a boy.

When the sun is directly overhead, Oginu sees Ser Rhodin and Sayo Toucan appear at the railing above. He waves, and the two of them trot down the stairs to the vaha. Ser Rhodin is slightly nervous, Oginu can tell, but trying to hide it. Sayo Navan, who has actually packed a bag about half the size of the guard ones, looks positively buoyant.

Ser Rhodin has brought them all a lunch of rolls stuffed with crab. Both the bread and the crab are very fresh, and Oginu savours every bite. When they get back to Solaara, he'll have to pay more attention to Ser Rhodin's food recommendations, he clearly knows how to spot the best.

Out of the berth, Oginu learns that Toucan also knows how to sail. Ser Rhodin sits in the very centre of the platform of the little outrigger canoe, and tries to stay out of everyone else's way. Sayo Tovo takes the tiller, which is also the job of telling everyone else what to do. Once they're clear of the harbour, with a course set, they can relax a little.

Without thinking very much, Oginu does what he always did with his brother-in-law. He starts singing one of the fishing songs. It's meant to call the fish into the nets, although he's always thought it's more about keeping the rhythm of the throw and haul. He's been singing for a few verses before he turns around and notices the others staring at him.

“Sorry,” he says awkwardly, and fidgets with a rope-end.

“You have quite a lovely singing voice,” says Sayo Navan, “Much deeper than your speaking voice.”

Oginu hunches his shoulders. His voice is adequate to a work-song, nothing more. “It was just a something we used to do, when we went out fishing.”

Sayo Navan nods, and starts singing something back. Oginu doesn't understand the words, it must be in a local language. He can feel something in it though, something of the way the vaha moves over the waves, or the sail cracks in the winds. After a verse Sayo Nava stops and smiles, “Most of our lore and traditions are passed through dance and song,” he says, “Like that one about tacking across the wind.”

Sayo Tovo snorts. “And do you remember the rest of it, boy?” he says.

“Yes, Buru Tovo,” says Sayo Navan, “I might not remember all the lays like Kip, but I do remember _some_ things.”

For the rest of the afternoon, they trade songs. Ser Rhodin doesn't join in, but he does listen.

As the light fades and the stars come out, Oginu notices Sayo (or is it Buru? is that a title or a name?) Tovo measuring the sky with his hands. He grunts, and adjusts the tiller slightly. Oginu didn't realise how large the rig of the Vangaveye-ve is, that you could be out of sight of land for so long.

Ser Rhodin stirs, and pulls the finding charm from his pocket. After holding it for a few moments, he nods and puts it back.

"Don't trust me to navigate as well as your mage, hmm?" asks Sayo Tovo, looking at Ser Rhodin sharply.

"Not at all, sir," says Ser Rhodin, "Just checking Cliopher hasn't moved. This is supposed to take us to the closest portal to him, but of course if he were moving, on a boat maybe, that could change."

Sayo Tovo nods, "Kip's never been one for staying still."
