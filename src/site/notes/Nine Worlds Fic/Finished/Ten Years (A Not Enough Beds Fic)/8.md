Once Sayo Mdang had left, you let yourself cry, silently. In your bed, with the curtains drawn, was one of only two places you could cry at all. 

You had begun to wake up slowly, not opening your eyes. Finding yourself touching someone, holding their hand, and you had thought that maybe it was one of your friends. But the weight and pain of the taboos was there, in every fibre of your body, and your magic was buried deep. You had opened your eyes to find yourself in the Imperial bed. 

Slowly, so slowly, your mind had added up the circumstances. The realisation had filled you with terror, like *you* were on fire. It was entirely unconscious, throwing his arm. You had shaken like a leaf, barely suppressed a scream into a squeak.

But your reaction had, entirely naturally, scared Sayo Mdang. 

He had probably spent the entire night waiting for you to wake up and demand more than just his arm. Fear would be entirely reasonable. But if he had felt resignation, well, just the possibility made *you* feel worse. Nobody should be prepared to accept the kinds of abuses of power your uncle had made habit. And yet, what else could they do? What other options had Sayo Mdang had available?

You had wanted to be different. You had removed every last crumb of your uncle you could find in the Imperial Apartments. But his spirit lived on, in the expectations of all those around you. Probably even in the mind of Sayo Mdang.

There was no way now that anyone would believe he was just your secretary. It would not matter what you said, what he said. What would the risk be now, of someone trying to assassinate him? Or kidnap him? Certainly, while he was the talk of the court, it would be dangerous to send him away.

While you wallowed in self-recriminations, your morning audience filed into the room. One of your attendants pulled open the bed curtains with a flourish, as the choir began. 

You slid out of bed, into the slippers placed at the edge, and wondered what you could possibly do to help Sayo Mdang. You held out your arms, so your attendants could put your dressing robe on you, and you questioned if it would have been better not to have dismissed him. You raised your hands upwards, to start the blessings that would spread in a wave over the Empire, and the magic and taboos twisted and curled within you.

Your face was serene, as the priest-wizards chanted and the choir sang and the incense wafted. Your posture was perfect, as the choir finished and the priest-wizards said a benediction and your attendants moved into the dressing room. You were polite, as the priest-wizards greeted you and your stomach rolled with nausea and the headache rose.

It was the same as any other day.

You strode into the dressing room, and saw what Lord Igel (or his assistants) had laid out to dress you in for the morning. Here was one of your few chances in your day to express an opinion.

“The under-robe is too highly-patterned, it clashes with the mantle,” you said, calmly, neutrally, “and this pendant is too long to wear on its own, these opal rings are part of a set, use that instead.”

You limited yourself to two comments. Three, and they would have been expecting someone to be fired. Two was only very bad. Lord Igel recognised this, and immediately turned to his most obsequious.

---

You read your reports over breakfast. The cover note was still written by Sayo Mdang.  
His words were as exacting as always these past few weeks. 

How could he face it? When did he do it? Surely some of these reports had come in overnight. He must have rushed from your dismissal to his desk. Did he get any sleep?

You turned over the pages, your posture perfect. You read every word, and if the numbers refused to be remembered you would do your best with the words. Your best could never be enough.

You turned your teacup in precise quarter turns in your hand.

---

When you entered the imperial study, Sayo Mdang was already there. He was tense, as he made his obeisance, but rose at your signal.

“Good morning, Sayo Mdang,” you said, exactly the same as every other morning.

“Good morning, Glorious One,” he said, but did not meet your eyes. He had a fresh sheet of paper and a clean brush in his hand, hovering over his ink stone.

Of course, he was scared. You had imprisoned him all night, woken him with violence, and then when he had tried to explain, you had angrily and callously dismissed him. For all you knew, he'd spent the morning so far preemptively drafting the order for his own execution. Or debating ritual suicide.

Even breaths, counting them out. You began by dictating a letter to one of your princes, congratulating them on their recent marriage. It was easy, formulaic. You could pace, and speak, and try to let the morning find its rhythm.

After an hour, you could see Sayo Mdang starting to relax into the work. Near the noon bell, he seemed back to his usual self. As he waited for the ink to dry, he looked at you.

You hid a sigh of relief. "We have Court this afternoon, Sayo Mdang, so we will not be needing you until tomorrow," you said.

"Of course, Glorious One," he replied, and smiled at you.

He smiled! At you! You did not, of course, deserve his smiles. But it seemed genuine, as if he had enjoyed this morning's work, and had a good afternoon to look forward to. Writing out copies of his notes? Researching in the archives? Almost anything you could imagine him doing sounded more interesting than Afternoon Court.

The only advantage of Afternoon Court was that it was shorter, limited to the time between noon and sunset. It tended to be attended by the oldest and dullest members of your court.

As Sayo Mdang cleaned his brushes, you realised that at some point, he was going to have to attend court. If he didn't, rumours would swirl that you were embarrassed by him, or that he was too uncouth and uncivilised to be presented at court. 

If he was _just_ your secretary, he could attend without a formal presentation. Maybe that would be the best way. The idea of presenting Sayo Mdang as your _concubine_ was obscene. 

"Have you been given a formal court version of your uniform, Sayo Mdang?" you asked, as he packed away his writing utensils.

"Yes, thank you, Glorious One," he replied, "they are most elegant."

"There will be a wind quartet playing today, I believe they have something new for the music competition" I added, trying to offer a temptation. It was honestly the only interesting moment I expected in the afternoon. The music would otherwise be selected to please the usual audience of Afternoon Court.

Sayo Mdang did not offer further reply, but fell into the obeisance so I could dismiss him. I dared offer no more direct invitation, I did not want to *order* him to attend. Not today.
