I knew, when I graduated Teachers Collage, that all the plum jobs in Cisven would go to the people with the best grades, or the best connections, or both. But I had grown up in a little market town at the foot of the Grey Mountains, I knew what it was to live far away from city life. Perhaps I was even looking forward to getting back there, away from the crowds.

Yet, somehow, I did not expect to be heading across the sea. To Jilkano, an entirely different continent. 

The poster stuck to the student noticeboard had offered the standard pay for a senior teacher, to anyone willing to take a posting at a remote school. It was a five-year contract, and on top of the pay you got food and board, plus transport paid and organised. Most of my fellow students were from Cisven, although they came from everywhere from the Indrogan Estate to the homes of wealthy merchants. 

A few had come from further afield like me, and we were the ones looking the poster over. We'd had to take side-jobs while studying, to pay rent and buy food and books. Some of us had needed to take out loans, to bridge the gaps. We were all tempted.

It wasn't just the pay, though, for me. I was curious, it had always been my greatest weakness. I'd been an insufferable child, always begging my parents for books. When I finished school, and my parents were thinking of sending me to do factory work for a couple of years, I argued them round to the benefits of continuing my education. I wanted to know how people were inside, what they thought, how the world worked. Why men wore hats, and why women's clogs were never red.

I wanted to see the world. Step one had been presented to me on a poster, and it came with such generous pay that I could send some home to my parents.

And that is how I found myself on the back of a camel, winding along a desert track, somewhere in the middle of the Hutabarrah.

The caravan was contracted by the Prince of Jilkano-Lozoi to supply his mining outpost there. I had been added to the sacks of rice and beans, the boxes of matches, and bolts of cloth. The caravan leader had decided to call me just 'schoolmistress', and everyone else had gone along with that, except when they quietly muttered that I was crazy.

The mining outpost, unimaginatively named Lozoi Outpost 3, but mostly shortened to Loe, was nestled into a mesa. On both sides, the scree fell away below, and the sharp verticals of the rock stretched into the sky. Just here, there was a gentle slope from the surrounding landscape all the way to the top. And here, they had built a little village. Mostly of dry stone, since that was abundant, and familiar to the miners.

A little shrine, to no god in particular, except perhaps the Emperor, was the first building. Then came the houses. The store and the school sat opposite each other in the centre. From what the members of the caravan had told me, the store was also a pub, and a post-office. The school was the largest building, but not by much. It had a bell out the front, and deep-set wooden shutters and door. Two floors, the upper would be my home.

I thanked the caravan leader (I did catch them mutter 'mad as a cat in a bath' as they were walking away), and looked up at the school building. My meagre possessions fitted in a few bags and a trunk, which the traders kindly dropped outside the school door.

The door opened, when I pushed the latch. It was dark and blessedly cool inside. Rows of benches at tables on one side, leading up to a plastered wall at the end. I initially thought the benches and tables were wood, but a closer inspection showed they were also stone. Wood was clearly in short supply here.

On the other side, three doors. The first one, I found led to a kitchen. The far one to a store-room, and the middle opened on a staircase. I dragged my things inside, and sighed. Was I ready?

---

After a month of wrangling a dozen children, I had started to gain a routine. Breakfast, then morning session. Lunch, nap, then afternoon session. Dinner, I always took at the pub. It was the pub that had been contracted to feed me, although I made my own breakfast and lunch from their supplies. Dinner was a time to chat with the miners, their families, and the sarcastic publican, Ted.

Ted and I got on very well. He enjoyed ribbing me about all the places I would send letters to, even when the post was only collected once a month. I, in turn, complained that his wife refused to talk to me. Which was only funny because he wasn't married, and didn't look likely to ever be so.

One evening, just as it was starting to get nicely cool, I was sitting outside the pub. Chatting with one of the children, as they played hoop-and-stick, trying to convince them that jungles really did have giant cats.

By the shrine, I saw a local. All the miners, I knew, had come from the coast. Paid by the Lazoi prince to mine for gold. The locals belonged to the desert, knew its ways, and the miners found them more than a little creepy. I waved.

The woman stepped out, and then another. Between them, I could see a small boy, perhaps five years old. “Hello!” I said, smiling. I didn't know if they knew any Shaian, so I tried to just appear as welcoming as possible. As they walked up to the centre of the town, I could feel the eyes of everyone in the pub behind me. Even the child with the hoop-and-stick pulled back against the wall. Well, there had been some aggression between the two groups. But it didn't seem likely to me that two unarmed women with a child were a threat.

“You,” said one of the women, and then frowned, waving at the school building, “grow the children, grow counting, words.” She knew a little Shaian then, I could work with this.

“Yes,” I said, nodding, “I teach the children.”

“Teach!” she said, clicking her fingers and smiling, “Teach the children, yes. Will you teach our children?”

“Yes!” I said, excited, “I will teach any children. There is no cost.”

The two women exchanged a glance, and I wondered if perhaps they understood more Shaian than they could speak. I tried something more complex. “The government pays for every child to go to school. Everything they need is free. Books, pens, ink, paper, slates. School is from sunrise until noon, and then half-way between noon and sunset until sunset. It runs for six days, starting today, and then there is a day off. Then another six days of school.”

They both nodded. The first speaker pushed the boy forward. “Please teach Aioru,” she said, “Tomorrow.”

“Of course,” I said, and then turned to the boy. He looked very serious. Skin not quite dark enough to pass as a high noble, with a short-cropped cloud of densely curled hair. He was wearing a long tunic, with busy red and brown geometric patterns. “Nice to meet you Aioru. I look forward to seeing you tomorrow.”

The boy looked between the two women, and then looked at me. “Yes,” he said, and I wondered how much he had understood. But he was young enough that he would pick up Shaian quickly.

They all smiled at me, and then disappeared back into the evening.

“You're going to teach the wild boy, schoolmistress?” said Ted.

“I teach everyone Ted,” I said, smiling, “I'll teach you if you can make it out of bed in time!”

He laughed, and I laughed, and everyone in the pub relaxed. Ted was a good man, and he knew his people well.

---

The next day, Aioru was standing by the bell when I went to ring it. “Good morning, Aioru,” I said with a smile.

“Hello,” he said, looking very serious for a five-year-old. 

I rang the bell, which didn't seem to bother him, and gestured inside. “Please come in, Aioru.”

A few of the other children had now arrived, and were looking at Aioru sceptically. “Please come in, everyone,” I amended, and moved to the front of the classroom.

The children all took their seats, except for Aioru, who stood just inside the door. “Aioru, come and sit next to Lin,” I said, and gestured to the empty place. It was right at the front. Lin was probably only three, and technically too young for school, but she was toilet trained and better behaved than many of the older children, so I didn't say anything to her adult.

Everyone stood for the prayer to the Emperor. Aioru a beat behind Lin, but clearly determined to get it right. I said the ritual words, and everyone chanted the last line together. I wondered absently what prayers Aioru's people made, and to whom.

The older children pulled out their work for the day. I directed the middle ones to copying out a lesson from their books to their slates. The youngest two I handed a box of coloured beads, and told them to count them in groups of four.

Lastly, I turned to Lin and Aioru. “Let's practice the letters in our names,” I said, and Lin slumped on her bench.

“But I know how to write my name!” she said, “I've been writing it for weeks!”

“Do you know how to write Aioru's name?” I asked. 

She frowned, and looked intently at the table, running her finger around it trying to guess the letters. “Maybe?” she said, optimistically.

“Do you know how to write my name?” I asked, mischievously. Nobody here used it, so I wasn't even sure if she knew what it was.

“No!” she said, excited, “Schoolmistress is a long word! Can we learn some long words now?”

I smiled back at them, carefully not laughing at the idea that was my name, handing out the trays of soft sand. “Yes,” I said, and Lin squealed with delight. The other children have her withering looks, but I couldn't condemn anyone who so clearly loved learning.

Wheeling the blackboard over to them, I said, “Aioru and Lin, attend.”

Lin sat up straight, puffing out her chest, and staring at me with wide eyes. Aioru looked at her for a moment, and then copied her.

“A,” I said, “That's the first sound in Aioru. What might we use to write 'A'?”

As usual, Lin's enthusiasm waned as the morning went on. Aioru's didn't. He couldn't possibly be understanding more than half of what I was saying, yet he was fiercely determined. 

After about an hour of sitting like the other children, he started wiggling and stretching. I wondered if perhaps his people didn't use chairs much. Eventually he squatted on the bench, bending over the table.

The older children gave him dirty looks, as if he had committed a social faux pas by putting his feet on the chair. I raised an eyebrow at them, before walking over to my own desk. Then I pulled myself up onto the top of it, and sat tailor-style to mark their morning work. 

At first, they looked surprised, but then they went back to their work. I saw a couple try tailor-style, and one try squatting on the bench. He fell over quickly, and everyone laughed, including Aioru.

It wasn't as if the boy didn't know anything, either. Once we got to numbers, Aioru was racing ahead of even the seven-year-olds. He could add and subtract single digit numbers, and if he had something to tally with he could do larger numbers.

I showed some of the middle students how to model multiplication with rows and columns of beads. Aioru's face lit up, and he said something fast and long in his own language. Then he stopped, and sighed, clearly frustrated with his ability to explain. “Yes,” he said, “Aioru...” 

He groaned, and set out four beads in a square. Then he added another, and showed he couldn't make a rectangle, no matter how he arranged them. Then he added another bead, showing two times three. Another, no shapes. Another, two times four. Another, three times three. Another two times five… He went all the way to sixteen, showing it could be made of four by four, or two by eight. The other children watching carefully.

I wondered if he was perhaps older than he looked. Teachers College didn't expect anyone to understand division until they were eight or nine, and prime factors until they were teens.

“Very well done, Aioru,” I said, with a smile.

He sighed, and said something in his own language, which I assumed to mean something like, “Thanks, I wish I could explain properly.”

“I wish I could understand your language too,” I said, “I'm afraid we'll just have to muddle along. You will pick up Shaian soon.”

---

I was right. Within six months, he was happily chatting with the other students. You couldn't even pick out his accent. Reading and writing started slower, but he was so determined, he caught up much faster than I thought possible.

After a year, he was joined by two others. On their second day, they whispered something to him in their own language. “I won't,” he said in Shaian, “School is in this language, you have to learn it.”

“Maybe you can give them a number of questions they're allowed to ask per day, Aioru,” I said, trying to balance their differing needs, “Then they can decide if it's really important, of if they can try to work it out for themselves.”

Aioru sighed, and turned back to them and said something in their own language again. “Okay, Schoolmistress,” he said to me, “But we are here to learn, don't be too nice to them.”

---

The miners still didn't trust the locals, and the locals didn't trust the miners. Yet, over the first four years I was there, things remained relatively peaceful. I hoped that some of that was the influence of children who had learnt that the Others were not so different. But there is only so much you can expect a child to do.

Or so I thought. 

One evening, a group of locals, mostly women but a few men, came climbing up to the pub. In the centre of the group, was Aioru. He looked at me and smiled, but then turned back to his elders.

“Hello!” he said, with a surprising amount of volume, “We see you are fencing off a new area. It's got a spirit tree in it, and you must not cut it down, or dig under it.”

Ted looked at me, and I nodded. Quietly I caught the eyes of the miners children in the pub, and gestured for us to go. There was a bit of resistance, but I guess even the children could feel the tension. Or perhaps they remembered the violence that had happened before.

The children and I walked behind the houses and around the village, and into the schoolhouse. I uncovered my one mage light, and set it in the middle of the floor. Then I went and got one of the books of stories.

“Will Aioru be alright?” asked Lin, her eyes wide. She had grown into a sweet seven-year-old, and was fascinated by the local children.

“He's doing an important job,” I said, carefully, “He's making sure your adults and his adults understand each other.”

“My dad says they're going to expand the mine no matter what the wild people do,” said one of the older girls, edging over to the window. Someone raised their voice outside, and she came scurrying back.

I nodded, but said, “And I'm sure Aioru's elders would say that the mine must not be expanded, no matter what.”

The children thought about this. It was complicated, and scary. And confusing to think that maybe their parents might not be entirely right.

I opened the book of stories, and read to them about a princess trapped in a tower. And if perhaps it wasn't quite the Traditional Tale of Alinor promised on the cover, well. It was only a bit banned.

After a while, I sent one of the older children to check at the window. They reported that it was just one local and one miner now, with Aioru translating. Ted lurked in the background. I sighed, and sent everyone home to their families.

Then I stood in the doorway, watching Aioru, as Ted was. Finally, Aioru and the local left, and the miner turned to Ted.

“It ain't as easy as all that,” he said, shaking his head, “This tree is just the start. We got to make quota, and the vein is running dry. They don't want no fighting and neither do I but when there's gold under a god, well.” He stamped off home.

“Do you think it will come to blows again, Ted?” I asked.

“Could do,” he said, “That kid is sharp, but he's only a kid. If we don't listen, if his people don't listen, don't matter if we understand.”

I sighed, “Everyone here is on a side.” Then I laughed, “Maybe if you write a letter to the Emperor he can come and negotiate.”

Ted chuckled and shook his head, “Maybe the Prince would send some guards to protect the gold, but what does the Emperor care about us?”

I shrugged, “He made school free, maybe he at least cares about the children.”

I left it there, and went to bed.

The next day, I was relieved to see Aioru and the other local children still came to school. Quickly, before everyone sat down, I said to him, “You did so well last night, Aioru, you should be proud.”

He sighed. I could see he didn't believe the peace would last either.

During my afternoon nap time that day, I did something I had never in all my years, thought to do. I wrote to the Emperor.
