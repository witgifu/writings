Cliopher's last day on Zunidh was a whirlwind. Lady Mairel was clearly concerned they were going to be _late_, even though the transport left at night. She didn't like that the last ritual was going to finish minutes before they would need to leave the hotel. After it was done, Cliopher mostly rubbed the oil in with a dry towel, rather than trying to wash it off properly. Then he scrambled into his clothes. Some of his new clothes, as Lady Mairel had ordered him to discard the ones from the ship as soon as the new things had been delivered.

They got in a coach, which took them to the edge of the city. When Cliopher stepped out, he saw, standing at the edge of the highway, what must surely have been a wonder of mage-work. An… omnibus? A coach? With four axles and two levels, seemingly pulled by nothing. Painted black, with gold accents and sun-in-glory details, it could only have been made for the Army or the Service. This must be the transport they had waited for.

An officious woman with a square hat approached them with a clipboard and a mage light. “My lady…?” she asked, with a bow.

“Lady Mairel, of Lind; Mage écuyer Exa; my ward, Cliopher Madon,” said Lady Mairel, haughtily.

“Very good, my lady,” said the official, consulting her lists, “Number three, please,” and waved toward the coach behind them.

Lady Mairel sniffed, and stalked off to the transport. Two sweaty-looking youngsters ran up to the coach they had just exited and collected their baggage. Exa stepped slightly ahead of Lady Mairel, and opened the door marked with a brass number three. This earned him a rare smile. Cliopher followed her, and Exa winked at him before springing up behind them both.

“Have you been in such transport before, my lady?” asked Exa, in an unusually obsequious voice.

“No,” said Lady Mairel, “I happened to already be in Exiputal when I received the summons.”

“Then I warn you, my lady, it is fast, but it is likely to upset most stomachs,” said Exa. He pulled out a paper bag of sweets from a pocket, proffering them to her. “Ginger supposedly helps.” Cliopher wasn't offered any. 

The lady with the clipboard came past, shutting the doors, making sure they were all seated, and the luggage secured. Then a loud whistle blew from somewhere in the front, and the contraption started moving. For perhaps the first thirty seconds, Cliopher thought it felt similar to the carriages. Uncomfortable, but mostly rattly. Then the speed picked up. 

What would have been a gentle winding road through the landscape, had they been in any sane mode of transport, turned into storm waves. He realised why they left after dark, when any others using the highway would be abed. This let them move at an insane speed without collisions. Holding tightly to the armrest, he tried to wedge himself as deep as possible, for fear he would be tossed out.

Cliopher had no idea how long he sat there, shaken like a dried bean in a rattle, looking at the darkness outside the window. Perhaps it was something about growing up sailing, or being disorientated by so many schooled rituals, but he didn't feel sick. Lady Mairel looked green, but that could have just been the dim light. Exa seemed to be almost enjoying the experience, but maybe he was just smirking at the other's discomfort.

Thankfully it did deliver on 'fast'. Far quicker than Cliopher would have thought possible, they passed under a great stone arch, and from deep night seemingly jumping into late evening. Kavanor, Cliopher knew, even if he hadn't seen, was famous for its broad fields of grain. In the last dregs of the light, and the flashes of things illuminated by the mage lights of the transport, he could see they were travelling through a forest.

Full dark arrived before Cliopher saw an end to the trees. But out the window he could see stars he didn't know the names of. A river of stars, but not one he knew. Automatically he felt for Loaloa. It was tenuous, but still there, a fine thread stretching out to another world.

Eventually, the transport slowed to just a bone-shaking rattle, before finally coming to a stop.

The woman with the clipboard opened their door, saying briskly, “My lady, if you could exit quickly, this is not the final stop for the night.”

Cliopher let Lady Mairel and Exa leave first, both looking as shaken as Cliopher felt. Their luggage was extracted while they stood to the side, breathing deeply and trying not to make eye contact with the other passengers. Looking around, they were standing on some kind of platform, beside a building that seemed to have an excessive number of turrets, in an architectural style that was unfamiliar. It was lit by a profusion of mage lights.

“Nearly there,” muttered Lady Mariel, quietly enough that Cliopher suspected she didn't think he could hear her. Did that mean they were at the edge of Astandalas?

“Come along, Cliopher,” she said, in her usual brisk tone, as a man in uniform with a trolley started collecting their trunks. Cliopher stood up straight, and fell in step behind her. She strode toward the building, with Exa loping along beside them. There seemed to be a sort of tunnel through it, with massive doors on either end. The far set of doors were closed, and two queues wound their way to a smaller inset door.

Lady Mairel joined the shorter line, and Cliopher stood behind her, with Exa lurking uncomfortably close behind Cliopher. It moved forward at a good pace, and soon Cliopher saw that before the door was a checkpoint. Illuminated signs above said 'residents' for the line they were in, and 'non-residents' for the longer line. 

He opened his mouth to say he wasn't a resident of Astandalas, and then closed it again. Where was he a resident of? Possibly nowhere, now. His heart sank. Officials at the checkpoint were obviously being shown some kind of documentation by the people going through. Cliopher could only assume Lady Mairel would have something appropriate.

Indeed, when they reached the front, she pulled two letters from her bag. One fairly ordinary, and the other dripping with seal ribbons. The official touched the seals with a fingertip, but didn't seem particularly interested. After she waved Cliopher and Lady Mairel through, Exa simply put his left hand on a copper plate, which made a pleasant chime. There were no further questions.

On the other side, Cliopher tried not to listen too closely at the 'non-residents' being grilled about their business. It _was_ late, perhaps that was a good reason to be suspicious. And a good reason that queue was carefully watched by over a dozen alert guards, while theirs had two, who looked bored.

Through the little door was a courtyard. It was lit so well, Cliopher had to look at the sky and confirm it was in fact still night. To either side of the path were gardens, formally laid out to make many copies of the banner of Astandalas. And in front, a sweeping ramp, up to the largest building Cliopher had ever seen. Not just seen, he couldn't even think how such a building was possible, unless held together with magic. It was almost more believable that it had been carved out of a massive cliff, rather than built.

_The Palace of Stars_, thought Cliopher, as he compared the reality to the picture in his schoolbooks. It simply wasn't possible to convey the scale with a picture, he supposed. Or the way the stone seemed to glow with the lights.

Lady Mairel did not stop to admire the palace. “Don't dawdle and gawk like a tourist, Cliopher,” she said, “We still have a twenty-minute walk ahead.”

Cliopher was tired, and overwhelmed. He felt like he had been ripped from every mooring, every point of reference. “Sorry, my lady,” he said quietly. Then he looked down at her feet, and followed where she led. Up ramps, up staircases, along corridors. Flashes of colour teasing at the edge of his vision.

Just when Cliopher was wondering if he was, in fact, a boat, on a stone floor sea, Lady Mairel stopped. Cliopher tripped, trying to make sure he didn't bump into her. Exa grabbed onto him. “You are tired, aren't you,” he whispered into Cliopher's ear.

“Sorry, sir,” said Cliopher, but carefully not pulling away, however much he wanted to.

“Good boy,” said Exa, and patted him on the lower back.

The door in front of them was opened by a maid. “My lady,” she said, with a deep bow. Then she pulled the door all the way back, to allow them in.

They walked in, and the maid closed the door behind them. “I'm _exhausted_,” announced Lady Mairel, not obviously to anyone in particular, “I will not be seeing anyone before nine o'clock.”

The maid bowed again. Three other people were waiting at the end of the entrance hall, lined up like soldiers on parade. They all bowed to Lady Mairel. She waved a hand at them vaguely, and then she hurried off down the hall.

An old man stepped forward to meet them. “Sir, young master, let me show you to your rooms,” he said.

Exa nodded, and the three of them followed after Lady Mairel. Ahead of them, Cliopher heard a door slam shut. Neither Exa nor the old man seemed surprised. Further along, the old man indicated a door to Exa, “I hope this is suitable sir,” he said, “I will be with you in a moment.”

Exa nodded curtly, and stepped into the room. “See you at dawn, boy,” he said, without looking back.

Cliopher swallowed, and followed the old man to the last door on the hallway. Stepping inside, he bit his lip. In a pure coincidence, it was laid out exactly like his room at home—no, at his mother's house. His old room. If a room was his, now, it was this one. Bed, cupboard, desk, chair. There was an addition of a washstand in the corner. Rather than a view of the tree outside, there were only strange stars.

The door snapped closed behind him, but there was no sound of a lock turning. No, Cliopher supposed, there was no point locking him in here. Even if he left the room, he had no idea how to get home. No idea how to even get back to Zunidh. There was no point imagining the Red Company coming to rescue him, even in his dreams he had known he would have to turn them down. Exhausted, he collapsed on the bed, only managing to remove his sandals.
