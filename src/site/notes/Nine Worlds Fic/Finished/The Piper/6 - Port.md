Three weeks of sailing later, they arrived at a port. Cliopher could tell from the change in the sounds. Gulls and other shorebirds abounded, and the waves sounded different. Nobody had told Cliopher where they were going, so he had no idea where it actually was. He wasn't even allowed up on deck after dark to see the stars. Only Exa and Lady Mairel talked to him. After that first meal she had declared, to his relief, that he was to eat alone or only with her until he had improved his manners.

Cliopher could feel his temper fraying. All his body hurt from the rituals, which were at least four times each day.  He tried pearl-diver breaths, and reciting the lays, to keep his mind and face calm. At night, he cried himself to sleep, in a futile attempt to drain away his feelings.

One of the sailors had brought him to Lady Mairel's room this time. He stood, just inside the door. Lady Mairel sat in a comfortable chair, sipping tea. “Again,” she said, and Cliopher bowed low, trying to make the exact angle he had last time. He tried to think of it as a dance move, the way he controlled his body to make the _exact_ correct steps when dancing the fire.

“Yes,” said Lady Mariel, and Cliopher straightened. “Now, shortly we will be disembarking. You won't give me any trouble will you?”

“No, my lady,” he said quietly, eyes down.

“Good. Exa is coming with us, to continue the rituals. You won't give him any trouble either, will you?”

“No, my lady,” he repeated.

“You know,” said Lady Mairel softly, “I think he might like you.”

Cliopher carefully didn't flinch, locking all his muscles. Exa had continued touching him when it wasn't necessary. He was the only person on the ship who had, unless you counted Lady Mairel's strap. It didn't feel companionable, friendly. It felt possessive, or perhaps like the other man was poking him to get a response. Cliopher didn't know what to say to Lady Mairel.

“Well,” said Lady Mairel after Cliopher had been silent for a while, “Go and pack.”

“Yes, my lady,” said Cliopher, with a bow. He withdrew from her room, and made his way back to his own.

In the bottom of the wardrobe, was a leather-wrapped trunk. It had presumably been used to transport the clothes he had been given, and it made him wonder that they were so sure someone would meet the criteria. All the clothes held together with ties, so it would fit a variety of widths, but he wondered what might have happened if someone particularly tall or particularly short had been chosen. 

He packed the clothes into the trunk, but was less certain if he should take the items in the washstand. After dithering for a bit, he packed the razor. On the second morning he had received a swat for failing to shave his head before breakfast; apparently Lady Mairel had been able to see a shadow of new growth. Since then, he had shaved it every time he washed, just in case.

Rather than packing the book of etiquette, he opened it to read. By now he had read it twice, and was making his way through it a third time. It was tedious, dry, and missing all the foundational rational that Cliopher usually found held a topic together for him. Yet all he had was this book, his lessons and meals, and the rituals. Of the three, the book was the least painful.

Before the next bell, a sailor came to collect him and the trunk. They went up and up, out into the sunlight. It was a warm afternoon, although the sun was initially blinding. He blinked, and stumbled a little. Lady Mairel tutted behind him, and he tried to walk slower while his vision cleared. Once it did, he had to pay careful attention to stop himself stumbling in shock.

The city was ginormous. Many times the size of Gorjo city, it stretched off as far as he could see in every direction. Of course, from the level of a boat in the harbour that wasn't particularly far out. But all around the bay, he could see buildings crammed together. Up the river that spilled into the bay, buildings. Right to the arms that stretched into the sea, where two lighthouses stood, cascades of buildings. Some sparkling in the light, some in near ruin, some tall and elegant, some cobbled together out of scrap wood and sheet metal.

Admiral Longren stood on the deck, watching proceedings. “Lady Mairel,” he said.

Cliopher bowed low, and Lady Mariel bowed as well, moving in front of him. “Yes, Admiral?” she said, deferentially.

“You're the last, stupid Wide Seas, why are they so damn _wide_, anyway. You're the last from Zunidh, so I expect you to be sending my mage back quickly. I'm seven short and it's a disgrace. I don't know how they're coping on Ysthar or Alinor where there's actual conquest that's supposed to be going on,” he said, clearly rambling.

“Of course, Admiral, my apologies for the inconvenience. I will return Mage Exa as soon as possible,” she said, inclining her head.

The Admiral sniffed, and turned away without further comment. He clearly considered Lady Mairel below him, although Cliopher wasn't sure if that was because she was of lower birth, or if he only valued the navy. It made him nervous not to know, perhaps there might be books with family trees in them so he could look people up and not get things wrong by _assuming_.

Lady Mairel bowed again, Cliopher bowed again for good measure, and they continued across the deck. When Cliopher reached the railing, he did balk. At the bottom of the gangway that had been placed, was a sea of people and animals. Donkeys and magic pulled carts, while horses and magic pulled carriages. Vendors hawked wares, children begged, nobles rode through them laughing and not looking.

Lady Mairel tittered. “If you're overwhelmed by this, Cliopher, try not to panic when we reach Astandalas,” she said.

“Yes, my lady, sorry, my lady,” said Cliopher, and followed her down the gangway, with the sailors close behind them. They had been at sea less than a month, and yet Cliopher felt assaulted by the sounds and sights and smells. He focused on Lady Mairel's back, and tried to ignore everything else. 

She led him through the crowd, which parted a little for them, and to a waiting carriage. Someone jumped down from the back to open the door, and Lady Mairel stepped inside. Cliopher stood hesitating at the bottom of the stairs. Was he expected to join her, or should he ride on the back like the person who opened the door?

“My lady?” he asked, nervously.

She stopped arranging her robes to look at him, and pointed to the seat opposite her.

“Thank you, my lady,” he said, climbing up quickly. Crouching, as there was not enough space to stand, he tucked himself into the far corner of the rear-facing seat. Doing his best to make himself as small as possible.

Lady Mairel ignored him, and rapped the roof with her knuckles. The carriage moved off with a jolt. Cliopher didn't enjoy the feeling, the vehicle joggled over the wood and then the cobbles. It was very much unlike any boat he had ever been in. He did appreciate that it kept him away from the sheer press of people outside.

This must be Kavinor, surely. Three weeks seemed like not enough time to get half so far, but presumably the navy had both the best ships and the best magic. Kavinor was the largest city, and the richest province, on Zunidh. It was ancient, he knew from school. It hadn't occurred to him that it would _smell_ so bad. Perhaps it was something about a city that had over a million people, and had been lived in for more than two thousand years. The smell of piss and animals and cooking ground into the very stones of the buildings.

Cliopher had no reference for how far they had gone when the carriage stopped. Lady Mairel got out, saying “Come, Cliopher,” as she stepped down. He scrabbled to follow her. Out, across a busy pavement. He barely had time to register the rough sleepers tucked into the columns, out of the way of the traffic that ignored them. Through doors that looked too large for a single person to push open, and into an atrium, lit by a giant round skylight.

A young woman approached Lady Mairel. Cliopher realised she must work here, since everyone else he could see was dressed the same, short tan robes. Clerks? Was this a bank of some kind? The co-op bank in Gorjo City didn't bother with a uniform. Perhaps the Imperial Bank did, he'd never had reason to go inside.

“What can we do for you, my lady?” asked the young woman, officiously.

“I am Lady Mairel,” said his instructor, haughtily, “I am escorting the special tithe from the Wide Seas for the Glorious One. I require transport to Astandalas.”

“Of course, my lady,” said the young woman, who led them over to a man behind a desk. He in turn looked flustered, and apologised profusely, before rushing off to a back office. Lady Mairel pursed her lips, in an action Cliopher associated with his worst mistakes. He did his best not to react, knowing that it wasn't _him_ that was in trouble for once.

From the back offices came a very tall man, his skin so dark it was almost black. Lady Mairel bowed _very_ deeply, and Cliopher copied her a beat behind. This had to be a Duke or similar rank, he thought as he recalled his hours of practicing bows. “I am afraid the transport left yesterday, Lady Mairel,” he said, his voice soft and breathy, and bowing slightly to her in return.

“I am sorry to have missed it, my lord,” she said, sounding pleasant and respectful, “Would you happen to know when the next would be available?”

“Four days,” he said, “Unfortunately most transports are cargo-only to Astandalas, and I would not wish to put you in _that_. Perhaps you can take advantage of the delay to get your charge better outfitted.” The last part was delivered with a very slight smile.

Cliopher wished the floor would swallow him up. Being talked about like he was a piece of furniture was discomforting. But the great lord's eyes had flitted briefly to him, and he could feel the distaste despite the neutral expression. Cliopher had no idea why the man should dislike him, perhaps it was just because he was a commoner being dressed and trained up like a noble.

“Of course, my lord,” said Lady Mairel, “Would you have any recommendations for accommodation?”

The lord waved at the clerk who had followed him back to his desk. “Find them somewhere suitable,” he said, “And release the funds to Lady Mariel.” Then he walked away without any further comment or acknowledgement.

The clerk was still flustered. He flipped through a number of ledgers, and then wrote out an address. “I hope this is suitable, my lady. If you send any bills to us, that is probably easiest, but—”

Lady Mairel interrupted him, “Yes, I will forward the bills.” She snatched the address and looked at it. “A _public inn_?” she asked, with obvious distaste.

“It is the best hotel, my lady,” said the clerk, smoothing his robes reflexively, “The name is unfortunate, but I assure you it is close to the best shops, the best gardens, and boasts exquisite private suites.”

“Will they be expecting us?” she asked, slightly mollified.

“We have a standing arrangement, my lady,” he said, and proffered to her a sealed letter, “The staff are very discrete.”

Lady Mariel nodded, “When Mage Exa gets himself here, you may forward him to the same place.”

Just as she and Cliopher turned around, the woman who had first greeted them approached the man in question. The mage was clearly having to carry his own trunk, and Cliopher wondered vaguely if his had been put on the coach or if it was still at the port. 

“Exa,” said Lady Mariel, “You may as well come with us.”

The mage looked at them, and bowed to Lady Mariel. Cliopher bowed to the mage, who looked him over.

“Yes, yes, I know the next ritual is due soon. Let us get to the hotel as quickly as possible,” she said, brushing past the greeter. “The carriage is waiting.”
