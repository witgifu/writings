Kip was escorted below deck. He could tell, because it was much darker, and his eyes took a while to adjust. The soldiers pushed him, tugged him, manoeuvring him through the narrow corridors and steep stairs of the ship. Soon, they pushed him onto something soft, a bed, perhaps.

There was the sound of a door locking behind him.

Frightened that he had been locked in with the soldiers, Kip scrambled around so he was facing up, with his back against a wall. He blinked to try to get his eyes to adjust. There was no light at all in the room, not a porthole for daylight, or a mage light, or even a lantern. His heart beat quickly, as he tried to discern if there was anyone in the room with him.

All he could hear was his own heart, his own breath. That was partially a relief, he was alone; and partially a worry, it meant there must be sound-dampening spells on the room. He should be able to hear the sound of the ship, of sailors, of water, even the creaking of the sails. Perhaps this was a room for fancy passengers who wanted peace and quiet, but that seemed unlikely on a navy ship. It could also be to stop the screams of interrogated prisoners disturbing the rest of the work.

He was just starting to feel his way around the room, when there was the sound of a key in the door. Twisting suddenly to face it, he was blinded momentarily by the bright mage light the person turned on with a clap.

“You understand the arrangement you have made… Cliopher Madon?” said a voice Kip didn't recognise.

“Yes, but it's Mdang,” said Kip, trying to untwist himself.

“That's yes, _my lady_, Cliopher,” said the voice, "Nobody can say that barbarian name, I'm changing it to Madon."

“Yes, my lady,” repeated Cliopher, dully. Even his name was being taken away. He would be whatever they needed him to be, he supposed. That was the deal.

She sighed. “It'll do for a start, I suppose, although your accent is _dreadful_. I am Lady Mairel, of Lind. I'm here to teach you court manners before your presentation. And no, I don't know why _he_ couldn't have just selected young nobles who already know all of this.”

Cliopher started to bow, over his hands, Islander fashion. He stopped himself, and switched to the type of bow he'd seen people do in the Astandalan temples. Hands flat on his thighs.

“Hm, well, at least you're a _polite_ young man from a backwater province,” she said, “I suppose I should be thankful I wasn't given a sheep farmer from Colhélhé.”

Cliopher didn't know what to say to that. So he fell back to Buru Tovo's constant refrain of advice, _listen first_.

“Before we go any further, all that hair has to go,” she said. Cliopher was starting to be able to see her as his eyes adjusted to the light, although they were still sore from crying. “The outfit is cute, but unfortunately you're supposed to be a courtier, not a scholar. There are some spare robes in the cupboard that should be close enough for now.”

She was short, that was the first thing Cliopher noticed about Lady Mairel. She had the pale skin and hair of a native of Alinor, although Cliopher wasn't sure about the accent. Her clothes were layers of silk, which must have been dreadfully hot. And her hair was piled high on her head in an elaborate confection of curls. Or it might have been a wig, he couldn't tell.

“There's a mirror behind that cabinet door,” said Lady Mairel, pointing, “And the sink has hot and cold water. I want you washed, your head shaved, and dressed, in half an hour. Can you do that, Cliopher?”

“Yes, my lady,” said Cliopher, adding another bow for good measure. He had no idea if he could, but he would try.

“Good,” she said, “I will leave you to it.”

She swept out, closing and locking the door behind her. Cliopher looked around the room. It was a tiny cabin, but it did indeed have its own sink, bed, and cupboard. He supposed that this was probably an officer's cabin, or perhaps a mage. A noble would want more glitz, and a sailor wouldn't be given their own room. It didn't look at all like what he expected an interrogation room to look like, or even a prison cell.

Cliopher opened the cabinet, to find not just a mirror, but soaps, creams, a safety razor, and scissors. The cupboard did indeed hold robes, in soft, fine linen. Sighing, Cliopher went back to the sink and picked up the scissors. He had given up his efela ko, his mother had sung Ani's Lament. Whatever he had been, whatever he was going to have been, was gone. What he did now was to protect his family, his friends, his community.

While he'd never been a particularly good student of Lazo's barbering skills, he thought he could probably manage simply removing all his hair. He cut it as short as possible with the scissors, pulling it off in chunks and dropping them into a bin in the corner. Figuring it didn't matter if he got the scholar's robes covered in hair, he continued snipping over the sink, until all he could feel was a soft fuzz. He only stabbed himself in the ear once, and in the neck twice. Finally, the safety razor removed the rest until his head was smooth.

Then he cleaned the sink, a skill he _had_ perfected at Lazo's shop. Now he could wash and dress.

It felt strange, having no hair, no efela. The robes also felt wrong, they had many more layers than he had expected. Cliopher had chosen what he thought was the simplest set, but it still had two more layers than he was used to. The sandals supplied were also new, didn't fit very well, and so were uncomfortable.

He went to test the door, but it was still locked. Well, he could hardly be blamed if he was late because she had locked him in. He sat down on the bed and waited.

It wasn't much later, that Lady Mairel returned. He stood up, when she opened the door, and bowed when she came in.

“Good,” she said, “I'm glad you're not completely ignorant.”

Cliopher tried not to take it as an insult. She did seem to genuinely believe it was a compliment.

“Hold out your hands,” she said, and Cliopher did so, wondering if she was checking if he had washed them correctly. Instead, she dropped into them a thick book. 'A Young Persons Guide to Etiquette' was written in gold scrolling letters on the cover. Below that 'Sixty-Seventh Edition'.

“Learn it,” she said, “I will begin quizzing you on it after dinner.”

“I can't possibly finish this entire book by then!” said Cliopher, caught between laughter and scorn.

Lady Mairel didn't smile, or frown. She said calmly, “Put the book down, Cliopher, and hold out your hands.”

Cliopher did so, confused. Was there a second volume? Why this ritual to hand over a book? She slipped something out of the pocket of her dress, but before he even had time to register what it was, he felt a painful sting across his palms.

“Lesson one: Do not talk back,” she said. He could see the object now, it was a short piece of leather, perhaps half or a third of a belt.

“Yes, my lady,” he said softly, not daring to move, or look her in the eye. He had heard of such things in novels, but he'd never known of any in the schools of the Vangaveye-ve. Perhaps they were not as obsolete as he had assumed.

“Good,” she said, “You may put your hands down.”

Cliopher did so, putting them behind his back, resisting the urge to grab at a pendant that was no longer around his neck. He wasn't even entirely sure what 'talking back' was. Did she just mean his attitude, or would asking it as a question also have got him in trouble? If he asked for clarification, he might get hit again.

“My lady,” he said, trying to be as obsequious as he knew how, “I apologise for my ignorance, would you be able to define 'talking back' for me?”

There was a pause, during which Lady Mariel gently tapped the strap against her other hand. Cliopher sensed that she was trying to decide if he was being genuine or pert. He offered another bow, and added, “Again, I am sorry, my lady if that question was rude. I am, as you said, from a backwater province.”

This seemed to mollify her. She sniffed, and said, “Don't question the instructions given to you by your superiors, or the truth of anything they say.”

“Thank you, my lady,” said Cliopher, bowing again. He had the feeling there was going to be a lot of bowing in his future.

“Also, Cliopher, do _try_ to say all your vowels like they are different letters. They aren't all meant to sound the same. Now, the ship's bell rings every hour. I will see you again at noon, Cliopher,” she said, and left again.

Cliopher rubbed his sore palms on his thighs. He'd always been rubbish at doing what he was told just because the person who told him was more important. Buru Tovo, and then Uncle Lazo, and probably every one of his school teachers, had been driven to despair by his constant questions. He would need to keep a firm grip on his tongue.

He looked at the book, and couldn't help but think of school textbooks. It wasn't _for_ him, he was doing this for those kids he saw sneaking out of home and hiding in the jungle. He knew how to study, how to cram. All he could give was his best. Flipping it open, he began to read.
