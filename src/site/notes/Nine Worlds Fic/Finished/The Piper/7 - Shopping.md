They had been shown what was really a small apartment in the hotel. A bedroom each, plus a dining room and a parlour, both in the Astandalan style. A porter took all their trunks up, and they were greeted by a maid, who bowed to Lady Mairel, and then backed into the corner of the room.

Before Exa had a chance to assess if Cliopher's room was large enough for the ritual, he presented himself at the mage's room. The maid's eyes followed him across the parlour in his underclothes, and he wondered what she thought was happening. Exa was clearly in a bad mood, grunting at Cliopher, and he tried to be as still and small and quiet as possible.

When it was over, and Cliopher felt like he had been scrubbed with a wire brush, he staggered back to his own room to wash and dress again. After he sat down to read, there was a knock on the door, which was something new. Wondering if it was perhaps the maid, he stood up to answer it.

“Your form said you played oboe, Cliopher?” asked Lady Mairel.

“Yes, my lady,” he said with a bow, wondering why she hadn't just opened the door. Had he been granted the reward of some privacy?

“I don't know anything about wind instruments. Do you, Exa?” she called back into the parlour.

“No, my lady,” came Exa's tired voice.

“Do you, girl?” she asked the maid.

Clearly surprised at being addressed, the maid said, “No, my lady. If you would like a tutor, or to buy music or instruments, I can ask the concierge to send someone?”

“Yes, do that,” said Lady Mairel, “Now, don't hide in your room Cliopher. Come out and show me you know how to serve tea correctly.”

Cliopher stepped out into the parlour, where Exa was spread across a couch designed for three. Cliopher glanced at the tea table, which he noticed was set for green tea. “Yes, my lady,” he said quietly, and knelt down by the table.

Mentally, he pulled the instructions from the book. Hot water into the tea bowls. Tea into the pot, trying to estimate the correct weight with only a fancy flower-shaped scoop. Water from the bowls into the teapot. Wait.

Cliopher sat back on his heels, and looked between Exa and Lady Mairel.

“Hands flat in your lap, Cliopher,” said Lady Mairel.

Cliopher didn't even realise she could see his hands, much less that he had fisted them. He relaxed them and said, “Sorry, my lady.”

“How long have you played oboe, Cliopher?” she asked.

Was this a conversation, where he was expected to ask questions back? Or a simple enquiry? “Since I was five, my lady. My whole family plays and sings. Do you play, my lady?”

She waved a hand, “Lap harp,” she said, with a sigh, “Don't get much chance to practice.”

“Sir?” asked Cliopher, as he poured the tea, “Do you play music?”

Exa sighed deeply, “Nope, unless you count winding a music box.”

Cliopher picked up the first tea bowl, and offered it on its plate to Lady Mairel. “My lady,” he said with a bow. Then he offered Exa his cup, “Sir,” and added, nervously, “Do you sing, sir?”

Exa took the tea, and raised one eyebrow at Cliopher. “You get to listen to me sing at least four times a day, what do you think?”

Taking his own cup, and choosing an armchair, Cliopher tried to think of a polite answer. Well, it was hardly _singing_. “My apologies, sir,” he began, “I am afraid the effects of the rituals make it difficult to hear.”

Sipping the tea slowly, Exa said, “Huh, fair enough.”

“My lady,” said Cliopher, now sure this was in fact conversation practice, and he had to try to keep it going, “Do you prefer a particular style? I believe the harp is quite a versatile instrument.” He definitely did not say why he knew that, or that he only knew of one person who played the harp.

“My friends always wanted me to play dancing music,” she said, with a smile, “We'll leave off teaching you court dances until we get to Astandalas, Cliopher, that needs a proper instructor.”

“Yes, my lady, thank you,” said Cliopher, wondering what exactly _court_ dancing involved. Some magazines had pictures of outfits of the rich and famous wore to dance, but no indication of what form the dances took.

There was a knock at the door to the hallway, which the maid answered. Cliopher slid to the edge of his seat, and put his tea bowl down gently on the table. He watched Lady Mairel carefully, to see if he should stand to greet this guest.

“Concierge Jabbot, as requested, Lady Mairel,” said the maid, with a bow.

Lady Mairel didn't get up, but did turn to face the man with a smile. “We need to acquire an oboe, a selection of appropriate sheet music, and so on,” she said.

Jabbot bowed to Lady Mairel, “Would my lady prefer to be recommended to the appropriate establishments? Or perhaps a more intimate experience? We can arrange for a master maker to visit, and perhaps a tutor?”

With a sigh, Lady Mairel replied, “We only have three days here, and much to acquire. Clothes, shoes, hats, gloves, books. A recommendation would probably be best.”

The Concierge bowed again, “I understand, my lady. Shall I prepare a shopping itinerary? We can also provide staff to collect packages.”

Lady Mairel nodded once, “Yes. This is for the young man,” she said, waving a single finger in Cliopher's direction.

“Of course, my lady,” said Jabbot, only taking the slightest notice of Cliopher, “Would you care to begin today, or tomorrow?”

“As soon as possible,” said Lady Mairel, “I'm sure the tailor will whine like nothing at being asked to produce court costumes in three days.”

Jabbot bowed, “Yes, my lady. I will send a footman and arrange a carriage within the hour.”

“Very good,” said Lady Mairel, waving him away. Once the Concierge had slipped out on silent feet, she turned to the maid, “I need to change.”

Cliopher stood up when Lady Mairel did, and looked down at what he was wearing. He had no idea if any of the robes he had been given were more or less appropriate for shopping.

“That'll be fine,” said Exa, stretching out, “You'll be spending most of your time out of it anyway, like you do for me.” He looked Cliopher up and down, in a way that made Cliopher feel slightly nervous.

“Thank you, sir,” said Cliopher. He retreated into his room to fetch the book, and then read it in the corner of the room, as far from Exa as he could. As far as he could tell, Exa took a nap.

The mantle clock chimed the half-hour when Lady Mairel emerged. She was, Cliopher thought, dressed in a different colour. He wasn't sure what else was different, so refrained from comment.

Perhaps she was lucky, or maybe a message had been sent to her room directly, but a man opened the door just as she made it to the centre of the room.

“Come along, Cliopher,” she said, “We have much to do.”

The first establishment was some kind of clothier. Cliopher was hustled to the back and asked by a teen who refused to meet his eyes, “If sir would please let me help remove his outer garments?”

Cliopher replied, “I can do it, it's no trouble.”

The young man flushed awkwardly, and stepped up to do it anyway. When Cliopher was as undressed as he was for the rituals, the teen began rapidly wrapping a cord around him, carefully placing knots at particular locations. When he was done measuring, he silently redressed Cliopher, who was feeling rather like a doll.

Cliopher returned to the front room, where Lady Mairel was examining fabrics with another assistant. He stood still while they discussed fabrics, trims, and colours. Neither of them asked Cliopher anything. Was he even needed, he wondered.

There were two more clothing shops after that. When they reached the cobbler, Cliopher's feet were sore from his poorly-fitted sandals. He could feel the assistant there trying to work out if it was Cliopher or Lady Mairel who had decided to put him in the sandals, as she gave them disapproving looks.

Just as Cliopher was starting to feel like his body was sort of floating separate from him, they arrived at what was unmistakably a musical instrument shop. More specifically a _woodwind_ instrument shop. Cliopher paused outside, to admire the display in the bay window, showing a flute, a clarinet, and, indeed, an oboe. When Lady Mairel looked back from the doorway, he hurried inside.

“Hello,” said a man with a droopy face and a magnificent moustache, “What would you be looking for today… my lady… and lord? I am Master Frédéric Triébert.”

It was the first time anyone had addressed Cliopher as _lord_. It was disconcerting. He had vaguely wondered what the other shop assistants thought he was, perhaps an impoverished younger cousin. Blinking in surprise, he tried to mask his feelings as he was supposed to.

“My ward tells me he plays oboe,” said Lady Mairel, “He needs a new instrument. I know little of woodwind, so I will have to trust you, Master Triébert, to recommend one, and any accessories of course.” Then she took a seat on the side, and to Cliopher's surprise, pulled a fashion magazine from her bag.

“Well, young master, how long have you played?” said Master Triébert with a smile.

“Since I was five, sir, so sixteen years. I'm afraid I haven't practiced as much as I should have the entire time, sir,” said Cliopher, unable to focus on the instrument maker, as his eyes kept drifting to the instruments displayed on the wall behind him.

“And will you be performing?” asked Master Triébert, “Or is this just for entertaining at home?”

Cliopher had no idea. Lady Mairel proved she was listening, by answering, “The best, Master Triébert, he will be going to court in Astandalas.”

“The best sounding, my lady, or the best looking?” asked Master Triébert, “There are many elegant instruments with gilt keys and mother-of-pearl inlays that are mostly for display.”

She lowered her magazine. “Ask him to play,” she said, dryly, “If you think he needs to compensate, then by all means, the flashiest.”

Master Triébert fetched an instrument that was so close to the counter, that Cliopher could only assume it was the student model. “Medium soft, young master?” he asked, pulling out a draw full of reed boxes.

“Yes please, sir,” said Cliopher, picking up the body and feeling the keys. This was definitely not a new instrument, the E flat key felt a little strange, and he wondered how abused it had been. Nevertheless, he took the pre-wetted reed that Master Triébert passed him, and carefully fitted it. The weight was perhaps very slightly heavier than his, as he ran up a chromatic scale with just his fingers.

The reed, when he put it in his mouth, was the wrong shape. He couldn't avoid grimacing slightly. He knew Lady Mairel and Master Triébert were watching closely, and judging him. It wouldn't be a good idea to try showing off on a strange instrument, so he played F major, up and down, just a single octave. Oh, he could feel he was out of practice, and his embouchure was wrong, _and_ the reed was out of tune. With a sigh, he fiddled with the reed, and massaged his lips between his teeth. He tried again, running down in F major, and trying to get a nice clear B flat at the bottom. Better.

“Where did you learn?” asked Master Triébert, gently.

“In the Vangavaye-ve, sir,” said Cliopher, looking over the reeds in the case to see if there was one that might suit him better. One at the end looked more familiar, and he said, “Might I try that reed, sir?”

“Of course, young master,” said Master Triébert.

Cliopher swapped them, and it did feel much better. He considered carefully what piece he might play, as he ran another scale. Probably one of the simpler concertos he was more confident with. Definitely _not_ any banned music he might or might not know, and probably not any Islander tunes either. Closing his eyes, trying to ignore the audience, he stepped back and played.

After the first section, he opened his eyes. Master Triébert smiled. “You could be quite good, you know, if you practiced more, young master.”

Cliopher grimaced, “Yes, that's what my mother, my sister, and my teacher all said. I do enjoy it, I just… forget to practice, sir.”

The instrument maker handed him another oboe, and Cliopher swapped the reed over. This one felt newer, the keys were slightly springier and less tacky. He played the first phrase of the concerto, but couldn't resist stopping to say, “Oh, that is much nicer, Master Triébert, thank you.”

Master Triébert smiled, and passed him another. Rather than the typical black, this was a warm brown colour, with a slightly red undertone, and a lovely wood grain pattern. “Are you sure, sir?” asked Cliopher, as he took it. This was a concert instrument, he could tell by shape of the keys. The instrument maker only smiled, and Cliopher swapped the reed over again.

It was not _his_ oboe, that he knew and loved dearly. It was, however, a truly magnificent instrument, with a beautiful warm tone. Without thinking particularly hard, he swapped to a different piece that fit the tone better. It flowed and swooped, reminding him of a bee, or perhaps a butterfly. One of his favourites, although he had never quite mastered it. He had spent so long trying that he knew the opening by heart.

When Cliopher fluffed a note and stopped, Master Triébert winked at him. “Excellent, young master,” he said, “Have you ever shaped your own reed before? Or made one?”

“Never to my satisfaction, Master Triébert,” said Cliopher, honestly. Perhaps another thing he should practice more at.

There seemed to be no censure for this, however. The instrument maker nodded, and pulled out two boxes of reeds, a case for them, and the case for the oboe itself. Cork wax, the little sheets of paper for drying the keys, two types of cleaning cloth… Cliopher was sure he had never needed all this before. But perhaps that was just because there were so many musicians in his family, it was easy to borrow a bit of wax from a clarinet player, or the paper from a flautist.

Master Triébert walked to the back, and Cliopher saw the shelves of sheet music. He drifted to the oboe section, but did not touch, putting his hands behind his back.

“Anything by the current court composer, and a book of standards,” said Lady Mairel, “_Nothing_ controversial.”

“Of course, my lady,” said Master Triébert, “Mostly solo pieces? Any likely accompaniments?” and running his fingers through the sheets and books. He plucked out a book that Cliopher vaguely recognised from his teacher's collection, although this one was new and his teacher's had been very dog-eared.

“I play harp,” she said, “And it's usually possible to get a pianoforte or a violin to play with.”

Master Triébert nodded, “Thank you, my lady,” he said, and plucked out half a dozen leaflets, and a further book.

“Now, you must practice _every day_, Cliopher,” said Lady Mairel, “Since apparently you warrant a good instrument and not a flashy one. Perhaps your musical skills can help make up for you conversational ones.”

Cliopher blushed, and hung his head. “Yes, my lady,” he said softly, “Sorry, my lady.”

Master Triébert packed up the instrument and accessories, without another comment.
