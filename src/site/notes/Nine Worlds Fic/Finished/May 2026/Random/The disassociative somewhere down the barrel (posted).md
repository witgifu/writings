Pali looked at the bottom of the tankard. “How strong did you say this was?” she asked, managing only through extreme force of will, honed by years of training, not to slur her words horribly.

“I didn't,” said the publican, with a smile, “But if you want to know, try setting it on fire.”

Putting the tankard down carefully, so as not to miss the counter, Pali said, “'S not enough left to light.”

The publican picked up the tankard, and his eyebrows went up. “And here I thought it was just your friend who knew how to sleight-of-hand away his drinks.”

“No,” said Pali, firmly, “He's just… magic. It burns it up or something, I don't know. I do the stabbing.”

“Yes, I noticed earlier. Although I don't recommend trying now, I like to keep a civil pub,” he said, raising one eyebrow.

Pali considered trying to explain why two Imperial spies had jumped them in the middle of the market, but it seemed too hard. “No,” she said, “Enough stabbing for today.”

The publican smiled. “More tomorrow?” he asked, “Or perhaps a lie in.”

Sardeet put a hand on Pali's shoulder, and asked sympathetically, “Enough yet?”

“No,” said Pali, miserably. “I still remember.”

It wasn't uncommon for people to drink to forget, as the publican well knew. His face showed his pity that someone so young should seek the bottom of the barrel like the old soldiers. 

“It doesn't actually make you forget,” said Sardeet, “It just gives you such an awful headache in the morning that everyone avoids you.”

“'S a good excuse,” said Pali, leaning into Sardeet, “Even Fitzroy…”

“You know he'd be thrilled to take you upstairs, any time,” whispered Sardeet, one eye on the publican.

Pali gave her sister a stern look, attempting to copy their mother. It didn't work, Sardeet only sighed. Somehow it never did work, even when she was sober. Finally, she said, “I don't want to.”

“Then why… why do you do this? When he takes someone else to bed, after you reject his proposal again?” asked Sardeet, confused and worried.

“I don't know,” said Pali, in a very small voice. “I just… I just can't stand seeing him with someone else.”

“Pali…,” said Sardeet, her voice gentling.

“I know it doesn't make sense!” said Pali, climbing down off the stool with much less grace than usual, “It's just how it is, okay? It's stupid, Fitzroy's stupid, I'm stupid. Stupid.”

Sardeet sighed, “You're not stupid. You're my sister and I love you. And you're going to drink an entire jug of water, and _go to bed_.”

Sardeet's imitation of their mother was _much_ better. Instinctively, Pali started, and looked around for the jug. A second later, she realised what Sardeet had done. 

“I'll get you back for that,” said Pali, “Tomorrow. When the world moves normally.”
