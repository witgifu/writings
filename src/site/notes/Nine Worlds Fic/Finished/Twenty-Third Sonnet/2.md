Conju returned. “Everything is ready, my lord,” he said, clearly slightly out of breath. Were you overworking him too? I couldn't — I needed — not now. You pushed the thoughts away again. He was allowed to be anxious for his friend.

Ludvic traded places with Domina Audry. Gently, so carefully, he slipped his arms under Kip, and lifted him up. You stood up with them, slightly less gracefully.

Kip's eyes opened again. “Thank you,” he said, to Ludvic, who smiled back.

“Kip, you need to let go of my hand,” you said, quietly.

He looked at me, confused.

“We will have to walk to your apartment through the public halls,” you said. Calm, serene, trying not to think about what would happen if you were seen in public holding the Lord Chancellor's hand.

“Okay,” he said, with a sadness in his eyes. Slowly, he let go of your hand. It was the right thing to do, but oh it hurt to let him go.

You stepped back, to the normal safe distance you kept from all but your closest attendants. You nodded to Ludvic, and Rhodin opened the door for him.

“Will you return to the Tower, my lord?” asked Rhodin. It was phrased as a question, but it was a reminder that you should not be seen trailing your sick Lord Chancellor. You did not sigh, you did not grimace, you did not even clench your hands.

“Yes,” you said, “Please go with Commander Omo, Ser Rhodin”. If you could not be there, where you wanted to be, but you could send everyone else. Kip should have his friends and his family by his side.

Ato and Pikabe formed up behind you, and you returned the way you had come. Serene, careful, acknowledging your princes and lords as they passed. Through seven anterooms, into your official study.

You looked at the door to the private study. Was I allowed to break down now? No, there were more things you still needed to do today. You had to keep as much of your schedule as you could, to make it clear that the government was fine (I was not fine).

The secretary's desk was empty. Not that Kip had sat there for more than a year (time was always troublesome, but you were sure it had been more than a year). You ran your fingers along the top of your desk.

“Ask Saya Kalikiri if she has anyone she can spare, not herself, and not Sayo Vawen. If she does not, it can wait for tomorrow.”

You waited, hoping for news of Kip, expecting some terrified junior secretary. You retrieved the book of poetry from your private study, and flipped it back to the poem.

> So I, for fear of trust, forget to say  
> The perfect ceremony of love’s right,  
> And in mine own love’s strength seem to decay,  
> O’ercharged with burden of mine own love’s might.

I felt it echo in my core, calling to me. Judging me. Had I injured Kip a third time? Once, when I worked him too hard. Twice, when I ventured too close. Thrice, when I forced him to separate? 

I thought I had done it for him, to save him from those who still held the taboos in reverence. Had I been ashamed? Afraid? To let people know that I loved him (surely they knew?).

Did he know? He had said, “If you were my brother or my cousin I could not love you more.” Had I ever returned the same? I didn't think I had ever said it in words, never dared.

I, who had once written some of the greatest love poetry in Shaian, was afraid to tell Kip that I loved him. My indomitable Kip. I would tell him. I _would_.

I must make this right. I must—

Spears stamped, interrupting my spiral. Was it news of Kip?

“Lord Eldo, sent by Saya Kalikiri,” said Ato.

You signalled to let him in. His obeisance was flawless, and you motioned for him to rise and take the secretary's desk. He did so without comment, without eye contact, as the very model of the Private Secretary (that Kip had never been).

You hadn't had a noble-born secretary since Kip came into your life. Kip had said *all* of this group of junior secretaries had been excellent. I should not hold his birth, or his father, against Lord Eldo. You tried not to be biased.

“We will need to notify the members of the Council of Princes, who are due to meet in two weeks time,” you said, as you swept around to begin pacing.

Lord Eldo held his pen ready, over a fresh sheet of paper, a neat stack beside him.

“Let us start with the furthest, Princess Oriana. The usual greetings,” you said. His pen made a few quick swoops of shorthand, and waited again.

You made it through all seventeen letters, and Lord Eldo performed admirably. He didn't even flinch when you wrote to his own father. True, it was just a form letter postponing a meeting, but still. You could wish he showed some personality, some flare.

Then it came back *why* you were writing these letters, which you had pushed aside with the need to do the work. Kip had collapsed with a heart attack. In front of Lord Eldo. He could be forgiven for falling back on formality. Had you not done the same?

No word had come yet. Nor had Rhodin, Ludvic, or Conju returned. “Please send a page to Lord Mdang's household to ask if there is any update,” you said, and word was passed through the doors.

Did this lack of updates mean things had worsened? Could you go? Would it be acceptable to go now?

You had just sent a page. It would be rude to follow them without waiting to hear back. You paced, and tried to distract yourself by thinking of what you needed to do next. You should have been preparing for next weeks appeals sessions. That required a calm and focused mind, it would have to happen some other time. Some other time you would have to carve from Court, or your time in your private study, or your sleep.

The quarter-hour bell chimed, you were due for a ritual with the Ouranatha. Not one of the useful ones that were unbinding you from the taboos. Just one of the regular ones to do with some conjunction of seasons and planets that had temporarily slipped your mind.

“Thank you, Lord Eldo. Return to us tomorrow at the third hour,” you said. There was no reason to make him sit and watch the priest-wizards dance around you like chickens rolled in silver gilt.

He left, the pair of wizards arrived.

The two junior wizards were about half-way through the second verse of a chant about fertility, when Rhodin came in. Unannounced. From the inner apartments. His eyes wild, as he locked on to mine.

I was afraid for a moment that he would try telepathy. Instead, in a horse whisper, he said, “The high priests have come for Cliopher.”

Without any of the correct ways of ending the ritual, I strode out of the circle of salt. With pure wild magic, I snapped the Schooled threads, and poured their energy back into the ground. The priests knew your schedule of rituals, they set it. They had waited until they thought you couldn't be interrupted. 

They were wrong.

I walked briskly, following Rhodin back to the Imperial Bedroom. I raised one eyebrow at Rhodin, and motioned my head in the direction of the guards following me. He nodded firmly.

Pikabe and Ato were in for a surprise. So were Bavezh and Iprenna.

Furling my magic tightly, I followed Rhodin into the cupboard that had once held a toilet. Pikabe and Ato were doing an excellent job of not looking as confused as they must have felt. Rhodin pulled open the door to the secret passages, and led us on.

I spun a tiny mage-light for us, as we moved away from the room lights. Rhodin signed for Pikabe and Ato to be silent, and prepare for action at his signal. The passage seemed endless, and tense.

We exited into Kip's empty study. Out of the passages, we could hear raised voices. Something in them changed Rhodin's mind, and he signed 'action'. We abandoned silence for speed, and raced through the rooms following Rhodin.

My wards on Kip still hadn't triggered. That likely meant that some of his household had got between the priest-wizards and their target. In Astandalas the priest-wizards had some very painful ways of disabling people who got in their way, I didn't want any of Kip's household facing that.

We turned a corner, and an image from my nightmares had manifested in front of me. Kip, desperately trying to struggle up in bed. Ludvic, standing in front of him, hands out in a classic pose of negotiation. Conju holding back a crying Féonie in the corner. Gaudy rising from a chair on the other side of Kip's bed. 

Bavezh was holding a long ritual knife, taking a step forward. Iprenna held out a hand, managing some kind of spell of compulsion on Franzel, who was bleeding slowly from a cut on his arm.

My skills learnt in my younger years had not entirely deserted me. I heated up the metal on Bavezh and Iprenna. Bavezh was forced to drop the knife, swearing. Iprenna was distracted enough to break the spell on Franzel, who slumped to the floor.

I released the magic, and let the guards do their job. I did, I admit, enjoy snapping every cantrip that Iprenna tried to release from the knots in his sash.

The guards got Bavezh and Iprenna under control. Then they turned Bavezh and Iprenna to face you, and pushed them down to kneeling.  

“What,” you said in dangerously even tones, “is the meaning of all this?” My rage, briefly satiated by the thrill of the struggle, was simmering just below your surface.

Bavezh blathered something about broken taboos and restoring the magic flowing out from the Emperor. Iprenna nodded, and tried to edge a foot over to where Bavezh had dropped his ritual knife.

You cut Bavezh off, “The magic of Zunidh is our concern, Lord Wizard.” They had never liked Kip, I had known that for years. This was their first direct attack since he had been made my Hands.

Iprenna gulped, “My lord … the ritual was not consummated.”

“Nor will it be,” you said, coldly, “Go now. Do not leave your rooms until you are summoned to our presence. Pikabe and Ato, escort the lords wizard there and see that guards are set on their doors.”

I walked past them to Kip, as Pikabe and Ato pulled the wizards back up, and led them out. Rhodin helped up Franzel, who seemed shaken, but had manage to stop the bleeding with a handkerchief. All of us, including Conju and Féonie, converged on Kip's bed.

“My lord,” croaked Kip, his eyes full of tears, “the law is the law.” He looked from me, to his golden palm, and back again.

“Would you be saying that if it was Ludvic or Conju?” you asked, softly. This man, who saw every unjust thing and remade it, could not seriously be telling you that you should execute him. You would not, you could not.

Conju held a glass of water up for Kip, and he took a grateful sip. 

“I have known,” Kip retorted, “since I first looked in your eyes, my lord, that they would come for me some day.”

It had a ring of truth about it. Had he really? The guilt gnawed at me. Had I let him live for years in fear of his life? You had said, at Navikiani, you thought, that you would never punish anyone for speaking their mind. But he had done so much more than that. So much more *good*, but Astandalas had always prized conformity over goodness. All of us who had lived in the Empire had been scarred by it.

I decided to be brave, I would make the *volta* for this poem. “My dear Kip, I have known since that day that I loved you, and would never let them take you,” I said, my voice filled with all the warmth and sincerity I could wrap into it. 

I reached out, and took Kip's hand again in both of mine. Sitting on the edge of the bed, my robes no doubt wrinkling under my awkward angle, I gently brought his hand up to my lips, and kissed it.

“I am sorry I overworked you, sorry I injured you,” I said, rubbing the gold marks, “Sorry I left you, and sorry I never told you how much you mean to me.”

Behind me, I heard Conju mutter, “Fucking finally.”

I ignored him, and focused on Kip. He gave me the most wonderful smile I had ever seen, and squeezed my hands back.
