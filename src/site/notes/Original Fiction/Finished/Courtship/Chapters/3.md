Most Procurators had a very busy schedule. Drinking, dancing, gambling, carousing, it could all take quite a lot of time. So when Sister Lilly inquired with Procurator Julius's private secretary about a meeting, she wasn't surprised that he grimaced. But she was surprised when he began flipping through a thick diary.

“Ah…” he said, without looking up, “…how much time do you think you'll need?”

Sister Lilly bit her lip, “Maybe fifteen minutes?” she replied, uncertain.

The door behind Sister Lilly opened, and Marcus walked in to join them. “Hello again, Sister Lilly!” they said enthusiastically, “are you booking in some time to see my illustrious cousin?”

“I am,” she replied, “if their Excellency can spare a few minutes.”

“How about now?” Marcus asked.

The secretary groaned, “Their Excellency is with the Board of Industry for the rest of the hour, and then is seeing the Bishop.”

“Don't fuss, Aiden” said Marcus warmly, “I'm sure the Board will be done early.” They waved, and walked through the other door, into the Procurator's main meeting room. 

Aiden sighed. “I can fit you in the day after tomorrow if you're okay—”

Marcus came bouncing back out, “They're done!” they announced.

And sure enough, six very serious men trooped out, all looking like they'd bitten into something very sour. None of them acknowledged Aiden or Sister Lilly, and they departed without another word spoken.

Marcus actually winked at Aiden. They made a dramatic bow, and waved Sister Lilly into the meeting room.

Sister Lilly walked past Marcus, and into the large room with a long dark wood table. A clerk was collecting papers that had been spread all over. Procurator Julius was sitting at the head of the table, leafing through a folder. “You didn't have to do that, you know,” they said, without looking up, “now they're going to be much harder to get answers out of.”

“I'm sorry, your Excellency,” said Sister Lilly, and the Procurator's head shot up.

“Oh! I thought you were Marcus, who just accused the entire Board of Industry of tax evasion,” he said, by way of apology, passing the folder to the clerk, who took it and made himself scarce.

“I did not!” said Marcus, returning to the room and closing the door behind them, “I just asked them if they knew anything _about_ tax evasion. Which of course they do. Because they are in it up to their necks. See, _now_ I've accused them.”

Procurator Julius winced. “Well, it's done now, and I guess at least I can blame you,” they said.

“Exactly my plan,” said Marcus, “I'm here to ask the dumb questions, and then you can tell me off in public all you like, and I won't mind.”

The Procurator rolled their eyes, and looked at Sister Lilly. “Did you need something, Sister?”

Sister Lilly took a deep breath, and decided to lay her cards on the table, “Why exactly have you come here, your Excellency?”

The Procurator didn't answer immediately, but considered the question for a moment.

“To get immunity from prosecution, and to gamble with other people's money, of course,” said Marcus.

The Procurator smiled a little, “Speak for yourself, Marcus”

“I,” retorted Marcus, “have a perfectly clean record. I'm just here to keep you in line.”

The Procurator shook their head. “Marcus is not _entirely_ wrong, Sister Lilly. I am here in part because I am seen as… meddlesome. I have been sent here to… atone.”

“I'm not sure that I understand, your Excellency, atone how?” said Sister Lilly, polite and deferential. 

“By cleaning up after my predecessors,” said the Procurator, “By repairing the relationship between your people and the capital.”

“Ha!” said Marcus, “You mean balancing the tax books and showing everyone who's boss.”

“No,” said the Procurator, looking at Sister Lilly, and not at Marcus, “although balancing the tax books is also needed. There are those in the capital who think the provinces are in need of more… direct rule. By military governors. I am hoping to show them an alternative.”

Sister Lilly bowed deeply, “Thank you, your Excellency, for your candour.”

“May I request the same in return, Sister Lilly?” inquired the Procurator.

“Of course, your Excellency, I am at your service,” she replied.

“How do I talk to a woman who is _not_ a nun?” the Procurator asked, their eyes glittering, “There are nine members of the Board of Industry, but only six of them will meet with me. Why?”

“Ah,” said Sister Lilly, “This may take a while to explain, your Excellency…”

-----

In the end, the Bishop arrived before Sister Lilly left. His Grace, Bishop Cyprus, was announced by the secretary, and was followed into the room by a clerk. The Procurator was still seated at the head of the table, with Sister Lilly seated to their right, and Marcus sitting on the chair to their left, with Marcus' feet taking up a fourth chair.

When the Bishop entered, Sister Lilly jumped up. “My apologies for taking so much of your time, your Excellency,” she said, moving to behind the chair, and hoping to be dismissed. 

But before she could make her retreat, the Bishop said, “No, do stay, Sister Lilly.”

Sister Lilly did not resume her seat. The Bishop was her superior, but this was the Procurator's space, and she wasn't quite sure who had the superior claim to tell her to stay or go.

“Yes, do stay,” chorused Marcus, waving at the chair she had vacated, “this probably concerns you too.”

Thankfully, the Procurator confirmed their cousin's request, “Yes, yes. Let's save Aiden the work of trying to find another slot later for the both of you.”

Sister Lilly cautiously resumed her seat, and the Bishop took the one next to her. His clerk stood behind him, balancing a pile of folders on his knee and trying to extract one from the middle.

“Take as much of the table as you need,” said Procurator Julius, waving down the length of dark wood, and looking at the clerk.

The clerk, startled at being addressed directly, dumped all the folders onto the table. He extracted the one he'd been looking for, and passed it to the Bishop, who passed it to the Procurator.

“As requested,” said Bishop Cyprus, “an up-to-date list of the curates in the city. I left off the ones for the cathedral, since they work under me directly.”

“Thank you very much,” said the Procurator, and without opening the file, passed it to Marcus. Marcus opened it, and started reading silently.

“I understand from Sister Lilly, that no respectable woman, outside the church, will be willing to come and talk with me,” said the Procurator, neatly summarising what had taken Sister Lilly almost twenty minutes to explain while desperately trying not to cause offence.

The Bishop laughed. “No indeed,” he said, smiling, “But why does that matter? Plenty of men will, and we've got lots of nuns.”

The Procurator smiled back. “I wouldn't want to be partial to any one group,” they said, “I have the utmost respect for the church, but I am sure there are unique viewpoints found amongst respectable women outside the church.”

“Well, your predecessors thoroughly burnt that bridge,” retorted the Bishop, “Unless you're hiding a sister in a suitcase you can use as an emissary.”

“A sister would still be an outsider,” put in Sister Lilly, “I don't think she would be trusted either, your Excellency.”

The Procurator rubbed his face with both hands. “I don't have a sister,” they said, “in a suitcase or back in the capital. So the point is moot.”

Marcus looked up from the file, “I could pretend,” they offered, “do you think I'd be convincing?”

“I think you've already been seen by half the notables of the city, and they'd remember you,” replied the Procurator.

Marcus pouted, and went back to reading the file.

“Do you have a wife, then?” asked the Bishop, “Or a mother who could drop by?”

Procurator Julius looked again at Marcus, who was definitely not reading anymore, but did not look up either. There was a long, awkward pause.

“No,” said the Procurator, finally, still looking at Marcus.

“You could marry a local. You know our marriages aren't recognised by the capital, right?” the Bishop said to the Procurator. He turned to his clerk and added, “Bring anything on that?” The clerk looked shocked, grabbed a folder that seemed to be random, and started rapidly turning over documents.

“I am trying,” said the Procurator, turning back to the Bishop, “to *improve* on my predecessors' reputation. I could be recalled at any time. I would not want to abandon anyone or drag along anyone else.”

Marcus covered even more of their face with the folder. Sister Lilly wondered if they were the reason for saying “anyone else” rather than “anyone”.

There was an even longer awkward pause. “Thank you for your advice,” said the Procurator finally, and turned the conversation to the ecclesiastical calendar.

---

Sister Lilly cycled back to her cloister, with an invitation to join the Procurator (and presumably Marcus) for morning service the next day. If Procurator Julius was seeking to improve the reputation of their office, then regular cathedral attendance made sense. See and be seen. Once word got out, general attendance would probably shoot up as people looked to make their own judgements on this new official.

Sister Lilly was still feeling uneasy. Procurator Julius and Marcus were likely to be more popular in many circles (particularly in the church), but they clearly had their own agenda. Marcus seemed to be playing the fool, but Sister Lilly suspected they were just as sharp as Procurator Julius, who might have even been setting them up.

She wandered through the refectory, where three fellow sisters were busy marking school assignments, and to the library. There she found Mother Grace, repairing book bindings. Hoping for some greater insights, Sister Lilly recounted her morning meetings.

“I'm not sure if it would actually be a good idea for the Procurator to meet more people, we've always had a quite hands-off approach. And I'm not much of one for making introductions,” Sister Lilly finished.

“It didn't sound like the Procurator asked for that, it sounded rather the opposite,” said Mother Grace, applying glue to another spine.

“Well, yes. But I'm not sure what else I could do,” replied Sister Lilly, finally joining in by cutting down endpapers.

“For now, unless they ask for something specific, I think you should just listen. Watch, listen, and pray,” countered Mother Grace.
