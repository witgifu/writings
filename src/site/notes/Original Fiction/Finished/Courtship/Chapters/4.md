Sister Lilly took Mother Grace's advice to heart. She went to events she was invited to, and listened carefully to what everyone else said. At the end of the day, she brought her concerns to the cloister chapel.

The Procurator rarely addressed her directly. For the most part she seemed to be invited to random meetings just to be an impartial witness, which was fine, if a little tiring. Once, after an evening service, the Procurator asked her to define “presbyter” as they left the cathedral. At a meeting discussing the collection of soybean tax, they asked about the number of nuns living outside the city. She answered politely, but offered no further commentary.

It was two months later, that Sister Lilly visited the little chapel near Cindy's house. Sister Lilly was on a roster of caretakers for the city's chapels, tidying the tiny corners of the garden and dusting the woodwork. Sister Lilly had met Cindy when Cindy had been in school, and Sister Lilly had taught the religion class. Cindy had been bright and enthusiastic, and Sister Lilly had kept in touch after Cindy had been taken out of school. But after her father's most recent marriage, Sister Lilly watched as Cindy disappeared into herself.

Sister Lilly had been standing beside the door when Cindy walked in, but Cindy hadn't seen her. Sister Lilly, however, saw the eyes red from weeping, the red marks on her chin, the hands red and cracked from scrubbing, and the clothes worn and over-mended. And she wondered what had happened to the daughter of one of the city's richest men.

When Cindy had said, “I'm going to marry Ana's brother,” several things fell into place in Sister Lilly's mind. Things casually mentioned in various meetings, rumours, gossip from the paper. After Cindy left, Sister Lilly abandoned her work in the Chapel, and went directly to Mother Grace.

She found Mother Grace was in her study, reviewing accounts. Sister Lilly opened the door cautiously, “Can I have a moment of your time?”

“Of course, Sister. What is on your mind?” she replied, slamming the ledger closed, and coughing at the cloud of dust that emerged from it.

Sister Lilly took the chair opposite. “I'm not sure where to start—” she said, looking anxiously around the room, hoping for inspiration.

Mother Grace sighed. “Why don't you start with what's upset you most,” she said gently.

“Ah,” said Sister Lilly, “Mathias Roy is selling his daughter to his brother-in-law, Andre Fortin, to get the money he needs to pay off his debts.”

“You know about the marriage—?” prompted Mother Grace.

“Because Cindy told me herself, just now. Poor thing, she's half starved, and I'm sure Ana Fortin treats her cruelly,” filled in Sister Lilly.

“And she's not likely to get better treatment from Andre, who is currently banned from the cathedral for 'harassing' church staff,” added Mother Grace.

Sister Lilly nodded, sadly. Money was good protection. While Andre was free with his hands, he was tight with his money, and spent only to his careful benefit. Including maintaining high-placed friends. The Bishop was walking a tight line, excluding him from the cathedral.

“And you know about the debt—?” led Mother Grace again.

“In a meeting with representatives from the Tin Miners Union, one of them pointed out that yields had been lower than average for the previous four years, but had returned to normal this year,” said Sister Lilly, counting on her fingers, “A report from the Soybean Growers said their largest local buyer for refined oil was the Roy family, and their purchases have been increasing. And most importantly, last year the Roy family imported a refurbished engine. But no city loan or capital loan of that size has been reported.”

Mother Grace's brow furrowed. “You think the Roy's mines were running out, so they spent all their money improving them? More money than they had, so they borrowed a lot, from friends? Maybe even from Andre Fortin?” she asked, a note of scepticism in her voice, “The Roys are very rich, they could have just paid out of their own pockets.”

Sister Lilly shook her head, “If anything, over the past year, Ana Fortin has been seen spending more money than usual. Her splashes are all over the paper.”

“But still,” said Mother Grace, “Mathias could just be marrying off poor Cindy because Ana suggested it.”

“His only daughter? He'd want her nearby, where he could control her. And he'd want her to keep the kids,” retorted Sister Lilly, “He won't get that from Andre.”

Mother Grace nodded slowly. Sister Lilly finally relaxed into the chair, which she'd been perched on the edge of. “Then it seems unlikely that anyone would be able to sway Mathias, if he is already giving up so much,” said Mother Grace.

Sister Lilly winced, “I know. But I just can't stand to see that poor girl in Andre's hands.” She sighed. “Or anyone else in his hands, for that matter,” she added.

Mother Grace pursed her lips. “I agree. But that swine is probably safe from any justice here. It would take major interference from the capital, which would come with side effects for everyone,” she said, the hesitation clear in her voice, “we may have to leave her to him.”

Sister Lilly looked shocked. She had been hoping that Mother Grace would have the solution, as she had so often before. “… But…” she said, and trailed off.

Mother Grace reached out across the desk and put her hand over Sister Lilly's. “I pray that I am wrong,” she said, her voice full of compassion, “I pray that Mathias will change his mind. I am sorry I cannot offer you more than that.”

Sister Lilly deflated in her chair. “Thank you, Mother,” she said softly, and dragged herself out of the chair. She didn't look at Mother Grace, she feared to see the pity in her eyes. Instead, she left the office, and closed the door firmly behind her. It wasn't a slam, it wouldn't do to slam.

It was nearly time for the evening meal. Sister Lilly couldn't face the room full of her sisters watching her, or even the thought of eating. She went to hide in the chapel instead.

*Just like Cindy*, she thought, taking a seat in the corner out of immediate view, *seeking divine justice, when earthly justice runs out.* Sister Lilly ran her fingertips over the carving around the window. *There are probably hundreds of young people beaten by their parents, and sold into unfortunate marriages*, she thought, with a pang of guilt, *who stands up for them?*

Sister Lilly stayed in the chapel, her mind full of stormy thoughts, until she heard her fellow sisters chattering in the hallway. They would be coming to the chapel soon for even service, but Sister Lilly was still in no mood to see them. Or to listen as Mother Grace read, her heart and eyes on other things.

She slipped back out into the hallway, where a few stragglers were hanging after dinner. “Not joining us?” said Sister Rosemary, her eyes twinkling, “Off to the cathedral again?”

Sister Lilly thought she may as well take the offered excuse, “Yes,” she said, “I'm off again” and smiled in what she hoped was a convincing way. The other sisters nodded, and Sister Lilly walked past them and out.

There was no reason she couldn't go to the evening service at the cathedral. It was open to all, even if she wasn't sitting with the Procurator. So she cycled over, and took a seat towards the back. 

The singing was average, the reading was halting, the homily was brief. Sister Lilly didn't really hear any of it. She let the liturgy and the prayers wash over her, a calm breeze, a comforting purr. Slowly her heart and breathing slowed down.

All Mother Grace had been able to offer was prayers. Sister Lilly couldn't think of anything more either. *I will hold vigil for Cindy* she thought, as the canon gave the dismissal, *I will hold vigil for as long as I can, or until she is free.* 

The cathedral was open all night, and all day. Chapels might be locked overnight to prevent animals from making their home, or vagrants, but the cathedral stayed open. So as everyone else filed out, Sister Lilly went up and knelt at the rail, just like she had done with the Procurator. 

Sister Lilly was a nun, and long hours of prayer were not unusual. She often enjoyed them, as a time to meditate. But years of practice had taught her that in her current state of mind, if she tried to meditate, she was more likely to stoke her anger. So instead, she began to recite psalms.

She finished all the psalms she knew in the small hours — too late to be night, too early to be morning. Her heart was finally calm enough to meditate, and she chose as her focus one of the cathedral picture windows. In the day, the light shining through would show a saint handing out bread to children. But with just the candlelight from inside, all Sister Lilly could see was the children along the bottom with out-stretched arms.

----

When a canon arrived to set up for the dawn service, Sister Lilly stood up. Her knees protested, they had gone numb, and now she was demanding they work again. She ignored them, and moved back into a chair in the front row.

Dawn in the spring, when it was getting earlier and earlier, was never well attended. Two elderly friars shuffled in, and a nun Sister Lilly recognised by sight only. They all sat at the front as well. Together they sang the dawn service, although Sister Lilly's voice was sore from whispering for so much of the night.

At the end, Sister Lilly stood up again to resume her vigil. Out of the corner of her eye, she saw that another person had come to the service, although she hadn't heard their voice. She turned a little more to see who it might be, and realised it was Marcus. Alone.

Marcus caught her eye and nodded in recognition, with a small smile. And then yawned, rather dramatically.

Sister Lilly hid her surprise, and nodded in return. Then she turned, and walked back up to the prayer rail. Before she could kneel again, Marcus caught up. 

“Will I see you at the planning meeting in a few hours?” they whispered.

Sister Lilly was furious at the world for continuing as normal. A planning meeting? Where she would sit and smile and contribute nothing? She shook her head, and said “No, I am holding vigil.”

Marcus looked a little confused. Sister Lilly sighed internally. Of course, they had no idea what she meant. “My friend is in trouble, I am praying for them,” she said, not really wanting to explain every cultural detail.

“I'm sorry,” said Marcus, “Will you be available tomorrow?”

Sister Lilly desperately wished they would just leave her alone. She was tired, and sore, and the world was a cruel and dark place. Yes, at some point she would have to return to her cloister, if only to collapse for a few hours and eat a morsel of food. And she had promised to see Cindy again. All she had to offer Cindy was her time in prayer, how could she offer her time to anyone else?

Probably because she was so tired, she just blurted out, “I will pray until my friend isn't forced to marry a leech. I don't know when that will be.”

She turned, not waiting for an answer, and knelt at the rail. She probably shouldn't have been rude, and she should have made a noncommittal answer. But she hadn't, and it wasn't as if she'd been so rude she needed to apologise. 

She began reciting psalms again, but this time in her heart. *O, the delight and joy of that one, who walks not in the council of the wicked…*

Marcus, presumably, left.

---

Unlike the dawn service, the morning service was popular. Schools and shops would open at ten, giving plenty of time for many people to visit a chapel or cathedral for a nine o'clock service. When the choir filed in to warm up, Sister Lilly left the prayer rail and retreated to the back.

She debated returning to her cloister. She wanted quiet for her vigil, but her knees wanted rest on a chair before attempting to cycle home. *It's not about me, it's for Cindy*. It seemed a like cheating to sit in a chair, when she could be praying in the chapel at her cloister. *Isn't the prayers of everyone here greater than my prayers alone?* she debated with herself. 

In the end, she decided to stay for the service, and then return. She watched people come in to the cathedral. Some chatting, some serene, some sleepy. A few who looked as anguished as she felt. But it was a comfort to know they all came here seeking.

Moments before the appointed time, the Procurator strode in, with Marcus following. Sister Lilly was surprised. Had they cancelled the planning meeting because she couldn't make it? It seemed odd, when she was the one who contributed the least. Surely they could have gone on without her.

Perhaps Marcus had told Procurator Julius where she was, and why she wasn't coming. And the Procurator had decided to come themselves, and convince her to abandon her prayers. Then it was a good thing she was planning on slipping out as soon as the service finished, and they wouldn't get a chance.

Sister Lilly put Marcus and the Procurator out of her mind, and tried to focus on the liturgy. The familiar words and music felt like a warm blanket on a cold night. Something that had always been there, would always be there. As always, the choir was beautiful.
