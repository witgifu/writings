Cindy moved through that day in a kind of haze. It was dangerous. If she wasn't fully alert Ana might catch her out, but Cindy still struggled to be present in her own body. She was watching it from above, as she cleaned and cooked and washed, like it was any other day. 

Ana didn't catch her. The front door slammed as the sun went down over next door's walls. Cindy quickly finished drying the dishes, and headed back to the chapel.

Sister Lilly was waiting for her, in the circle of chairs, a lit oil lamp on the chair to her right. She patted the chair to her left. Obediently, Cindy walked over to join her. Sitting down, Cindy felt like she had slammed back into her own body. She let out a small gasp.

Again, Sister Lilly took her hand.

“I have a proposal for you,” she said softly.

Cindy didn't look up. She knew what was coming. It could only be a three-year wedding contract, since Cindy was eighteen, not a five-year contract. And afterwards, she would be free of her father and able to refuse. But it would be three years of hell. He couldn't kill her, but she would probably wish he would. And after three years, if she left, she would never see any children he forced on her again. That had been her mother's awful choice as well.

“I know someone else who wants to marry you,” Sister Lilly continued.

Cindy almost jumped with shock. She hadn't known anyone outside her family and Sister Lilly since she'd been taken out of school by her father on her sixteenth birthday. 

“Who?” She said, incredulously.

“Someone who can buy your father off,” said Sister Lilly, watching Cindy carefully, as if she might run.

“Wha— why me, I'm nobody! I don't think anyone apart from my family knows I exist!” Cindy said, increasingly nervous.

“You are important, love,” said Sister Lilly, gently. “Your father is the chair of the Board of Industry, and you're his only legitimate child.”

Cindy's mouth made a small “O”. She knew her father was busy, but nobody had ever thought it was essential to tell her what exactly he did. She only knew what the Board of Industry was from school.

“Ana wants to solidify her family's power,” continued Sister Lilly, “If you marry someone else, you could be a threat to her and her family. And your father owes someone a lot of money, which he can get from Andre.”

“But,” said Cindy in a small voice, “I don't want to marry. Like you, sister.”

Sister Lilly squeezed Cindy's hand, “If I thought any of us could convince your father to let you, I'd do it in heartbeat”

Cindy swallowed, and took a deep breath, “But if I agree to this mysterious man…”

“You will be free to do whatever you want after three years. And they won't lay a hand on you,” said Sister Lilly.

“He says, anyway…” said Cindy, and bit her lip, “Can he be trusted? I know Ana's brother is awful, but I also know…” Cindy trailed off.

Andre had started coming to visit the house when she was seventeen. One morning, as she had been cleaning the bedrooms, he had hid behind the door and grabbed her from behind. She'd frozen, expecting to get a slap for coming in the room while he was still there. Instead, he'd pulled her tight against his body, a grasping hand around her waist. “Soon,” he whispered in her ear, “If you're a good girl, soon.” Then he'd pushed her away, and walked out of the room as she staggered forward.

At dinner that night, while she was serving, Andre had said to her father, “Your eldest is a good girl, isn't she”, and her father had smiled and said nothing, not even looked at her.

It wasn't the last time. But if she stayed still, he would let her go, and do nothing more than run his sweaty hands over her.

Cindy didn't tell Sister Lilly this. She didn't know how to say it.

“It's complicated,” said Sister Lilly, “but I do believe they will be kind.”

Cindy couldn't quite believe Sister Lilly. It sounded impossible. Everyone's first marriage was arranged by their parent, to someone older who would introduce them into society. Some few marriages lasted beyond that, but marriage was mostly seen as an exchange — adulthood for children. You were then usually expected to make your second marriage to introduce someone else into society, often a relative of your first spouse. 

“But if I say yes, my father would still say no,” she said softly, “Father does whatever Ana asks.”

There was a twinkle in Sister Lilly's eye, “He could try.”

“Who _is_ this suitor?” Cindy asked again, “Who could stand up to my father?”

“The Procurator, Julius” said Sister Lilly, watching Cindy's face carefully.

Cindy turned white as a sheet. A foreigner. Procurators had a horrible reputation, they taxed ruthlessly, and skimmed off the top to fund their hedonistic lifestyles. And when they were recalled, they left without a care to their “favourites” left behind.

“No!” gasped Cindy, “I'm, I'm a good child! A good girl. No. No! I'll obey my father, and Ana, and marry her brother.” The shakes returned, and she tried to stop them by bracing her arms on her knees.

“This Procurator is new, and I promise you, they're nothing like anyone we've had before,” said Sister Lilly, stroking Cindy's hand, “They haven't thrown a single party.”

“So they're a bit less stupid than their predecessor,” said Cindy bitterly, “My father would never agree. And how do they even know I exist?”

“Your father dare not say no,” said Sister Lilly, her voice firm, “since the Procurator is the one he owes money to.”

“As to how the Procurator knows about you, well,” she added somewhat sheepishly, “I told them. Or rather, they wheedled half of it out of me, and guessed the rest.”

Cindy nearly fell off her chair in surprise, “And you think—you think this could work?”

Sister Lilly nodded, “I think it *will* work. I'll set things in motion. If I need to reach you, I'll leave a note by your back door.”

There was a lot to organise. Sister Lilly cycled home to the cloister with her mind spinning. But, now filled with a hope that made her feel so light it was as if she might drift off in the breeze.

The Procurator, with good reason, wanted to meet Cindy first, and hear from her own mouth that Cindy wanted to take part in this scheme. The problem was that Cindy was basically a prisoner in her own house, only able to sneak out occasionally to the local chapel. Sister Lilly didn't want Ana to be made aware of what was going on, so simply sending an invitation for Cindy to the Procurator's residence was out of the question.

And Procurator Julius was an important public figure who's every move was noted in the press. They could hardly sneak into Cindy's house, even turning up to a minor chapel would be commented upon. The list of those they met with at their own residence was a topic for discussion, and while Sister Lilly was an unremarkable visitor, her accompanying a young woman would be a cause for comment.

Sister Lilly needed to get Ana, Mathias, and Ana's two daughters out of the house for a good chunk of time. And then spirit Cindy out and back. And find somewhere for the Procurator and Cindy to meet that was both unremarkable and relatively private. Most public places, like the cathedral, were *too* public. Most private places, to suspicious. 

She arrived at the cloister, and put her bicycle away. Then she went looking for Mother Grace. She found her praying in the chapel after evening service. Sister Lilly's mind was so focused on the current problem, that the first thing she said to Mother Grace was “How wrong would it be for someone who was not a nun to dress as one?”

Mother Grace raised an eyebrow, “I don't think we could hide Lucinda here, Sister Lilly. At least not very long.”

Sister Lilly suddenly realised that Mother Grace had no idea what was going on. She quickly explained the updated situation. But instead of Mother Grace appearing hopeful, she looked concerned.

“Marriage is a sacred bond, Sister Lilly, it should be entered into out of mutual respect, for the benefit of both, and not on a whim,” said Mother Grace, sternly.

“And I would be so happy if that was an option Cindy had,” retorted Sister Lilly, “but the only legal requirement is for her parent, and both spouses, to consent.”

Mother Grace pursed her lips. “I don't approve of this… interference… from the capital,” she said, and Sister Lilly looked about to interrupt her, but she ploughed on, “But I conceded that it is not unprecedented.”

“And surely even a foreigner can't be any worse to her than her father has been, or Andre would be,” Mother Grace added.

“So you'll help me work out a way for them to meet?” asked Sister Lilly, hopefully.

Mother Grace groaned. “All right then, let's play matchmaker.”

For the rest of the evening, they thought hard about the problem of getting Cindy out of the house. The obvious solution would be for Ana and her daughters to go out to some event that would take many hours, and where they would want to stay the whole time. Unfortunately neither Sister Lilly nor Mother Grace were the kind of “society ladies” who threw all day and all night parties.

“Would Ana take her daughters to White Sunday celebrations, do you think?” asked Mother Grace, who looked like she would really rather like to go to bed.

“She'd probably make an appearance, but we want them out of the house for as long as possible,” replied Sister Lilly, rubbing her eyes. She'd been awake all night, and busy all day, and her brain was seriously fogging up.

Mother Grace sighed, and put her head on the table. “Anyone planning a fancy dress party, or a fête?” she asked, her voice a bit muffled.

Sister Lilly snorted. “As if anyone would tell us if they were,” she retorted, “first any of us would likely know is when they walked out the door in costumes.”

Mother Grace groaned. “I am not hosting a party,” she said into the table, “not here, not anywhere. I am too old to be up after midnight.”

Sister Lilly sat up straight, “What if the Procurator hosted a party?” she asked.

Mother Grace replied, “But then how would they meet Cindy if they're at a party with Ana?”

Sister Lilly shook her head, “No, like a fancy dress ball,” she said, but too tired now to get excited. “Ana and her daughters would surely be invited. And we can sneak Cindy in somehow.”

Mother Grace looked up, “It's not a bad idea, if you can convince them. I think we should sleep on it” she said.

Sister Lilly groaned her agreement, and went off to find her bed.
