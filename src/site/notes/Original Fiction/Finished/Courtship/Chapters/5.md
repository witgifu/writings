When the service finished, as planned, Sister Lilly slipped out. Down the stairs, and round the corner to the many bicycle racks. When she'd arrived, there had been few bicycles there, and she had put hers politely at the back, knowing she would be a while. Of course, now her bicycle was unreachable, and tangled in the bicycles of everyone else who had come for the morning service.

*Blast* she thought, and resigned herself to waiting. Over ten minutes, the tangle rapidly diminished, until she could almost reach hers. She watched the corner of the cathedral, hoping for more people to come and collect.

Marcus popped their head around the corner, ever so briefly. She'd been spotted. 

Sister Lilly sighed. She wasn't going to get away politely. At this point, she'd have to walk to get away before they reached her, and the next time they met she'd have to explain what she'd been up to. The tangle of bicycles felt like a metaphor for her emotions.

Sure enough, seconds later the Procurator stepped around the corner, with Marcus one step behind them. The Procurator smiled gently, but Marcus looked a little embarrassed.

Procurator Julius dispensed with any small talk. “You have been invaluable to me since I arrived,” they said, candidly, “Is there any way I can be of help your friend?”

Sister Lilly wanted to say that it wasn't their business, and they should just let her be. Instead, she went for a more diplomatic answer. “This is a local matter, your Excellency, and your involvement would probably only make things worse,” she replied, and bowed.

The Procurator did not give up. “Please, would you explain it to me?” they asked, “My ignorance could also be a danger.”

Sister Lilly groaned internally. She wanted to yell at them, to storm away. But how could she refuse what amounted to a direct order? She would have to hope that Mother Grace hadn't forgotten her promise to pray. *I will hold vigil for you again, tonight, Cindy* she silently promised her friend, *and I will pray in your chapel, where I won't be interrupted.*

“Very well, your Excellency,” she replied, trying to keep the anger out of her voice.

---

Sister Lilly had expected the Procurator to lead the way back to their official residence, but instead they went back into the cathedral. It was much closer, but not very private. As if reading her mind, Marcus approached the canon by the door, who was shaking hands with the last of the morning congregants.

“Excuse me, would there be a private space we could borrow? Only for fifteen minutes or so,” said Marcus, in their usual cheerful voice.

“Of course, of course,” replied the canon, looking at the Procurator instead of Marcus, “right this way, your Excellency.”

The canon led them to a minor vestry, that happened to have a pair of chairs with discarded robes. Embarrassed, the canon cleared off the robes and shuffled out.

Procurator Julius took one of the chairs, but Marcus chose to sit on the floor. Rather than taking the other chair, Sister Lilly just grabbed on to the back of it.

“What is happening to my friend is not unheard of, your Excellency,” she said, trying to keep emotion out of her voice, “there will probably always be parents who are cruel to their children.”

“Just because something has always been that way, doesn't mean it must,” said the Procurator, “You know nobody who might be able to persuade your friend's father?”

Sister Lilly shook her head, “Technically, your Excellency, he is acting within his legal rights. She is his child, and old enough to marry.”

Marcus looked unusually serious, “What if she married someone else?”

Sister Lilly grasped the edge of the chair tighter. “She doesn't want to marry anyone,” she said, trying not to give away all of Cindy's secrets, “And, your Excellency, she needs her father's permission to marry.”

The Procurator nodded, “I see. Why would her father pick someone so… unsuitable? Is it his friend?”

“I think he needs the money,” said Sister Lilly, “A *lot* of money.”

Marcus gasped, and rocked back. “He's selling his daughter?” they asked, astonished.

Wincing, Sister Lilly nodded. She had said the same to Mother Grace, but hearing it from Marcus made it seem even more horrifying.

Procurator Julius did not look so surprised. “My predecessor did something similar,” they said, glumly.

Marcus turned to look at the Procurator. “What?” they hissed, “They sold their child?!”

The Procurator shook their head. “No, they took a girl as a bribe. Possibly more than one,” they answered, softly, “Marriages here aren't recognised by the capital, so they did nothing illegal. When they left, the girls were sent back to their parents. My predecessor took the children home.”

Marcus swore, quietly, and then said, “My apologies, Sister Lilly.”

Sister Lilly smiled wryly. “I agree, your Excellency,” she said, forgetting that Marcus didn't like the honorific. They didn't appear to notice.

A thought flashed across Marcus' face, and they sat up straighter. “Do you know how much money?” they asked.

Shaking her head, Sister Lilly replied, “Not exactly, no, your Excellency. Perhaps enough to buy an engine, maybe more, maybe less.”

Marcus frowned, and narrowed their eyes. “Who did they borrow that kind of money from?” they asked. But the Procurator held up a hand to stop any answer, and looked closely at Sister Lilly. Leaning forward, they asked, “An engine?”

Sister Lilly nodded.

Procurator Julius kept their hand up, and Marcus gave them a quizzical look. “Is the father Mathias Roy, the mining magnate?” asked the Procurator.

A gasp escaped Sister Lilly. She didn't want to confirm or deny, but it seemed like the Procurator had pieced it all together too. She paused, frozen for a moment, before giving in. “He may have borrowed the money, your Excellency, probably from Andre Fortin, the groom, and just be trying to settle the debt,” she said, morosely.

Marcus winced, and, ignoring the Procurator's hand, added “Andre Fortin is a nasty bit of work. He might force something like that.”

“I don't think he did,” said the Procurator, slowly, “because Mathias owes that money to me.”

---

Marcus giggled. Then realising that wasn't appropriate to the gravity of the situation, covered their mouth to try to muffle it.

Sister Lilly, conversely, looked absolutely dumb struck. *How could Mathias Roy have borrowed money from Procurator Julius? They've only been here two months!*

“I mean,” clarified the Procurator, “he spent the money he owed in taxes to the capital. He was cooking the books. My predecessors didn't care, but I've been checking over the entire Board of Industry. Because, as Marcus is fond of saying, they are all in it up to their necks.”

Sister Lilly's stomach dropped. Mathias hadn't borrowed money from Andre, only to have Andre turn around later demand his daughter in return. He really was selling his daughter. He could probably have pieced together loans from others, and spent a bit less extravagantly, but it would need to be public, which would be embarrassing. Instead of facing the shame himself, he was selling his daughter. To pay his *tax bill*. 

Sister Lilly pushed aside the chair, and fell to her knees. “Your Excellency,” she said, her heart pounding, her eyes downcast, “I do not ask for Mathias, or for myself, but only for my dearest friend. Please, your Excellency, if I have found any favour with you at all, please.” She wasn't even sure what, exactly, she was asking for.

When there was no immediate response, she looked up. Procurator Julius looked torn. “I...” they said, and trailed off. They coughed, and tried again, “It would set a precedent, excusing a debt of that size. Once word got out, more than the Board of Industry would be withholding tax money. I would be recalled home in disgrace, and the army would be sent in to clean house.”

Sister Lilly crumpled, staring at the Procurator's elegant shoes. It was just like Mother Grace had said. Capital interference would cause devastation, not just for Cindy but for the whole province. And neither Mathias nor Andre would see justice. Sister Lilly understood the calculation the Procurator was making, and it filled her with despair.

“I am so sorry,” added the Procurator.

Marcus shuffled along the floor, and put a hand gently on Sister Lilly's shoulder. She wanted to shake it off, but she knew it was meant kindly. It wasn't Marcus' fault, but Marcus and Procurator Julius were both part of the system that was going to grind Cindy into dust.

They all sat there in awkward silence for a few moments. Eventually, Marcus broke it.

“Julius,” they said, cautiously, “I have a crazy idea.”

“Spit it out, Marcus,” replied the Procurator, “because I don't have any ideas.”

“If he would sell his daughter to pay his debt,” said Marcus, quietly, “make him sell her to you.”

There was another awkward silence.

“I do not want to set a precedent,” repeated the Procurator, “and I would make a bad spouse.”

“Not as bad as Andre Fortin,” retorted Marcus, “and you've been breathing down everyone's necks. A little corruption would make them relax.” The Procurator sighed.

“And I'm sure she could introduce you to the society of many reputable women,” Marcus added, a note of their usual cheer returning to their voice.

The bells above them rang for ten o'clock. They all started, and looked up, as if the bells were new, rather than something that rang every hour of the day.

As they finished ringing, Sister Lilly looked back at Procurator Julius. They were foreign, and strange, and Sister Lilly still wasn't entirely sure what their motives were. But as the last echoes died away, she resolved to beg anyway.

“Please, your Excellency,” she said, still on her knees, “Please, oh, please. If it is at all possible, if you could find it in your heart, your Excellency, please.”

Procurator Julius did not respond immediately. They looked at Marcus, as if hoping they might suggest another plan, or maybe asking for permission. Then they looked at Marcus' hand, still grasping Sister Lilly's shoulder, and Sister Lilly, still kneeling.

“Alright,” they said, eventually, “but only if the young woman agrees. And you'll have to tell me what to do, Sister Lilly.”

Sister Lilly looked up, her eyes shining with unshed tears. “If there is any way I can help at all, your Excellency, I will.”

“Well first,” said the Procurator, awkwardly, “I should probably know her name.”

Sister Lilly smiled, “Lucinda, your Excellency,” she replied.

The Procurator nodded, “I should like to meet Lucinda, if you can arrange that.”

“Of course, your Excellency, I would be delighted to introduce you,” replied Sister Lilly.
