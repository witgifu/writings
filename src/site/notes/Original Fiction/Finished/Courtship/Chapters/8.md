When Marcus confirmed the date, Sister Lilly sent word to Cindy.

Cindy had served breakfast, and was carrying carefully balanced trays back to the kitchen to wash. She saw a slip of paper just by the back door, and almost dropped everything in her excitement. Instead, she carried everything to the bench by the sink, put it down gently, and then dashed to grab the paper. 

> Dearest,
> Everything is going well. I'll visit at the full moon.

Cindy was glad the paper had no name, so if anyone found it, there was no link to her. Cindy assumed Sister Lilly was meaning for Cindy to go to the chapel again. She had to hope that for at least some of that day Ana and her daughters would be out for a good while. Cindy sighed, and went to do the washing up.

Later that day, Cindy heard a squeal of delight from one of Ana's daughters, coming from the front hall. Cindy crept along the back hallway to the door that joined them, polishing the door knob and plate. Listening to things you shouldn't get you in trouble, but *not* listening could also get you in trouble, so Cindy decided it was worth the risk.

“A ball!!” yelled the youngest, in a voice loud enough that Cindy did not need to be listening at doors to hear. Surely people on the street outside heard.

“A masquerade!” gasped the elder, with slightly more decorum.

Ana tutted. Despite being hidden, and knowing it wasn't pointed at her, Cindy tensed.

“Girls, girls, how delightful, what a lovely invitation,” said Ana, dryly, “we shall simply have to get you both the most stunning costumes. It will be the place to be *seen*.”

“It's less than a month away!” cried the younger, “We won't have time to get anything new made.”

“Mama has it in hand, darlings, we'll have you dressed beautifully, under the full moon, worry not,” replied Ana. Cindy could see the sickly sweet smile that would be on her face, dotting on her daughters.

“But,” continued Ana, “We really must be going *now*, or everyone else with an invitation will have all the best seamstresses sewn up.”

All three left the house in a whirl of chatter about who they might go dressed as.

As the door clicked behind them, Cindy smiled. A ball would have them out from mid-afternoon until the small hours of the morning. Plenty of time for Cindy to visit the chapel and catch up with Sister Lilly.

---

As the ball approached, the air in the house got tenser. 

Both girls tore clothes, pulling them on and off with repeated fittings at the dressmakers. Cindy had to mend every tear. They were both angry that the rips were not repaired to their impossible standards. The younger slapped Cindy, the elder threw a slipper. 

One morning, Cindy was cleaning the kitchen.

“CINDY!” screamed Ana, from overhead.

Cindy dropped the scrub brush onto the floor, and bolted upstairs. As she ran, she wiped her soapy hands on the back of her apron. She slowed as she approached Ana's bedroom door, and knocked.

“Enter” said Ana, calmly.

Cindy carefully turned the handle, and stepped inside. Ana was standing beside her dressing table, with a vial of perfume at her feet and a slowly spreading damp patch around it.

“How many times,” said Ana, her voice now dangerously quiet, “Do I have to tell you to tidy up?”

Cindy bowed, “I am so sorry ma'am,” she said, her voice meek and quiet. There was no point denying anything. If Ana said she had left a mess, she must have. Cindy did not remember it, but Ana had a keen eye for detail.

“Clean it up then,” said Ana, pointing to the puddle.

Cindy hadn't thought to grab a rag on her way up, and she knew that leaving to get one would be seen as dallying. So she untied her apron, and knelt on the floor by Ana's feet. The perfume was sickly sweet, but Cindy dared not do anything more than silently dab it up.

Ana waited, standing perfectly still, until Cindy had finished dabbing the oily mess, and moved to pick up the broken glass. As Cindy's hand stretched out to pick up the two pieces, Ana stomped on it, crushing her hand into the thick, cut glass.

Cindy bit the inside of her mouth to stop herself from screaming. But she couldn't stop the tears from streaming down her face as Ana slowly removed her slippered foot. Ana grabbed Cindy's hair, pulling her head back, so Cindy was forced to look into Ana's mirthless grin.

“Let that be a lesson,” she said, quietly.

She threw Cindy backwards, and stormed out of the room.

Cindy rolled over, and got to her knees, cradling her cut and bleeding hand in her skirt, trying not to move it. She went back to the apron, and with her good hand, picked up every tiny fragment of glass she could find and put them on the apron. She rubbed the tips of her fingers over the wood floor, feeling for any specks as small as a grain of sand. 

When she was sure she had everything, she wrapped up the glass in the apron, and awkwardly stood up. She made her way out of the room, back down the stairs, trying to make sure any blood dripped onto her skirt and not the floor. 

She put the apron with the glass pieces on the kitchen table, and grabbed the big bottle of iodine and some clean rags from the laundry. From a drawer in the kitchen, she pulled out a pair of tweezers meant for removing fish bones.

Cindy sat down at the table. Trembling, and with her less favoured hand, she began pulling tiny pieces of glass out of her hand. After each, she dabbed iodine on the cut.

She wanted to cry out as each piece came out, but she must be silent. She stopped to wipe away her tears. Adding salt to her wounds would not help.

As she worked, she racked her brains to see if there was anything else she could think of that she had done wrong, that Ana might have been punishing her for. Had she failed to remove a stain from one of Ana's dresses? Had the toast been burnt at breakfast? Sometimes Ana liked to make Cindy guess the crime, but Cindy couldn't think of anything. It must have simply been that the perfume was expensive, or rare.

Eventually, she got the five little pieces out. None of the cuts were deep, thankfully. Cindy soaked the rag in iodine and gently wrapped the rag around her hand. A small hiss escaped her, but despite the streaming tears, she made no other sound.

“For the moment all discipline seems painful,” quoted Cindy to herself, and braced herself against the chair. She wrapped a clean bandage rag over the iodine soaked one. And then, realising she had lost half an hour, hurried to the pantry to try to start lunch with one hand.

---

Ana slapped Cindy repeatedly over the next few days for being clumsy. Cindy took it stoically — she was clumsy, with only one good hand — and offered no more reply than a small “I'm very sorry ma'am.”

Thankfully, Ana and both her daughters were out of the house for much of the time. Fittings, shoes, hats, all sorts of shopping. And of course meeting up in tea houses to talk with all her friends about what they and their daughters were wearing.

And then, finally, the day of the ball arrived. At lunch, Ana declared that dinner would not be required, and they would be back late. “You'll need to clean up after we get back,” said Ana, haughtily, “so no dozing off in the parlour, girl.”

Cindy bowed, but did not reply. She cleaned away the dishes, and wiped down the dining table. After lunch, she re-wrapped her hand. It was almost healed enough to leave off, but fear of infection had Cindy slathering it in iodine again and wrapping it back up.

Cindy didn't get a chance to see the costumes, although from hints dropped over the weeks, gathered some details. The youngest was going as a fairy princess. The elder had wanted to be a fairy, but couldn't well go as the same as her sister, and so was going as the virtue “Prudence”. Ana was going as some foreign goddess, no doubt wrapped in expensive foreign fabrics, just to show them off.

Cindy was looking forward to a good stretch of time alone, even if she would probably only get four hours sleep at the end of it. She could maybe sneak a read of a magazine in the parlour, or wash her hair. But first, she would get to see Sister Lilly.

The hired cyclo driver rang the door promptly at five. Cindy listened to the bustle of Ana herding her two daughters out the door, and then the slam. She counted slowly to a hundred and twenty, in case one of them realised they had forgotten something and came back. When she heard nothing, Cindy raced out the back door and round the corner to the Chapel.

---

When Cindy arrived, Sister Lilly was already waiting. Belatedly, she put her bandaged hand behind her back.

“Oh dear, what happened to your hand?” asked Sister Lilly, standing up and walking over to close the gap.

Cindy shrugged, “I cut it on some broken glass, it's almost healed now.”

Sister Lilly gave a sympathetic tut. “Can I look, dear?” she asked.

Reluctantly, Cindy held out her bandaged hand. Sister Lilly took it gently, turning it left and right, but not unwrapping it. “Did you trip while holding a bottle? And it broke?” she asked softly.

“Mmm… something like that,” said Cindy, embarrassed. “But tell me all the news! Ana and the girls are gone all night so we can have a good long chat.”

Sister Lilly dropped Cindy's hand reluctantly. But her eyes sparkled. “I know they are,” she said, unable to keep a note of excitement out of her voice, “I arranged it.”

Cindy looked aghast. “So there isn't a ball? Oh, Ana is going to be in such a state when she gets home, I must get back before they notice—” 

Cindy turned and took a step to leave, but Sister Lilly grabbed her elbow gently. “There really is a ball,” she said quickly, “and there's midnight fireworks so they should be there at least until then.”

A gasp of relief escaped Cindy, and she turned back. “You paid for a ball for the whole city so you could come and talk to me?” she asked, confused.

“No, I don't have that kind of money,” answered Sister Lilly, laughing, “I asked someone else to host it, and they were happy to. And it's not for *me* to talk to you.”

Cindy looked even more lost, “But…” she said, unable to put together a question.

“It's for *you* to go to as well, and talk to their Excellency, Procurator Julius,” said Sister Lilly. “And to have some fun,” she added.

Cindy tripped over backwards, but thankfully the wall was not far behind her, and she fell against that rather than the floor. Sister Lilly, her face all concern again, held her by both elbows and looked into her eyes.

“Are you all right, dear?” she asked, kindly, “We should be going, but if you aren't well…”

Cindy shook her head, “I'm fine, just shocked is all,” she said, “What will I wear? What if I'm seen?” She looked down at her worn dress and apron. The perfume had faded, and she'd got the blood out fine, but while they were *clean* they were hardly what one would wear to chapel, much less to a ball. She would stand out a mile away, and Ana would surely spot her.

“I've got a costume for you,” said Sister Lilly, “Come to the cloister with me, and we'll dress you up. Nobody will recognise you, I promise.”

Obediently, Cindy followed Sister Lilly out of the chapel. Sister Lilly pulled out her bicycle onto the street, and pointed Cindy to the parcel rack. Cindy hopped up, as she hadn't done since she was a small child, and they were off.
