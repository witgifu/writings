Sister Lilly watched Procurator Julius and Cindy take a turn about the kitchen garden. She hoped that in time they could be friends. Cindy had always been such a bright, happy child, but Sister Lilly had watched as she faded away once her father remarried. Perhaps, outside the home of her father and his wife, she could be happy again.

Cindy wasn't used to walking in the costume. Well, who would be. She tangled herself on a particularly overgrown rosemary bush, as the stars grabbed on to the thick stems. Sister Lilly watched her pull herself free. The Procurator wanted to help, she could see them offering words of encouragement, but they chose very carefully to not touch Cindy.

Of course, a young lady should only touch her suitor's hands with gloves on. But it would have been perfectly acceptable for the Procurator to help re-arrange the hem of her skirt. For those with a romantic turn of mind, that would give them the chance to look up adoringly into their intended's eyes.

It seemed, however, that Procurator Julius had read into Cindy's questions something that even Sister Lilly hadn't noticed. Of course, Sister Lilly knew Cindy really wanted to be a nun, but not all nuns were virgins, and Cindy had often expressed a strong calling of faith. But now, Sister Lilly wondered if perhaps Andre *had* touched her. Oh, she wanted to ring that man's neck. And Mathias, for letting his daughter be treated like that. And Ana for good measure, who was probably complicit.

Sister Lilly had planned to return Cindy home after the ball. But for the first time she realised just how much danger that could put Cindy in. If Mathias came home with the news of the Procurator's proposal, Ana, and maybe Andre, would retaliate. If Mathias hadn't protected Cindy before, would he do so now? Could he?

She ruminated, and watched the other two walking amongst the vegetables. It would be safer, she decided, if Cindy never went home again. Sister Lilly stood up, and went over to join them.

“The sages tell us that watercress tea may cure jaundice, or reduce a fever, your Excellency,” said Cindy, as Sister Lilly approached. Apparently the Procurator was getting a lesson in traditional remedies.

Sister Lilly bowed to the Procurator. “Your Excellency,” she said, “I would advise, that to… shrive her spirit before taking solemn vows… you send her into religious retreat until your wedding.”

Both Cindy and the Procurator looked extremely confused, but the Procurator caught on first. “Thank you for your excellent spiritual advice, Sister Lilly. Would this be traditional?” they asked.

“Many women choose to spend time before important life-altering events in prayer and contemplation,” she replied, “before the birth of a child, or after the ending of a marriage, and yes, sometimes before one starts.”

Cindy looked between Sister Lilly and the Procurator trying to work out what they meant. She didn't have any objection, but she wasn't sure exactly what she needed to confess before marrying.

Sister Lilly realised Cindy was still lost. “Your father will tell Ana,” said Sister Lilly. Cindy's mouth formed a perfect 'O', her eyes went wide to match, and she looked like someone had tied her to a garden stake like a tomato plant. “If you come home with me,” continued Sister Lilly, gently, “she can't get you.”

“But,” said Cindy, her voice shaking, “If I'm not there when she gets home…” Vases would be smashed, doors banged, voices raised. And if something went wrong, if Cindy did have to go back… Cindy's mind went blank with horror. She would be beaten black and blue, starved, and locked outside to sleep in the garden.

“I assume your father is here tonight?” asked the Procurator of Cindy.

“Ye— yes, your Excellency,” replied Cindy, still shaking.

Procurator Julius bowed to Cindy, “I will leave you in Sister Lilly's capable hands then,” they said, and walked out of the garden.

“S— Sister Lilly?” said Cindy.

“Leave your father and Ana to Procurator Julius,” said Sister Lilly, a bit of steel in her voice, “let's get you back to the cloister so you have an alibi.”

---

Rather than heading back through the ball, Sister Lilly showed Cindy a way through the library. They interrupted a couple having a blazing row, with the woman storming off into the garden, and the man slumping onto a chair and looking moody. From there they went out some french doors, and onto the front lawn.

Sister Lilly spotted their cyclo driver, having a drink and a game of cards with some other drivers by the front stairs. She waved him over, but he didn't seem upset to be leaving. “All right?” he asked them, casually.

“It's lovely,” lied Sister Lilly, “but I'm afraid the noise has given this young lady the start of a migraine.”

The driver nodded, and went to get his vehicle out of the parking. While he was gone, Sister Lilly turned to Cindy. “I'm afraid you'll probably be stuck in the cloister for two months,” she said, sheepishly.

“How much laundry is there?” asked Cindy, “How much cleaning?”

“Oh,” replied Sister Lilly, “Well we're all on the roster, so you usually have to do a bit of something every day. Today I helped make breakfast and clean up afterwards.”

“Sounds like a wonderful holiday,” said Cindy, her voice warm and dreamy.

“Twice a week we have midnight service?” offered Sister Lilly.

“What's the punishment for falling asleep?” countered Cindy.

“A gentle shake from the person sitting next to you,” replied Sister Lilly, “Or an elbow in the rib if you start to snore.”

Cindy grinned. “I think I'll be alright.”

The cyclo driver pedalled over to them, and Sister Lilly handed Cindy up into the back.

“Aren't you coming too?” Cindy asked, when Sister Lilly didn't join her.

“*You* need an alibi,” said Sister Lilly, “*I* want to hear your father dressed down by the Procurator”

Cindy shuddered, and huddled back into the cyclo seat. “Better you than me!” she said, and the cyclo driver pulled away and headed down the street.

---

Sister Lilly returned through the front door to the party. She spotted Marcus again, in the same place, but couldn't see the Procurator or Mathias Roy. Most of the crowd had moved on from the entry hall, so Sister Lilly was able to walk over to Marcus without any trouble.

“You look magnificent,” she said, honestly.

Marcus smiled, “Did you have success?” they asked, ignoring the compliment. 

“Oh yes,” said Sister Lilly, “I believe their Excellency is cornering the father now.”

Marcus' smile converted into a grin, “Oh, I'd love to hear this,” they said.

“Me too,” she replied, “I was going to see if I could find them and eavesdrop.”

“I'm afraid I'll have to stay here and be the life of the party,” said Marcus, “but if Julius has any sense, they've had the culprit summoned to that main meeting room we use all the time.”

Sister Lilly thanked Marcus for the tip, and headed off to the meeting room.

Outside, as usual, was Aiden. Sister Lilly raised an eyebrow in question. Aiden pointed to the meeting room, and nodded with wide eyes. Then he beckoned Sister Lilly over.

“I just let Mathias Roy in,” he whispered, “Their Excellency said if you arrived you could go in.”

Sister Lilly had expected to be listening at keyholes. She didn't want to pass up the invitation. She walked over to the door, took a deep breath, opened it and stepped in.

---

“Why is she here?” spat Mathias, as soon as Sister Lilly walked through the door.

Sister Lilly bowed respectfully to the Procurator, and ignored Mathias Roy.

“This is my house,” said Procurator Julius, as if they were discussing expected grain yields, “and I invited her.”

Mathias grunted in response. Sister Lilly could not believe how rude they were being to the Procurator. This was a side to Mathias she hadn't seen in any meeting.

“As I was saying,” continued the Procurator, “I have evidence that your family is behind in taxes—” 

“—I'm sure it's just an accounting error,” interrupted Mathias, “I will have it fixed shortly.”

Procurator Julius steepled their fingers together, resting their elbows on the table. Mathias had not been invited to take a seat, and was standing, grasping onto the back of the chair closest to the door. The Procurator stared at Mathias, without blinking, for at least thirty seconds. Mathias looked defiant, and then awkward, and then looked away.

“Are you quite finished?” asked the Procurator.

“I just think, if you give me a bit more time, I'm sure I can have it all sorted,” burst out Mathias again, leaning forward across the table, “it's only a few coins, man, give me time.”

“Oh no, I don't think so,” said the Procurator, calmly, “I currently have proof of tax debts of 1,302 sol, but I expect the final number to be much higher.”

Sister Lilly swallowed hard. That was an extraordinary amount of money. An almost unthinkable amount of money. A single sol could house and feed a family of four for a fortnight. She had assumed the tax fraud had begun recently, but that kind of debt would have taken many years. Would he have even been able to get that much money from Andre?

Mathias also looked stunned. “That can't be right,” he said, flustered, “there must be some mistake, it can't be more than point one percent of that.”

“Yes, I was quite surprised too,” said the Procurator, as if commenting on a sudden change in the weather, “but I've had three accountants go over the papers, and that's the current minimum. As I said, I expect the final total to be higher, as we haven't gone back more than five years.”

Mathias began to visibly sweat. The Procurator let him, leaving another, even longer, awkward space. Sister Lilly almost wanted to say something, anything, to break the horrible tension in the room. But she held her tongue.

Eventually, Mathias broke the silence. “Your Excellency,” he said, his tone much more conciliatory, “surely we can reach some kind of agreement…” and trailed off.

“I could forcibly sell everything you own,” said the Procurator, now with the tiniest hint of steel in their voice, “and send you to a debtors' prison in the capital. That should clear it.”

The silence resumed. Sister Lilly imagined the auction, as every stick of furniture from his house, every bit of rusting mining equipment, even the mine itself, was sold off. The public humiliation and shame would be sweet. But it wouldn't save Cindy.

It was left to Mathias to break the silence again. “Your Excellency… please… my wife, and her daughters…” he didn't seem to know exactly how to frame his plea.

Procurator Julius waved their hand, as if swatting away Mathias' objections. “Are no concern of mine. As far as I am aware, they owe the capital nothing,” said the Procurator.

Again the silence settled down. Although this time, Sister Lilly could hear a squeaking sound, as Mathias twisted his hands over the back of the chair. His knuckles were white, and a large sweat patch had formed on the back of his white poet's shirt.

But this time, the Procurator spoke first. “You *do* have something I want though,” they said, in the same steel-tipped voice.

Confused hope spread over Mathias' face. “Yes, your Excellency?” he asked.

“Your daughter,” said the Procurator.

Mathias looked wildly around the room, as if expecting her to suddenly appear. When nobody else was to be seen, and the door didn't open, Mathias turned back to the Procurator. “How— how much is she worth?” he said, swallowing hard in the middle.

Sister Lilly was disgusted. He hadn't asked what the Procurator intended to do with her, even if he intended to marry her. He just wanted to know how much of the debt hole she could fill in. As if she was a prized piece of art, or a home in the countryside.

The Procurator didn't skip a beat. “One thousand, five hundred sol. You have a year to clean up anything else after that,” they said, looking Mathias straight in the eye.

Mathias visibly deflated. Sister Lilly guessed that was just under how much he owed, once everything was fully accounted for. There might be less expensive wine and fine new fabrics in his house, but he would get out with most of his dignity. Sister Lilly wanted him to suffer more, it hardly seemed like justice, but she knew that the Procurator was walking a fine line. He needed Mathias to agree, and he needed it to be more money than Andre could offer.

“I accept, your Excellency,” he said, very quietly.

“Good!” said the Procurator in their previous, calm, discussing-the-weather voice, “I will send you the paperwork tomorrow.”

Mathias simply nodded in response.

“You may go, Mathias,” said Procurator Julius. Mathias shuffled out of the room, not looking at the Procurator or Sister Lilly. He left the door to the meeting room open, but slammed the door to the vestibule.

The Procurator sighed, “I'm going to have to keep an eye on that bastard.” In front of them, on the table, Sister Lilly noticed a single glittering silver star.
