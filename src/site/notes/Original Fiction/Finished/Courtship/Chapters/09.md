Cindy was greeted at the cloister door by a gaggle of excited nuns. Some of them she recognised, others were new. They led her inside to the refectory, as Sister Lilly put away her bicycle. Covering the long tables was a cacophony of costumes, and at the end was a pile of old trunks they must have come out of. 

A golden gown with a spiked tiara must be the sun, draped next to a silver and black confection that Cindy supposed was a spider. Next was a pile of lace-edged white silk that resembled nothing more than a giant handkerchief. And down the tables they marched, fantastical creatures and allegories, miners and farmers, and some costumes that were surely not meant to be worn in public.

“The rest of these will be going to schools,” said Mother Grace, “… well, most of them anyway. I'm not sure exactly what to do with a couple.”

There was an anonymous snigger from the crowd in the doorway.

But Cindy's eye was immediately drawn to an indigo velvet dress, covered in tiny silver stars. It had billowing sleeves, and a beaded bodice that twinkled. Beside it was a pair of long indigo gloves, and a black wood staff topped by a silver moon. “The night sky,” she whispered, in awe.

“I thought you'd like that one,” said Sister Lilly, who had just walked in the door.

“Oh, but they're all too lovely, I couldn't possibly—” began Cindy, but her words were cut off by several nuns at once, telling her that of course she could. They gathered all around her, and undressed her and helped her into the beautiful 'Night Sky' dress she had admired so. 

When they found her bandaged hand, they hissed with sympathy, and tutted at the makeshift bandage. One of them ran off and returned with a brand-new bandage, that smelled of cloves. They wrapped up her hand gently, and then slid the indigo glove over the top. 

Someone covered her hair in some kind of powder, and deftly piled and pinned it up. Someone else washed her face, and rubbed a little red powder on her cheeks and lips.

Sister Lilly handed her the staff. “You look beautiful,” she said, warmly.

Cindy felt like an imposter. How could she go to the ball? She had no idea how to dance, or what to say, or where to stand. She was a cook and a cleaner, dressed up like her father's wife.

Seeing something of this in Cindy's face, Sister Lilly said, “Let the dress lead. You're not going to the ball — the Night Sky is. The night sky belongs everywhere.”

A flicker of relief passed over Cindy's face. Sister Lilly winked, and pulled her back outside. “Let's go!” she said, and pointed to a cyclo. The driver nodded to them both, and they climbed in the back.

They arrived at the ball late, but not awkwardly so, and not alone, others were still arriving too. They had missed the first dances, and most of the chaperones had wandered from the ballroom towards food or card games as their preference. But this kind of party tended to start small, and build to a crescendo.

Sister Lilly helped Cindy out of the cyclo, and they went up the stairs to the Procurator's residence. Sister Lilly proffered an invitation to the person at the door, who bowed them inside. Cindy held gently on to Sister Lilly's arm as they entered the main hall, like an elegant lady.

There was an elaborate electric light display, twinkling through thousands of cut glass, hanging from the ceiling. Even Sister Lilly, who walked through this hallway multiple times a week, was impressed. Swirling around the room were people of every age, in all sorts of elaborate costumes, most incredibly fine, and some bejewelled. And at the back, Sister Lilly spotted Marcus, dressed as promised as an old philosopher.

Somehow, Marcus' plain costume of pure white linen and no headdress or crown, made everyone around them look overdressed and gaudy. They were in the middle of a knot of some members of the Board of Industry, Sister Lilly couldn't quite recognise who, in their costumes.

Cindy knew, though, her grip on Sister Lilly's arm changed from a gentle rest to a tight grasp. Her father was here. Dressed as a romantic poet, which was almost comical, but here. He wasn't looking at her now, but any minute he would see her, and she would be in so much trouble.

He did turn, but his eyes passed over the whole assembly. But he didn't stop, he didn't notice her. And Cindy realised Sister Lilly was right, “The Night Sky” had come to the ball, not Cindy, and since nobody was expecting to see Cindy, nobody did.

Sister Lilly began steering Cindy through the crowd. More people looked at Cindy, and then looked away without recognising her. Slowly, she relaxed her grip on Sister Lilly. 

Sister Lilly took them past a table of refreshments. There were piles of fruit, carefully cut into balls, and with a pick stuck into each one so you didn't have to use a plate or a fork. Also, tiny pastries, and sweets moulded into beautiful miniature animals. There was glass after glass of wine, and even glasses of shaved ice to cool down those who had danced too much. 

They went through an open doorway into the ballroom, where the dancers made such a whirl of colour, that Cindy couldn't identify anyone. She envied them, it looked like such fun, but Cindy had no idea how to dance. The music was loud, and so was the chatter and laughter, and it began to press on Cindy.

Sister Lilly led her around the edge of the dancers, and through into another room. Here, it was blessedly quieter. A scatter of card tables, mostly occupied by people chatting rather than playing cards. Another nun, presumably also an escort for an otherwise lone young person, nodded to Sister Lilly. But nobody interrupted them as they walked out the open glass doors, and onto the patio.

Small groups walked among the gardens, young people laughing, and their chaperones chiding them, or ignoring them. The gardens were lit by hundreds of tiny candles, and more floated in the pond. Sister Lilly led Cindy onwards, down the stairs. But not into the formal gardens, where the others were.

Instead, they took a gravel path, between the gardens and the house. It led them around the corner, through a gate in a high brick wall, and into the kitchen garden. On a bench in the corner, sat someone, dressed as an ancient general. Their tunic did not cover their knees, and while it was a warm night, they looked a bit under-dressed.

Sister Lilly wanted to burst out laughing. Marcus had had their way here too, and the Procurator was indeed dressed almost exactly like the statue by the port. They looked extremely uncomfortable. Sister Lilly wondered what Marcus had done to get the Procurator dressed so outlandishly.

Procurator Julius must have heard the gate close, because they looked up suddenly from their contemplation of the herb beds, and saw Sister Lilly and Cindy. They smiled, and waved.

“Follow my lead,” whispered Sister Lilly, and Cindy resumed her tighter grip on Sister Lilly's arm.

Sister Lilly led Cindy up to the Procurator, who stood up and attempted to pull their tunic down to cover more of their legs.

“Your Excellency,” said Sister Lilly, bowing while attempting to pry Cindy's hand off her arm, “may I present Lucinda Roy.”

Cindy bowed, as she would have to Ana or her father, and kept her eyes firmly on the Procurator's sandals. She had just now realised that she was afraid of her father, and her father was afraid of the Procurator. Cindy wasn't entirely sure how anyone could be more frightening than her father, but she wanted to throw up into the lavender.

Procurator Julius didn't step forward, or reach out a hand. Instead, they bowed in return and said, “Would you like to join me? The garden does smell wonderful at night.”

Cindy didn't know how to reply, her tongue seemed to have swelled up in her mouth. But thankfully, Sister Lilly filled the space for her.

“Why thank you, your Excellency, that would be delightful,” she said, and moved to take the middle of the bench. She waited to sit until the Procurator had sat down, and then sat down herself. Cindy, who was still holding tightly to Sister Lilly's arm, was forced to sit or fall down, and chose to sit.

Cindy looked at her own feet, in delicate indigo leather slippers. Why were they here alone? Was the Procurator going to withdraw their offer? But surely they could have just sent a note rather than hold this elaborate party?

“I wanted to make my offer in person, Mistress Roy,” said the Procurator, slowly, “to be sure you understood. I cannot offer you what you might expect from a marriage.”

Cindy looked up a little, instead watching Procurator Julius's hands on their knees, but did not make eye contact. Since she hadn't been asked a question, she assumed no answer was required.

“I cannot offer you children, or love,” continued the Procurator, “I cannot even promise to stay for three years, as I could be recalled at any time. I can only offer you protection while I am here, and the freedom to choose your future when I leave.”

Cindy was startled into speech. “No children, your Excellency? But why… would you marry me…?” she said, haltingly.

The Procurator shuffled a little on the seat. “I cannot offer you children,” they said, quietly, and paused. “But I would not want you to imagine you will owe me some debt,” they continued, “your presence, if you agree, would mean I would be able to talk to many more respectable women. It would smooth many things in the relationship between your people and the capital.”

Sister Lilly added, “It would not be a one-sided contract, it would be an unusual marriage contract, to be sure, but it would be fair. But Cindy, do you understand what you would have to do?”

“I will be an ornament to your house,” quoted Cindy, softly, “your Excellency.”

“There will be meetings, and dinners,” said Sister Lilly, “and when the men who come to them see you there, they will tell the women, and they will start to come too. It may take a year, or more, to convince some people.”

“And some may never change their minds,” added in the Procurator, honestly, “I have many decades of bad behaviour to make up for.”

Cindy finally looked at Procurator Julius's face. She saw no pity, or anger. Only hope.

“… but…” said Cindy, unsure how to word her question, and her heart thundering in her chest, “a marriage… two people… even if no children…?”

The Procurator understood the question between her words. “You would be to me as beautiful and untouchable as the night sky,” they said, gesturing to her dress, “I would not assume to even hold your hand.”

Cindy, reminded of the state of her hand, blushed furiously, and rubbed her injured palm through her glove. The clove had eased the lingering pains, but it still hurt to press into.

It was, if not everything she had dreamed of, none of the things she had most dreaded. Perhaps over time, they would change their mind, and want more. But she would get some respite, far more than she could expect from Andre. Whatever her dreams had been, these were her choices, and she offered up a silent prayer of thanks that she was able to choose at all.

“I accept, your Excellency, with most gracious thanks,” she said softly, “I only hope that you will teach me kindly what you need of me.”
