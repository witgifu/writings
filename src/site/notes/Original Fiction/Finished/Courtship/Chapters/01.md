Cindy woke up as the predawn light started to creep into her tiny room through the bare window. For a precious few moments, she imagined she didn't have to get up, savouring the crisp air and soft old blanket. In silence, she recited a morning prayer.

Giving in to the inevitable, she carefully folded away the blanket and sleeping mat, and took her dress down from the wall hanger. It had dried overnight in the cool spring air. She shrugged it on, and tied the waist strings behind. It was almost scandalously short, but Cindy had already let down all the hem and left it with the smallest possible roll. She tied her apron over the top.

Cindy knew better than to ask her father or his wife Ana for a new dress. The less time she spent near them, or their twin daughters, the better. Her old dress was warm enough if she kept busy, and if she was busy her father's wife probably wouldn't be angry.

It was morning, so it was time to work. Cindy swept the kitchen and scullery, which was also her bedroom. She assembled trays of breakfast, and delivered them to bedroom doors, knocking and dashing before they were opened. She wiped down every kitchen surface, and dusted and swept the front rooms.

When she heard footsteps coming from the bedrooms, she quickly grabbed her tools and retreated to the kitchen. As the morning wore on, her father was heard leaving the house. Cindy took deliveries of groceries to the back door, as the daughters finally emerged from their bedrooms, yawning and chatting, to lounge in the parlour and browse through books of prints. 

Once she was sure all the bedrooms were empty, Cindy began working on those rooms. She hung the blankets to air, tidied discarded clothes, and put away rejected ribbons. Just as she was finishing in her father's room, the door creaked shut behind her. 

Cindy froze, her heart racing. Ana was behind her, she had snuck in, Cindy was sure of it. What had she got wrong? Cindy's mouth went dry. Maybe Ana was just in a bad mood, and wanted someone to take it out on. Maybe Cindy had made the breakfast wrong, or forgotten to order something in.

“Girl, look at me,” said Ana, her voice offering no indication of anger.

Cindy spun around on the spot, putting her hands behind her back, and looked at Ana's feet. Eye contact would be a mistake, it would be insubordinate. Cindy tried desperately to disappear. To be as still and invisible as possible, even while she knew Ana was looking right at her.

“You're eighteen today,” said Ana, in the same flat tone.

Cindy had known this, but she didn't respond. It wasn't a question, although she wasn't actually aware that Ana had known when her birthday was. It wasn't as if Cindy would be having a party with friends, or even a cake. Cindy had faintly hoped that maybe her father might remember, and give her a hair ribbon or a sweet, like he had when she was a child, but it was a very faint hope.

Ana walked up to Cindy, such that she could no longer take refuge in looking at Ana's feet, and was forced to look at her chest instead. Cindy desperately wanted to step back, she was too close, but moving without permission was also not allowed. Ana reached up a thin hand, and grabbed Cindy's chin painfully, forcing her to look at Ana's face.

Cindy's body was braced, every muscle taut, her mind blank, her nose full of Ana's perfume. She waited for Ana to strike her, or yell. But Ana didn't look angry, just curious. So it wasn't the breakfast, or something she'd missed. What was going on?

“My brother is looking for a wife,” said Ana, turning Cindy's face as if examining it for spots, “He needs a good girl. You're a good girl aren't you?”

Without thinking, Cindy replied automatically, “Yes, m'am.”

Ana smiled, “Perfect, I'll make the arrangements then,” she said, releasing Cindy's chin. She turned, and swept out of the room, leaving the door open. Cindy could hear her shoes click down the stairs and across the hall as she joined her daughters in the parlour.

Cindy collapsed, as if Ana's grasp had been all that had held her up. Shivers racked her body, as if a wind blew off the snow, but the sun beat on her through the window. Her mind was full of the image of Ana's smile, and the words “my brother” seemed to echo through her mind like a bell in a cathedral.

Part of her mind broke free of the terror, “not here,” it whispered, “not now.” Cindy dragged herself up, still shaking. She put the rest of the room to rights, holding everything with two hands lest her shakes cause her to drop it.

When she had finished with the room, she quietly made her way back to the kitchen. Closing the door behind her, she slid down against the smooth paint, until she was kneeling on the floor. Now, where nobody would find her, it was safe to break down. 

She didn't cry out, but tears streamed down her face, as she gasped for air, and her shivers turned to shakes. She rubbed her hands over the slate floor, polished by use, as if looking for a way to dig through it. Mind whirling, she couldn't form the words of any prayer, but in the same small voice inside her mind, she screamed.

Nobody came to comfort her. Nobody would. Cindy knew how the next two months would play out, her father and Ana and her daughters going on with life just as any other day. And then Ana would give Cindy a new dress — wouldn't want to be shamed by the dress Cindy wore now — and would take her to the registrar. 

Her father would sign, and Ana's brother would sign. The pen would be handed to Cindy, and of course she would sign, what choice would she have? If she refused, she would be taken home and beaten, and then dragged back the next day and the next, until she complied. Her father was within his rights to beat his child for disobedience; although he often delegated such things to Ana.

And if Cindy signed? Ana had one brother, Andre, with no legitimate children. She would be a brood mare, trapped until his hunger for children was satiated. The thought of his hands on her made her retch. She hadn't eaten yet today, so only bile came out, burning her throat.

She heard footsteps in the hall, Ana laughed, and then the front door slammed.

Slowly, the tears stopped, and the shaking reduced. A small part of her remembered that Ana and her daughters would be out until dinner time today. Probably shopping for more dresses or new books of prints.

Cindy had planned to go to visit the little chapel down the street. Hardly anyone was there during the week, and it was one of the few places she could go out and not be stared at. It seemed almost pointless now, but she ached for even being able to pretend she had any control in her life.

The little voice came back, “okay then, get up.” She felt like a puppet in her own hands. She scrubbed her face in the kitchen sink, sure it was red and puffy, given how sore and itchy it felt from crying. But the cold water helped soothe her, and she gulped some of it right from the tap to rinse out the taste of bile.

Cindy locked the kitchen door behind her, and walked out into the alley. Everything looked the same as always, which felt like an extra cruelty. She walked up the alley, and turned onto the street and down to the chapel. Cindy looked at the pavement in front of her as she walked, not wanting anyone to notice the state of her face.

The render on the high wall around the outside was chipped all over, and vines had made their way over it. The flimsy metal gate was shut, but not locked. Cindy pushed it open, and it brushed over the weeds that crowded the pavers. The chapel wanted to face east, and so had to be at a skew to all the other buildings around. This gave it some little triangles of garden, although they were mostly hardy shrubs that didn't need much if any tending. The door was also shut, but unlocked, and Cindy slipped inside.

The chapel smelt of warm dust and candle wax. Cindy picked a prayer book off the top of the rack by the door, and shuffled in. The builder or architect who had designed the chapel had clearly loved yellow. The tall narrow windows that repeated down both side walls were all filled with yellow glass. At the end was a round yellow window, and it almost made it feel like you were looking through the bottom of a yellow glass bottle.

Most of the chairs were stacked against the wall, but three formed a circle in the corner, as if having a quiet conversation. There wasn't an altar, since it was only a chapel, but at the end under the big round window were three shallow steps and a narrow stage. Normally Cindy liked to sit on the stage, under the round window, as if it was a heavenly eye watching over her.

Today, Cindy knelt on the bottom step, and placed the prayer book on the stage. She flipped through the pages, looking for words to put to her feelings. She found the confessions, and began there. Maybe she was being punished. Maybe if she could cleanse her soul enough, Ana would forget the whole thing and everything could go back to normal.

The prayer book had a lot of confessions, some of which were very specific. Had Cindy used unfair measures when selling in the market? She'd never sold anything at the market, but maybe it was metaphorical. Cindy prayed them all anyway, in a whisper that was barely more than a breath.

A hand grasped her shoulder.

Cindy whipped around, scrambling to stand up. She was sure it was Ana, she must have come back for something, and noticed Cindy was gone, and she was going to be in so much trouble.

“Love,” said a soft voice, and Cindy's mind cleared enough of the panic to realise that it wasn't Ana. Cindy looked into a warm, familiar face.

“Sister Lilly! Oh! I'm sorry, I was praying, and you startled me,” said Cindy, trying to shove down her panic.

Sister Lilly was probably plump, but her straight gown hid any curves. Only her apple-shaped face was visible, with her hair tucked tightly under a scarf. She didn't seem surprised by Cindy's reaction.

“Love, what is so wrong?” Asked Sister Lilly.

Cindy looked around, “Oh um, nothing is wrong? I don't think? I just came in to pray because I had a little spare time, is that a problem? Does someone need the chapel? I can go, it's no problem.” Cindy realised she'd started babbling and stopped suddenly, biting her lip.

Sister Lilly raised an eyebrow. “You just prayed the confession for returning soldiers,” she said, a hint of amusement in her voice, “have you, um, engaged in any war crimes recently?”

Cindy blinked three times. “Oh, uh, I guess I hadn't really noticed what I was reading…” she mumbled.

“And you look as if you've cried an entire river,” said Sister Lilly gently, “what happened?”

“Nothing,” said Cindy, and when Sister Lilly gave her a disbelieving look, she added, “Well, nothing yet.”

“Come sit with me love, and tell me about this 'nothing yet'” said Sister Lilly, and gestured to the chairs in the corner.

Cindy, used to doing as she was told, dutifully went and sat on one of the chairs. Sister Lilly took the chair next to her. Cindy grabbed her skirt in her fists, and started twisting it with anxiety.

Sure she would cry again if she looked at Sister Lilly while she talked, Cindy talked to the centre of the circle. “I'm going to marry Ana's brother,” she said it very quietly, and so flat, that it felt almost like someone else had said it.

Sister Lilly reached out, and put one chubby hand on top of Cindy's thin ones. “Oh, love,” she said gently, “I'm so sorry.”

If Sister Lilly had tried to make light of it, or tried to say it might not be so bad, maybe Cindy would have made an excuse and left. But with her sympathetic understanding, Cindy's composure broke again. Tears began to run down her face, as she stared at the empty floor in the middle of the circle of chairs.

“Can I pray for you?” Sister Lilly asked. Cindy nodded mutely.

Sister Lilly knew every word of the prayer book by heart. But she also knew Cindy. So she prayed the prayers for little children, prayers Cindy had been taught as a small child learning to string words together. Prayers Cindy had said with her mother, so many years ago. A bit of the pressure in Cindy's heart eased.

When she had finished, Cindy's tears had stopped again. For a little while, she had felt that she was safe. As if Sister Lilly was an older sister who could hold her and tell her it was all a dream.

“Can I come back tomorrow? Will you be here?” Cindy asked softly, “They will be home all day, but out in the evening and I can get away.”

Sister Lilly squeezed Cindy's hand. “Of course, love. I'll be here.”

Cindy thanked Sister Lilly, and left the chapel, going back to her kitchen. The world was ending, but Cindy still had to make supper.
