Two months later, and Kip found himself walking through the manicured gardens of the Palace with the Cavalier, on a lovely warm evening. The Cavalier was always on time, meticulously dressed, and able to actually _explain_ those parts of Court culture that Kip found so confusing. Perhaps, maybe, he had finally found a friend.

“So, the Marchioness Noirell isn't a widow, but she lives at court, without her husband?” asked Kip, trying to keep the characters in the Cavalier's gossip sorted.

“Yes, yes, the Marquis is apparently the rustic sort, and stays on their estate on Alinor—” said the Cavalier, trying to move on with the story.

“—But, they have a daughter? How?” asked Kip.

“Oh! Well, the Marchioness did not spend her entire life at court. I imagine she spent more time on the estate when she was younger, you know,” answered the Cavalier.

“So they're separated, the Marchioness and the Marquis?” pursued Kip.

The Cavalier stopped, and looked thoughtful. “I do not believe so,” he said carefully, “If the Marquis wanted a spare heir he could divorce her and marry someone younger, but he seems content.”

“Ah,” said Kip, finally putting the pieces together, “So it wasn't a love-match. And provided the parentage of the children isn't questionable, they do what they like on the side?”

“Most assuredly. The Marchioness came from minor nobility, and the Marquis wanted someone mostly for their political skills.”

Kip nodded. He found 'marriage' an odd word to use for what seemed more like a business venture, but he could at least see how the relationship could work. Curious, he decided to venture a personal question, “Would you ever need to marry like that?”

The Cavalier smiled, and winked at Kip, “Only if my two older sisters *and* my older brother died. Then I might have to marry my cousin, since the estate is entailed to the female line.”

“Seems unlikely then,” said Kip, with a matching smile, “I'm glad some of you nobles get to marry for normal reasons.”

His smile slightly faltering, the Cavalier turned the question back on Kip. “Nobody in the wide, Wide Seas ever married for reasons other than love, then?”

“Not that I can recall,” said Kip, trying to be precise, “But I imagine there are pushy parents somewhere who managed to talk their child around to the person _they_ wanted.”

“Not your grandmother? She sounds quite intimidating from your descriptions,” asked the Cavalier, pushing a little.

“Oh, well, only to marry _someone_, not who to marry. Unless you count prohibiting cousin marriage, but I can't imagine marrying your _cousin_. To me that sounds as strange as marrying your _sibling_. We count consanguinity to the fourth degree, so with such a large family, I did make sure to check with her about that.”

“There are at least three incredible statements there, my dear Sayo Mdang,” said the Cavalier, “But I shall have to start with the last one. Was the use of the past tense there accidental? You _did_ check with her? Are you, in fact, already married?”

“Not yet,” said Kip, with a small smile, “Ghilly didn't want to come to Astandalas, you see, so she's waiting until I go back.”

“That's very... understanding of her. You must write a lot of letters, I don't think I could do that,” said Conju, slightly awkwardly.

“Oh, I suppose so?” said Kip, blinking, “I've never really thought about it like that. I write a lot of letters generally. I have a lot of friends, family. Everyone is always asking when I'm coming back, and telling me what's happening at home. Sometimes I get the same story from a few different perspectives.” He smiled at the last part, remembering hearing from both his mother and aunt about a desert they both claimed the other had put too much salt in.

“But you do write? And you have a date set to go back?” said the Cavalier, perhaps a little coldly.

Kip blinked, confused. He'd not actually ever made concrete plans. There had been so much work in the _going_, that the return had only been assured, never timed. He stared at the perfect yellow rosebush, identical to all its neighbours. If he had been fired, which had seemed likely on a number of occasions, he would have gone back. He had been so focused on not _failing_, he had never really thought what success might look like.

“Oh,” he said quietly, suddenly seeing all his letters to and from Ghilly in a different light. She had a job at the university, was expecting it to be made permanent soon. If he went home now, or next year, perhaps he could make good on those vague plans for an accountancy business. Become _tanà_, have children, be the person his mother had hoped.

But if he stayed longer in Astandalas… he suddenly realised how unfair he had been to Ghilly, asking her to wait for him for an indefinite period of time. That he had been holding on to her, to the dream of their family together, as a balance to his fears about his work in the Service.

Had anyone tried to tell him? Maybe Vinyë had, in some of her later letters. Where she had been talking about her own family.

“Oh,” said Kip again, quietly. “Shit. If one of my friends had done what I've done, I'd be livid. Ghilly should be livid.” He bit his lip, to try to stop himself from crying. Public displays of emotion were _not done_ in Astandalas, and he didn't want the Cavalier to think badly of him. Or, more badly than he already did, for stringing a woman along for years.

The Cavalier sighed. “Yes, she should be,” he said, with a mild sarcastic tone, “You are quite the bewitcher, Cliopher. Now, come back to my room, drink some of my mother's good brandy, cry all over my sofa, and decide if you are going to quit your job or break your engagement.”

Kip smiled weakly at the Cavalier, “Thank you.” He didn't think he could say any more without bursting into tears right in the gardens.

The Cavalier took him by the arm, and steered him through the gardens. Into a door that Kip wasn't familiar with, and up a few flights of stairs. They arrived at an unremarkable door, which opened at the Cavalier's touch.

“A charcuterie board and the good brandy, please,” said the Cavalier to the woman folding laundry.

Kip looked around the Cavalier's room for the first time. The immediate view out of the large glass windows was of the gardens they had just come from. The other walls were all sliding doors, presumably hiding away the more prosaic parts of the space. One was open, allowing a small magical heater to radiate into the room. Another was partially open, showing a cupboard-bedroom that reminded Kip of his days as a page.

The woman whisked her work from the central table, with a bow to the Cavalier. At the table were two Voonran floor chairs, a Ystharian eating-couch, and an Alinoral sofa. Despite the differences, they managed to be coherent in an artfully asymmetrical way. Matching wood tones, and complementary fabrics made it feel intentional.

Since he had been invited to cry on the sofa, Kip took a seat there. The Cavalier chose to recline on the eating couch. Both of them waited, Kip feeling tense and the Cavalier at least doing a good _impression_ of being relaxed.

After the Cavalier's valet deposited two glasses of brandy (and the bottle) and a selection of savoury bites, she disappeared, possibly behind one of the doors. The Cavalier picked up his glass, and took a long sniff.

“So,” he said, as Kip picked up his own glass, “How are you going to fix this, Cliopher?”

Kip slumped even further, as if he could fold himself between the plush cushions. “I don't know, Cavalier an Vilius, I—”

“Oh, call me Conju,” interrupted the Cavalier, “If we have made it this far we can surely use first names.”

“Thank you, Conju,” said Kip, selecting a green olive, “I have no idea how to fix this. Or what fixed looks like. I can't believe I was so… unfair.”

Conju nibbled on a pickled quail's egg. “How, my dear, did it get this far? Surely someone told you to break it off before you left?”

“Oh no,” said Kip, now taking a sip of the brandy, “I think everyone hoped that I would miss Ghilly and come back quickly. They certainly thought I was mad for leaving at all.” This was obviously a confusing statement to Conju. Kip clarified, “Almost nobody leaves the Vangaveye-ve, and if they do, they stay on Zunidh. I know one wizard who came to Astandalas, but even she only stayed for two years.”

“I cannot say I can relate, but I suppose it is a very long way to travel,” said Conju. “But when you got here? Or at least once you had been here a year or so, surely you… thought about things?”

Kip shook his head. “I kept, I keep, expecting to be fired. I could never make plans for more than a month or so away. Anything more seemed… an unlikely dream. I've just been living week to week.”

“For what, four years now?” said Conju.

“Nearly, yes,” said Kip. The brandy was indeed very good. Knowing he had the next day off, he decided not to worry about how strong it was. “But nothing much has changed. I mean, I'm making fewer stupid mistakes, and your advice has been very helpful. But…,” he sighed deeply, “I still get all the thankless jobs, and none of the credit. Most of my work is ignored. I can't stand that my family was right.”

Conju snorted. “That is what family is for, being irritatingly correct. So why not quit and go home, if it's hopeless?”

“I felt called to serve,” said Kip, barely a whisper.

“Felt, or feel?” asked Conju, sharply.

Kip closed his eyes. He could feel the pull of his island, like a thread running out of his heart. There was also, still, something else. It had felt like the noon sun, when he had first looked at that portrait of the Emperor. Blazing down on him, warming him, filling him with energy and purpose and pulling him up and away.

It was still there. Less bright now, but perhaps more piercing, more direct. Something like the early morning or late evening light, casting long shadows that pointed to the source. He hadn't noticed before, but it did indeed point to the centre of the Palace of Stars. But was it dawning or setting? He didn't know.

“Feel,” said Kip, quietly but firmly.

“Please don't tell me you believe you have been _destined by the gods_ or something,” said Conju, with an exaggerated eye-roll. He was gently throwing almonds into his mouth.

“I don't believe in destiny,” said Kip, with a sniff, “But I do believe in choices.”

When Kip had drunk a further two glasses of brandy, they had been around the same circle of conversation three times. He was too afraid of being fired to commit to staying, and so couldn't break it off with Ghilly. He was too convinced he needed to be here, to give up and go home and marry her. The sofa was free of tears, but Kip had used three of Conju's handkerchiefs, in addition to his own two.

“What would you need,” said Conju, as he took the last cracker and spread it with fresh cheese, “to be sure you _would not_ get fired.”

“A lot of money for bribes,” said Kip, now drunk enough that he was being more brutally honest than even he would have usually felt comfortable with, “Or a noble patron. Most commoners in the Service come from merchant or industrial families, so they have plenty of the first. They stick around for a few years making chummy connections, and then leave to take up 'government liaison' positions in their family companies.”

There was a pause, while Conju ate his cracker. Then he said, seemingly out of nowhere, “Does passing the Imperial Bureaucratic Service Exams expire?”

“Nope,” said Kip, “Every now and then you find some middle-aged noble turn up as a page who has run through the family fortune, and had to resort to the pass mark they got on it at sixteen.”

“They still have to do the year as a page?” said Conju.

Kip was wondering if maybe Conju was just getting tired of talking in circles, and was taking a break with a different topic. “You can pay your way out of it,” he said with a sigh, “Or if you know someone who will just give you a position. Like everything else.”

They sat in companionable silence for a while, sipping their drinks and eating the last of the food.

“What does a noble patron in the Service do?” asked Conju.

“Oh, provides you with advanced warning for re-orgs, helps you get the plum jobs and projects, invites you to the right parties and dinners where the real decisions are made,” said Kip, waving his hand to indicate more of the same.

“So, a mentor?” said Conju, with an arched eyebrow.

“I guess,” said Kip, “If they ever let a commoner into the Upper Secretariat, that's what they might call it. If they chose based on, I don't know, skill, rather than just who their uncle likes to invite to hunting parties.”

“Would you take a noble patron, if someone offered? I have heard you rant about bribes often enough,” said Conju, with a small smile. Kip worried if Conju actually enjoyed how _much_ Cliopher could get, but he felt a certain kinship in their shared desire for things to be done the _right_ way.

“If! If! Ha, no, nobody would see anything worthwhile in me,” said Kip, not bothering to keep the bitterness from his voice. “You saw me at that party, and that was when I just tried to be friends. I don't want six months of walking on glass only to be thrown out when they get bored. There's a give-and-take, as Buru Tovo says, and they only know how to take.  I would rather go home.”

He didn't say Buru Tovo had also left, and then gone home for that same reason. As open as his great uncle was about his love life, it made Kip feel weird.

“Has someone tried that before?” said Conju carefully.

“If you are trying to politely ask if I have been sexually assaulted at work, the answer is yes. And if you're going to follow that up by asking why I didn't report it, I did, once or twice. I was told not to take things so seriously,” said Kip. He looked at the bottom of his empty glass, and thought that more brandy would probably make tonight easier, but tomorrow much worse. “When it's the word of a commoner against a noble...”

It was one thing the Vangaveye-ve could be thankful they had just a few of, nobles. A prince, a duke, not much else. It meant that for the most part there was one set of laws for everyone.

“I wish I was more surprised,” said Conju. He put his empty glass down on the table, and then stretched to stand up, “I need some water.”

Conju found a carafe in the sink behind one of the sliding doors. He filled it up, and brought it back over. Kip was impressed with how steady he looked.

“I would get water glasses, but I can't remember where Liliane keeps them,” he said, as he poured some water into his brandy glass. “And she will be mad at me if I wake her when I'm drunk.”

He looked disdainfully at the glass, and drank down the water.

“Thank you for believing me,” said Kip, slightly wetly. Then he blew his nose.

“Of course I believe you, Cliopher,” said Conju, and patted Kip's hand. He looked out at the dark gardens for a while, something clearly on his mind.

“I passed the Service exam,” said Conju, “More than ten years ago now. My mother said I might as well sit it, since I had spent so much time studying for it. Before…. When I was fifteen my plan was to come to the Palace and be somebody's secretary.”

“But you came as a companion?” said Kip, curious.

“Yes,” said Conju, sounding a long way away, “But she will be married soon; I need to find another position. Or I can wait until my patron's lease on this room runs out at Silverheart, and then go home and annoy my family again.”

They both sat there, looking at a garden neither of them could see. Mostly what they saw was their own reflections, slightly yellow in the warm mage light.

Eventually Kip asked, “Have you been flirting with me?”

“Hmm? Oh, only outrageously since the day we met. I did not know you were engaged, for which I entirely blame you. Maybe I would have flirted anyway, it was nice.”

Kip sighed deeply. “I'm sorry.”

“Don't worry about me, I'm used to it,” said Conju. Though he tried to sound light, Kip thought he wasn't quite hiding an undercurrent of pain. “But for the record, I will not fuck you unless you break it off with your fiancé first. I don't have a problem with a ménage à trois, but I cannot do long distance.”

“Fair,” said Kip, “I don't think I could cope with two partners, one is hard enough.”

Conju gave him an incredulous look. “Your one fiancé, a world away, is too much?”

“No...” said Kip, struggling to explain, “I'm just not very… I don't know, romantic? Being lovers made things complicated. You know.”

“No, I do not,” said Conju, “Maybe it's a women thing. Being lovers has always seemed like the easy part of the relationship to me.”

Kip laughed in return, “Mix us together, and you get one good husband for someone, and one hermit.” Then Kip remembered his Buru Tovo's boyfriend. “Well, some kinds of hermit anyway. The only one I've ever met was quite randy.”

There was another silence, which eventually Conju broke. “You should break it off with Ghilly, Cliopher. Even if you go back, you want different things. Oh, you could probably force it to work. But don't, okay?”

“Okay,” said Kip, his voice cracking slightly. He took a deep breath, and then said quickly, “But you should apply for the Service, Conju. There's good work that needs good people.”

Conju groaned into the bolster.

“You could get a position with the Upper Secretariat, you know the right people. And you like going to all these parties,” argued Kip.

“And you did not?” said Conju, “You just came for the wine?”

“I… I liked… I like spending time with you,” said Kip, nervously.

“Fuck! I want to kiss you *so badly*, please stop doing this to me,” said Conju, trying to cover his head with the bolster. This wasn't very effective, as it was reasonably rigid. Conju couldn't help but notice it wasn't the only thing that was rigid, and curled himself into a ball.

“Sorry,” said Kip, “I'll write to Ghilly tomorrow. Then you can try kissing me? But you'll probably be disappointed.”

“That. Is not. Helping,” said Conju through gritted teeth. “Why don't you try explaining why I should join the Service, and why I won't just be sidelined as a 'boring old stick', hmm?”

Kip shrugged, and outlined each of the various ministries, who ran them, and what kind of corruption was most widespread there. It took a while, but eventually Conju began to look sleepy rather than tense.

“Any of those sound appealing?” said Kip, and surprised himself with a yawn.

“Maybe the Offices of the Lords of State, or Treasury,” said Conju, “I am not amazing with numbers, but managing the other tithes sounds interesting.” He yawned as well.

“Tomorrow,” he said firmly, “I will talk to someone. And you will write to Ghilly.”

“It already is tomorrow,” said Kip, pouring himself some water.

“Ugh,” said Conju, “Well, you never took me up on that offer to stay the night before. Will you take it now? No kissing, no sex, just sleeping.”

“Yes, thank you,” said Kip with relief. He wasn't actually sure he could walk all the way back to his room.

After folding aside his uniform, Kip climbed into Conju's bed in his underwear.

“Don't snore,” said Conju, tucking the blankets around himself.

Kip fell asleep too fast to answer.
