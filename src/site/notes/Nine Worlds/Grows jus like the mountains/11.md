The dance hall was nicer than Kip had expected, possibly because it was the kind of place that charged an entrance fee. Conju had put on a light pretence of surprise and insisted on paying for both of them. It smelt of floor polish and the fresh cut greenery that was hung around, rather than cheep wine and sweat.

For the first dance Conju and Kip watched from the side. Conju supplied whispered comments on what was happening, as the dancers moved up and down, swapping places and kicking. Kip had to admit, it did actually look like it might be fun. He tried to copy some steps at a smaller scale in time with the dancers.

When the chamber orchestra made the dramatic flourish that indicated the end of this dance, everyone clapped and swirled around.

“How do you know what dance is next?” asked Kip, “Is it the same order every night?”

“Oh no,” said Conju, waving his hand dismissively, “That would be _very_ dull, plus dances come in and out of fashion. The list is on the board by the bar.”

He wasn't sure how he could have missed it, there was indeed a large rack beside the bar that had cards slotted into it with different names on them. He saw some that sounded slightly familiar, perhaps he had seen the music for them, or heard other people discussing them. A pointing finger was attached about a quarter of the way down, and as he looked, it slid down to the next name. Some kind of magic, he supposed, kept it correct. Or perhaps the lead musician did something to move it along.

For the second dance, Conju insisted they actually try. It was a dance for four, but they just left the other two spots empty. Kip quickly realised that this dance was made up of the same moves as the previous one, just in different sequences. It repeated after not too long, and Kip was able to make fewer mistakes, and keep up better. The movements might be unfamiliar, but his own skills did make these a lot easier to get a grip on.

“These two have been country dances, the next one is a formal court dance. Do you want to try it, or watch?” asked Conju, turning elegantly around a non-existent dancer.

“Does it repeat too?” asked Kip. He _thought_ all the court dances had repeats, but he had never watched very closely. It did not generally pay to ignore those near you that much.

“Of course!” said Conju, “Your dances don't?”

Now it was Kip's turn to step around an invisible person. “The casual dances and group dances do, but we have some… important… dances that don't.”

“That must be difficult,” said Conju, his eyes wide, “The main difference with court dances is that they are usually done in pairs, either in rows or scattered. And the counts are not just four and eight. Oh, and not all dances done at court are court dances, we do some country dances there too.”

Kip nodded, that fit with what he had seen. “Are the moves the same?” he asked, holding up his palm to Conju's, and turning with him clockwise.

“There is a lot more stepping, and no clapping, but they are not entirely different. Although a couple have some little jumps in them,” said Conju, swapping hands. “You should try. We can always stop if it is too hard, or you get lost.”

So they tried the court dance. Kip did lose his place a few times. (Seven steps to the upper-right? Then eleven back? Why?) But he could see how it fit with the swell and movement of the music. It required much more concentration, and he didn't attempt to make any conversation.

Four dances in, they went and got drinks. It was hot, with so many people moving about. Conju was asked to dance by a nervous looking professional, perhaps a lawyer. He looked briefly to Kip, who waved him away. Conju deserved to spend some time enjoying himself.

Kip kept an eye on them, trying to focus on the dance. So it wasn't until a hand reached across his lower back that he noticed anyone around him.

“Hello, Madon,” she whispered in his ear, “I've never seen you here before.”

He racked his brain trying to place the voice. “Lady Ullimire,” he said, trying to keep his tone formal, “The Cavalier an Vilius invited me.”

“But I don't see him,” she said, with a quiet chuckle, “Did he find something more to his tastes?” She pretended to look around. “Don't worry about him, I know what you need.” 

Her arm slid lower down, and Kip stepped very slightly forward, trying to move out of her grasp. He could still see Conju, but the other man was now progressing towards the front of the room and facing away.

“Now, now,” Lady Ullimire said, “Don't go wandering,” and grasped him more firmly. Kip didn't want to make a scene, he knew how that would end. So he stood there, frozen. “If your Cavalier doesn't come and fetch you for the next dance, you will dance with me.”

“I am only just learning, Lady Ullimire, I would not want to embarrass or injury you with my lack of skill,” said Kip, trying to put her off.

“Oh, that's not a problem,” she whispered, “I will hold you close. It's the waltz.”

It was a tense few minutes of waiting, with the Lady Ullimire's arm clenched tightly around him. Kip very much did not want to spend a whole dance with her hands wandering all over him.

Eventually the dance ended, and Conju bowed to his partner, before looking around. Something in the way Kip met his eyes must have given him some cause for alarm, as he wove through the crowd with surprising speed. As Conju approached, Kip felt the arm around him loosen, and then fall away.

Nearly back, Conju's path was interrupted by a woman telling a bawdy joke to a large group of guffawing friends. 

“Come to the front corner if he changes his mind,” whispered Lady Ullimire, and slipped away.

By the time Conju made it to his side, Kip couldn't even see her in the throng.

“Do you know her?” asked Conju, taking back his drink.

“We have been introduced,” said Kip, coldly, “At the same party where you and I met.”

“Should I find her and warn her off?” asked Conju, looking around.

Kip appreciated the sentiment, but sighed. “No, don't, please,” he said, “Then I'll be forbidden fruit, and she'll be following me around the Palace trying to lock me in a meeting room with her.”

Conju gave Kip a startled look. “But… you're under my protection… I can't just let it go. She must not know.”

“*Please*,” said Kip, “I'm sure she thinks you will blame me, that she'll say I led her on, and you'll listen to her because you're both nobles.”

Conju looked ready to continue arguing.

“I've been here before. Please, let it go, my lord,” said Kip, keeping his voice down, his eyes down.

The honorific did what reasoning had not. “Cliopher?” Conju asked gently, “Sorry, did you want to leave?”

Kip didn't answer the question directly. “You wanted me to learn to dance.”

“I wanted you to *enjoy* learning to dance. I can buy an enchanted music box with some tunes, and we can practice with that. Or I can book a room with a harpsichord and find someone to play it. It doesn't have to be like this.”

“It's fine, nothing happened, I'm used to it,” said Kip.

“You know enough rhetoric to know it can't both 'nothing' and 'fine',” said Conju.

Kip could feel Conju was teasing, trying to lighten the mood, but he didn't care. “Yes it can,” he said firmly, “And I don't fix it by hiding away, or you slapping her.”

“All right,” said Conju, irritated “What do you want to do then?”

“I want nobles to stop being able to treat commoners like property. I want *everyone* to have rights, and be protected by the law and the state. Nobody should need to be rich to file an assault case, nobody should be so powerful they can wave one away. And *nobody* should be allowed to fight an honour duel.” 

Kip took a deep breath. That was not the kind of thing you said aloud in the Palace. He could name three previous bosses who would have called Internal Security after the first sentence. Conju could march him there right now.

“I'll go back, you can stay if you want,” said Kip, still not looking at Conju.

“I'll come with you,” said Conju, putting down his empty glass on the shelf behind him.

“I don't need your protection on the _walk_,” said Kip, still feeling amped up.

“Hmm, no, I was thinking rather more of protecting people from you,” said Conju, “You look like you're itching for a fight.”

“I'm not that stupid, I know how to control my temper enough not to pick random fights with strangers in the city, *Conju*,” said Kip, setting his own glass down rather more firmly than was needed. “I haven't started a fight since my second year of high school.”

Conju gave him a dramatic arched eyebrow and lent in, “*Really*,” he said, conspiratorially, “And what would you call correcting the Undersecretary for Higher Eduction in the budget meeting with her superior?”

“Fact-checking,” said Kip, “Nobody punched anyone. No-one even fell off their chair.”

“Mmm,” said Conju, sounding entirely unconvinced, as they walked back outside together. “But the yelling was heard two corridors over. Well, how about you tell me your plan for banning honour duels.”

Kip sighed, “It would be easy, really. If the Emperor orders the Ouranatha to stop blessing them, they come under the regular laws against assault, battery, murder, and so on.” He kept his eyes on his feet, trying to keep out of the drifts of refuse and piles of dung.

“But as you say, who will prosecute them? Surely not the looser, as they would also be at fault,” said Conju, pointedly.

“The Ouranatha could get them for blasphemy, for taking the form of an honour duel without the blessing. But I'd rather not give them more power,” said Kip, stepping quickly back when someone ran out in front of him.

“If it's just a criminal law banning them entirely…” said Conju, as they wove around a group leaving an early theatre performance.

“Then it would be up to the Crown Prosecutors,” said Kip, “Who are easily bribed. We'd need to think about it from the other direction. Who would be willing to prosecute? How can we make it their problem?”

“The Army and Navy have successfully banned their officers from duelling,” said Conju, as they waited for a cart to cross in front of them.

“Yes, but I don't think we want more people joining the military,” said Kip, “And anyway, it's not like we could conscript every noble in.”

They walked in silence for a while, both thinking about the problem.

“What if,” said Kip, as they approached the gate, “The Emperor just made duelling against the _Palace_ rules. Get caught, you're kicked out of the Palace and your family looses their rooms. The Palace is always short on space, it would also help with the congestion. And the Imperial Guard could enforce it, like the dress codes. Or the rules against bringing weapons into the Presence.”

“Banishing _entire families_ for one duel? That's…” said Conju, slightly stunned.

“Too much?” asked Kip, with a sigh.

“It's genius. The family heads will crack down on all the young hellions,” said Conju, and Kip couldn't believe he'd actually used 'young hellion' to describe people that were surely the same age as him. “Once the Emperor has done it once, forced some family from their ten-bedroom suite to a two-room box at the end of a wing, or back to their country estates, everyone will stop and think. Duelling will become *immediately* unfashionable.”

They passed through the gate, and the front courtyard, smiling at each-other.

“But,” said Kip, not wanting to drag down the atmosphere with practicalities, “How do we even ask the Emperor? Would he be interested? What if he says no?”

“You write up the proposal, Cliopher,” said Conju, “Let me think about how to stage it.”

“Are you planning on sneaking it into the morning reports?” asked Kip, as they climbed the front stairs.

Conju thought about the question, as they approached the doors. “We could,” he said, honestly, “But ideally we get the Emperor to _ask_ us to find a solution.”

“Says the man who hasn't managed to say more than 'thank you' to the Emperor,” said Kip, grinning.

“Oh, because you did so well,” said Conju, but Kip could tell he didn't really mean it.

“You could always put yourself on the audience schedule,” said Kip, poking him right back. They wound their way up the central stairs.

“Maybe I _will_,” said Conju, teasing right back, “And I will make _you_ do the presentation.”

“Which I will do, in the form of a dance,” said Kip.

“Naturally,” said Conju, deadpan.

By the time they reached their apartment, they were both smiling, and declaring new and more ridiculous ways they would present their idea.
