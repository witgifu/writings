Cliopher woke with the dawn bells, confused about where he was. Looking around, he saw his son (his son!) curled up beside him, and let out a little sigh. Nothing about his life was going to be the same now, but little Dimiter seemed sent by the gods to tug at his heart.

A more rational part of his mind made the comment that he might think differently the first time Dimiter had a screaming tantrum in a public area of the Palace. Or was sick all over his bed. Or if Cliopher had to travel for work. He hadn't even thought about how he would manage that yet. Would he take his son with him? He might have to leave him in the care of a paid nanny then.

That reminded him that Conju had threatened to hire staff for Cliopher, and he wondered when they might arrive. Or what kind of person they would be. He knew Conju had multiple people himself, and of course managed the Tower staff, but what most of those people actually _did_ was somewhat of a mystery.

It would be easier to think with coffee in him. There was, again, much to do today. An interview with the childcare staff, a meeting with a lawyer Rhodin had recommended, and he really did need to write to his family and tell them what had happened. He sighed again.

Dimiter scrunched his nose, and blinked his eyes open. “The bells are _loud_,” he mumbled, wriggling in the blanket.

“They have to wake up the heavy-sleeping nobles to do their dawn rituals,” said Cliopher, “You do get used to them.”

With a frown, Dimiter mumbled, “Don't want to.”

Cliopher nodded, “What about breakfast?”

Dimiter sighed, “Okay. Will there be grapes again?”

“I'm not sure,” said Cliopher honestly, “We'll have to get dressed and find out.”

They did, and wandered into the kitchen together. Someone had delivered a plate of pastries, and a bowl of fruit, which did indeed include grapes. Cliopher made coffee, and they had a peaceful breakfast together, in the comfortable silence of good food.

Not long after they finished, a bell rang. It wasn't one of the Palace bells, and after a moment of confusion, Cliopher realised it must be the doorbell. His old rooms hadn't had one, given you could hear a knock at the front door from every corner. “I wonder who that is?” he said to Dimiter, who looked confused.

At the front door they found an elderly woman, holding a carpet bag with one hand, and a book with the other. Dimiter, who had been bounding ahead of Cliopher a moment before, went very still. Then he shuffled back to stand next to his dad, grabbing a fistful of Cliopher's robe.

Cliopher was also surprised. She wasn't dressed in the uniform of the Palace staff, and he didn't recognise her. He put an arm around Dimiter's shoulders, and said, “Good morning, Saya...?”

With a smile for both of them, she said, “Lazunar Tito. Lord Conju said you were looking for some help?”

“Yes...” said Cliopher, uncertainly. She didn't seem much like the staff Conju tended to hire, she didn't look like she would serve silently at a formal dinner. In fact, she reminded him most of one of his Varga aunties. She was short, with a medium-brown skin not so different from Cliopher's, but the round face of a local of Eastern Dair. Her hair was fully grey, and in a practical, short style that would have passed for a Service cut.

“Come in,” said Cliopher, remembering his manners. Letting Dimiter continue to partially hide behind him, he led her to the parlour.

“I was a nanny for one of the Dairian minor noble families,” she said as they walked, “Then I had kids of my own. When the eldest got a job here in Solaara, I brought the rest. They're all grown up now. I thought maybe I'd get a part-time job again, rather than bothering my children.”

“As a nanny?” asked Cliopher, gesturing her to a seat. He thought he'd explained to Conju that he didn't want Dimiter to be raised by hired help. Though he wasn't sure what there was between a full-time live-in nanny and a babysitter.

Saya Tito shrugged, and took the indicated chair. “I don't think I'm fast enough to keep four or five spoilt noble children in line anymore,” she said, “Plus with age I'm less inclined to keep my mouth shut in front of their parents. I was thinking more of an… auntie. Lord Conju suggested you were looking for a different sort of arrangement.”

Cliopher looked at Dimiter and said, “Do you want to sit with me? Or would you rather go and see what else is in the toy box?”

Dimiter nodded slowly, but didn't let go of Cliopher's robes.

Deciding that seemed to mean the first option, Cliopher sat on the sofa opposite Saya Tito, lifting Dimiter up to sit beside him.

“My job often requires odd hours,” said Cliopher, “Responding to emergencies, sometimes travel, but also unfortunately parties. I will of course decline what I can, but...”

“Many parents face similar difficulties,” said Saya Tito, in a conciliatory tone.

Cliopher looked at his son, who still seemed nervous. “I have a large family, *we* have a large family. You have a grandma, and an aunt, and many great-aunts and great-uncles. But they all live in the Vangaveye-ve.”

Dimiter looked wide-eyed with surprise, but didn't offer any questions.

“If I get called suddenly, I will let you know,” said Cliopher, “But I don't want to leave you alone, or drag you to midnight meetings.”

He turned back to Saya Tito. “Do you live near the Palace?”

“Not really,” she said, “I was, ah, hoping this might be a live-in arrangement.”

Cliopher's eye flicked briefly to the carpet bag. He wondered if perhaps part of looking for a job was needing to not live with her children anymore. Despite having lived in multi-generational households, he knew it didn't suit everyone.

“Of course!” he said, with a smile, “I didn't want to presume.”

She turned to Dimiter, “Would you mind if I came to live with you?”

Dimiter shrugged, but didn't answer. Cliopher wondered that his previously exuberant son was suddenly quiet. Was he simply overwhelmed by meeting so many new people? Was there something in Saya Tito that frightened him?

“Have you met Saya Tito before?” he asked gently.

Dimiter shrugged again. Saya Tito looked confused, and thoughtful.

“Did you used to live with your grandmother? And you carried a toy boat with you everywhere?” she asked, looking embarrassed.

Dimiter looked at Cliopher, who said, “Yes?”

“Ah,” said Saya Tito, with a sigh. “Then until recently, I lived in the large apartment at the top of the building where they lived. My family often have loud disagreements.” 

She looked from Cliopher to Dimiter, “You probably heard me yelling at my children, and them yelling back. When you know someone very well, that sometimes means you're just as good at making them angry as happy.”

Looking back to Cliopher, she moved to get up. “I apologise, Sayo Mdang, for taking up your time.”

It was Dimiter who interrupted. Not with a word, but with a sort of whine. They both looked at him, and he tucked his head under his father's arm.

“You don't want Saya Tito to leave?” asked Cliopher. Dimiter shook his head.

“Are you scared?” asked Cliopher gently. Dimiter shrugged.

Patiently, Cliopher continued asking yes or no questions. Dimiter was scared, but not of Saya Tito, or her family (at this, Cliopher saw her relax noticeably). He wasn't scared of her coming to live with them. Cliopher racked his mind, trying to work out what was most likely.

“Are you scared of coming to look for me, and I'm gone?” he asked. Dimiter shrugged.

Then he realised how stupid he was being. It had been not long since Dimiter's whole world had been upended. He'd probably been told he was going to the children's home while Nana was at the hospital, just until she got better, and then she would be back. But then she hadn't gotten better. Of course he was afraid of that happening again.

How could he help Dimiter? It wasn't like he could promise not to die unexpectedly. He started with a hug. “How about I ask you if you want to come with me,” he offered, “Maybe if I get called in early one morning, or in the evening? Then you can see how I'm safe at a desk, being boring.”

Dimiter looked interested. 

“I'll always let you know when I think I'll be back, and if you're worried, … Auntie Lazunar...?” he turned to Saya Tito for the last part, unsure if she would be okay with the informality. The people of Solaara often took on the Palace hierarchy and culture.

Saya Tito smiled, “Auntie Lazu can take you to see your dad. And will make sure you get dinner, and bath time, and stories.”

Dimiter looked between them, and nodded resolutely. Then he slid out of Cliopher's arms, off the couch, and dashed over to the toy box. It would take time, Cliopher was sure, for his son to trust him. But he was so relieved that they had managed this big step.

“As to living space,” said Cliopher, keeping one eye on Dimiter as he bounced the shark along the floor, “There’s an empty bedroom in the main hall, or you could have the staff parlour and both bedrooms, which have their own bathroom.”

There was a gleam in Saya Tito’s eye. “Would you mind if I used the other staff bedroom as a craft room?” she asked, politely.

“Not at all, feel free to use the rooms however you like, we will consider them your space,” said Cliopher, “What kind of crafts do you do, if you don’t mind me asking?” He remembered his aunt’s toy workshop.

“Oh, little things for my own amusement. I like to mix clockwork with a bit of magic,” she said, “Nothing very powerful, you know, I only have the tiniest gift. Music boxes mostly.”

Cliopher was clueless about magic, and knew very little about clockwork, despite a strong appreciation for timekeeping. “That sounds fascinating,” he said, hoping that didn’t sound too asinine, “Hopefully you’ll have plenty of time for it. I *am* planning on putting Dimiter into the Palace childcare. We have the interview there in about half a bell.”

As if to highlight his sense of time, the half-bell rang. As the echo died, Cliopher remembered there was a topic he still hadn’t raised. “To be honest with you, Saya Tito,” said Cliopher.

“Auntie Lazu is fine,” she said, and then hastily amended, “Unless you would prefer to remain more formal, of course, sir.”

“Auntie Lazu, then,” said Cliopher, with a smile, “I have no idea how much or what structure to use to pay you. Would you prefer to be on a small ‘on-call’ stipend, with an hourly rate for when you are called on? A salary for an expected number of hours with overtime for more?”

With a smile, Auntie Lazu said, “When I worked full-time as a nanny, I got a fixed salary no matter how many hours I worked. Which was basically every waking hour of the day, and often longer.”

Cliopher frowned. “That wouldn’t be legal now. His Radiancy did manage to get a maximum workday of twelve hours for all domestic staff. Over the next few years he will whittle that down further. So many time-and-motion studies to prove it was more efficient.”

“Lord Conju mentioned that you are entitled to more staff than you currently employ?” said Auntie Lazu. 

Cliopher sighed. He had tried to get the budget assigned for a personal assistant and secretary reassigned to more general department staff. The real problem there was the lack of willing and qualified candidates. With no ability to fill the roles if he created them, Cliopher had dropped the issue. It still felt wrong to use the budget for what it hadn't been allocated for. He could pay her out of his own salary, if he was frugal otherwise.

With rather more embarrassment this time, Auntie Lazu said, “Lord Conju also said to say that if you try to pay me out of your own pocket he will, um, 'inform his Radiancy that Cliopher is in need of more household staff allocation'.”

Cliopher winced. “Given that, I assume Lord Conju provided you with a standard contract?” he said, trying not to think about what other staff his Radiancy might consider 'required'. Cliopher was a commoner, he neither needed nor wanted to be waited on.

“He did, yes,” said Auntie Lazu, with a smile.

“Do you want to help show Auntie Lazu her rooms?” said Cliopher to Dimiter, who was trying to balance a ball on top of the elephant.

Dimiter nodded, and danced over to the door. There he turned back to look at them both, rolling his eyes.

“We're coming,” said Auntie Lazu, with a smile, “Some of us just aren't as fast as you.”
