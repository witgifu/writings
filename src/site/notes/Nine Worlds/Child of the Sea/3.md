The Parkside Children's Home was obviously a grand aristocratic house that had been repurposed. How there had been enough time in this part of Solaara for a noble to build a house, decide it was no longer fashionable, and sell it, Cliopher tried to ignore. If everything went smoothly, he'd return to the Palace on the same day he left it.

Squaring his shoulders, Cliopher walked up the slightly weedy path to the grand front door. When he pulled for the bell, there was a corresponding sound that echoed somewhere inside. That was followed by the sound of running footsteps, and then a teenager opened the door.

“Are you a donor sir? Only the housemother's a bit busy with the babies. I can get you coffee in the parlour?” asked the boy. He looked clean and well-dressed, if cheaply.

Cliopher smiled, “I'm here to pick up my son, Dimiter.”

The teen stopped, and looked a little sad. Cliopher wondered how long he had been there, how often he had hoped an unknown family member might turn up and claim *him*. How often he had watched his friends leave and smiled and waved and wished them well. They needed a better solution than the care homes. Oh, it was good not to have child beggars, but everyone here deserved a family, even just a temporary one.

Brightening, the boy said, “Well, I'm afraid you'll still have to wait. Coffee? Sugar? We might even have a little milk left.”

“Just plain, thank you,” said Cliopher, and then, although he had already guessed the answer, “Do you work here?”

“Oh! No,” said the boy, “I'm Marc. Maybe I'll get a job here when I age out though, I do love the little ones.” Marc ran off, presumably to the kitchen.

Cliopher took a seat in the parlour, which he was pleased to see showed signs of recent use by children. There was a scattering of blocks, and a few well-loved picture books. Deciding he had at least a couple of minutes, he pulled out a notebook and began adding to the lists of things he needed to do.

Marc returned with the coffee, and a careworn woman. Cliopher quickly slipped his documents out of his writing case.

“Cliopher Mdang,” he said, proffering the papers.

“Housemother Ani Bird,” she said, flipping through the papers. “You don't need the temporary order if you're on the birth record, but I suppose it doesn't hurt,” (Cliopher mentally underlined 'find a lawyer' on his list) “Now, you're not expecting to just pick Dimiter up and go to work, are you?”

“I've taken the day off,” said Cliopher, since his leave form hadn't been formally accepted yet. He looked down at his Service uniform, “It's just, this was rather unexpected, and I didn't have anything else fit to wear.”

She looked up at him from the papers, “That's fine, dear. Some people think I should have their child packed and ready to go.”

Saya Bird sat on the chair next to Cliopher, “Do you have any other children?”

“No ma'am,” said Cliopher, sitting down as well. Something about her had reminded him of one of his school teachers.

“Just Ani is fine, or Saya Bird if you prefer. Is there anyone else in your household?”

“Not yet?” said Cliopher, taking a sip of coffee. “My friend is helping me hire, well, has probably already hired, one person for live-in help.”

“And you would trust your friend's advice on this? Do they have children?”

Cliopher smiled at the idea of Conju with a brood of children. “No, Saya Bird, but he's very experienced at hiring for demanding roles. Our friend in the guard will do the security clearance.”

Saya Bird didn't seem entirely mollified by this, but she moved on anyway. “Have you thought about what you will need to take care of him? Do you have somewhere safe for him to sleep, someone to watch him while you're at work?”

“Yes, to both, Saya Bird,” said Cliopher, pulling out his lists, “But I would very much appreciate feedback from an expert such as yourself. Also, anything you know about Dimiter. I'm afraid,” Cliopher sighed, and continued, “I do not know my son at all. I should have asked after his mother, but we lost touch probably a month after he was conceived. I had no idea.”

She smiled back. “When did you find out?”

“Yesterday,” said Cliopher, “I would have come to see him immediately, but I needed to get myself organised, move somewhere larger, find out how to apply to the childcare.”

“Well, let's go and meet Dimiter then,” said Saya Bird, standing up, “What's on your list?”

Cliopher put down his empty mug, and explained what he had already done, what he had planned, and what he didn't know if he needed to do. Saya Bird made encouraging noises as they walked down the hall and outside into the garden. She led him over to a group of children yelling and chasing a ball. On the edge, looking uncertain, was a little boy who looked exactly like Cliopher's cousin Haro in miniature.

“Oh,” said Cliopher, completely lost from his train of thought. “Oh.”

Dimiter noticed the two adults approaching. Cliopher saw him recognise Ani, and shy away from Cliopher, the stranger.

Cliopher squatted down, as if he had been in the garden at his mother's house. The Service uniform wasn't quite made for it, but he barely noticed how it was binding around his knees. Shocked at how familiar Dimiter looked, he thought he just needed a moment to breathe. Lifting a hand up to his face, he realised he was crying.

If Saya Bird noticed, she didn't act like this was unusual. “Dimiter,” she called gently, “I've got someone who would like to meet you.”

“Am I going home?” asked Dimiter, and Cliopher's heart nearly broke. The tears began to fall so thick he could hardly see.

“Not with your Nana, no,” said Saya Bird gently. “Your father has come for you.”

“Nana said I don't have a dad,” said little Dimiter, looking suspiciously from Saya Bird to Cliopher.

“The hospital managed to find him,” said Saya Bird. “Would you like to say hello with a wave or an arm clasp?”

Dimiter thought for a moment. “If he's my dad, can I hug him?”

“If you want to,” said Cliopher, “You don't have to.”

“Are you sad about Nana too?” asked Dimiter.

“Yes,” said Cliopher, “I wish I could have known her, and met you both a long time ago.”

Dimiter nodded, and walked over. Then he clasped Cliopher's forearms, and Cliopher held his back. Still crying and sniffling a little, Cliopher nevertheless smiled. “Hello Dimiter,” he said, “My name is Cliopher Mdang. You can call me Kip, or Papa, or Dad, I don't mind.”

“Hello Dad,” said Dimiter, “I'm Dimiter, I'm four, and I like boats. Do you like boats?”

“I do!” said Cliopher, “What do you like about boats?”

Dimiter's eyes lit up. “Everything! Will you take me to see the boats on the river? I've still not seen the inside of a clinker-built hull. I've seen the outside, loads of times.”

Cliopher was now grinning from ear to ear. “I would love to take you down to the wharves,” he said, “But we can't go right now. Hopefully soon.”

“Tomorrow?” said Dimiter, still holding on to Cliopher's arms. “Or maybe the day after?”

“I hope so,” said Cliopher, unwilling to make a promise he couldn't keep, “First, I have to do everything Saya Bird asks of me.”

Dimiter looked up at the woman, who was also smiling. The other children had followed the ball around a clump of bushes.

“Let's get your things packed, Dimiter,” said Saya Bird, “Then you need to say goodbye to everyone.”

“Am I going away like Nana?” asked Dimiter, his voice small.

“No, Dimiter,” said Cliopher gently, “You're going to come and live with me. Would we be able to visit, Saya Bird? Just so he can say hello to his friends?”

“I don't,” said Dimiter, paused, and dropped his hands from Cliopher, and whispered “The other kids don't like boats. But you like boats, so maybe we can go and see *them*? Not just if I'm good but if you're good too.”

The logic was unassailable. “Why not?” said Cliopher, “Let's go and pack then.”

They went back into the house together, and Dimiter explained all about the time Nana had taken him on the ferry. The tale was a little garbled, but the enthusiasm was unwavering. Cliopher couldn't help imagining taking Dimiter out on his vaha some day.

Saya Bird handed them off to Marc again, and they went upstairs to gather to what must have been once a grand lady's bedroom. Four boys now shared the bed, and as such the blankets were all in a pile in the middle. It rather reminded Cliopher of sleepovers with his cousins.

Dimiter helped Marc gather his few things into a carpet bag. Mostly clothes, but also a hairbrush, and a face cloth. Dimiter had one precious toy. It was, of course, a boat. Made by someone out of odd bits of wood and cardboard and string. He held it carefully and declined putting it in the bag. 

Marc picked up a small sketch, which had been propped up on a shelf. It showed an old woman smiling. “Should Nana go in the bag?” asked Marc gently.

“Will she be squashed?” asked Dimiter, nervously, “I don't want to drop her.”

“I could put the picture in my writing kit?” offered Cliopher, “It would be safe there, I've never had a paper even get bent.”

Dimiter nodded, and watched as Cliopher put the paper carefully away.

“There's a retired portraitist who visits the hospital sometimes,” said Marc, “If he hears one of our kids has family there he sends the pictures here.”

“That is…” said Cliopher, unable to express how grateful he was that Dimiter had a picture of his Nana.

Marc nodded, he understood.

Cliopher carried the bag and his writing kit, and Dimiter carried the toy boat, down the stairs to the entrance hall.

“Who do you want to say goodbye to?” asked Marc.

“Cook!” replied Dimiter promptly, “And Mama Ani and the others.”

They did so, weaving their way through a bustle of children and toddlers. Cook gave Dimiter a biscuit, which he only accepted after moving the boat so he could hold it tightly with one arm. Finally, they said goodbye to Marc, at the front door.

“Where do you live?” asked Dimiter, as they walked down the path.

“At the Palace,” said Cliopher, carefully slowing his steps to match his son's (his son!) shorter legs. “Have you been there before?”

“No,” said Dimiter, “It's over there.” He turned, and pointed at it, the towers glinting. “Do you live with the Emperor?”

“No, he lives in the tall tower in the middle, right at the top,” said Cliopher, “My apartment is lower down.”

“Does the Emperor live with his dad? Or his kids?” asked Dimiter, “And does he have a boat?”

“The Emperor lives alone,” said Cliopher, “Although his sister and great aunt visit. He has a ship, the Dawn of Zunidh, and a barge for travel on the river.”

“Wow!” said Dimiter, clearly impressed that one person could have both. “Does he go sailing often?”

“No,” said Cliopher, who hadn't known his Radiancy to use the ship at all, and had only travelled with him once on the barge when the fens were bounded. “His Radiancy… works long hours.”

This clearly caused some complex thoughts for Dimiter, who was frowning and looking at the path. Eventually he said, “People call the Emperor lots of names.”

“That's true. One of them is even the Lord of Five Thousand Lands and Ten Thousand Titles, although I've never counted them to check.”

“What's his real name?” asked Dimiter, “Doesn't he like it?”

“Artorin Damara,” said Cliopher, the name feeling strange on his tongue. How could he explain the many titles of his lord to someone so young? “The names are to show respect. When I talk to him I call him 'my lord'.”

Dimiter didn't look convinced. But they had reached a main road now, and Cliopher was worried about loosing him in the traffic.

“Could you hold onto the bag as well please?” he asked, “And I'll try to flag down a rickshaw.” With Dimiter, the bag, and the toy boat, it seemed safer than walking. After not too long, Cliopher spotted a wiry young man peddling along, and hailed him. Dimiter's eyes were wide with excitement. 

It was true that when the Palace had been in Astandalas, Cliopher would have never dared the extravagance. Even in Solaara he preferred to walk. But it seemed more practical, just at this moment. And he did get paid much better now.

Feeling that perhaps the front gates might be one excitement too many today, he asked the driver to take them to one of the side gates that would also be closer to his (their!) new apartment. Dimiter got in slowly, with much care for his boat. Once in, he wriggled into the back of the seat and looked out with awe. Cliopher climbed in next to him.

As they went along, Cliopher pointed out the various buildings they passed. Temples, banks, public baths, gallery, library, city council buildings. Eventually, they arrived at the garden gate.

Cliopher paid the driver, and Dimiter looked over at the guards. “Are those sharp?” he asked, pointing to the spears.

“Yes, they are,” said Cliopher, who had on occasion seen the guards maintaining them. “So we have to be careful and not surprise the guards.”

“Good day, sir,” said the left guard, “Is this boy with you?”

“This is my son, Dimiter Yana, he's come to live with me.”

Both guards saluted. “Good day, Sayino Yana. What a lucky boy to have Sayo Mdang for a father."

Dimiter looked surprised and a little intimidated. He hugged his boat close.

“Shall we go inside?” asked Cliopher, “It's rather hot, isn't it?”

Keeping his eyes on the guards, Dimiter walked through the gate. He stayed close to Cliopher, who wished he had a free hand to offer his son. By juggling the writing kit and bag, he managed a slightly tenuous hold on both, and offered his then empty right hand to his son (his son!). Dimiter took a deep breath, and then sighed, and put his hand in Cliopher's.
