Together Cliopher and Dimiter walked in silence through the greenery and up to a side door of the Collian Wing. Cliopher reflected that he didn't much walk in the gardens in the day, and they were indeed rather too hot and humid. Especially in all the layers of his uniform.

The guard by the door actually smiled at them, which Cliopher thought was probably against protocol. But then, Dimiter was objectively very cute, holding tight to his little boat.

“If you turn left, and go up three doors, there's a lift,” he said helpfully.

Cliopher hadn't been looking forward to the climb up the stairs. Not for himself, he was used to traipsing all around the Palace. But he had noticed Dimiter was walking slower and slower. “Thank you,” he said, and made for that door. It cut the walk to their apartment significantly shorter.

In the daylight, the entrance room was much more appealing. Still holding Dimiter's hand, and pushing the door handles with his elbow, Cliopher took them through to the hallway with the bedrooms. That was now lit through a beautiful abstract blue stained-glass window.

“Do you want to choose a bedroom?” said Cliopher, both of the furnished rooms had open doors.

Dimiter peeked into the white one first, and then the other. “This one,” he said, “Do I share?”

“No,” said Cliopher, putting Dimiter's bag down on the chair, “It's just for you. Do you want to try the bed out while I unpack?”

Dimiter nodded. After carefully depositing his boat on top of the dresser, he pulled off his sandals. Then he climbed onto the bed. Lying down he looked up at the ceiling.

Moving quietly, Cliopher put Dimiter's few things into the dresser. He wasn't sure how quickly a boy of this age would grow out of clothes. Should he buy more, or would that be foolish? Perhaps he could ask some of the other staff in the office. It would take too long for a letter to get to the Vangaveye-ve. The postal system had improved, but more work was still needed. Cliopher hoped the Lights that his lord was working on would change things again.

Cliopher also carefully removed the picture of Nana from his writing kit. He would try to get it framed to protect it, but for now he slipped it into the edge of the mirror.

Dimiter's boat was a little pleasure yacht made by someone who had seen them but clearly had no idea how they were made. Carefully not touching it, Cliopher looked it over. It reminded him painfully, but also sweetly, of the little toy vaha his own father had made him. He thought briefly of making Dimiter a little vaha, but he didn't want his son to think he was trying I replace this boat. Or that somehow this one, which had clearly been made with such love, shouldn't stay in the boy's life.

Perhaps Dimiter would like a stuffed whale. Or another animal. Cliopher only knew a very little about sewing, and had not paid much attention to his aunt's craft. Also, he had so little *time*. It felt wrong, but perhaps he could buy one. After all, his aunt sold her works, so if you weren't lucky enough to be her nephew, you would have to do the same. Maybe, someday, he could write to his mother and get her to find his own whale, and send her to him.

Turning back to Dimiter, he could see his son was crying. Cliopher rushed over to the bed, but then hesitated. "Would you like a hug?" he asked, hands caught almost reaching out.

Dimiter made a sniffly noise of ascent. Cliopher lay awkwardly on the bed, keeping his sandals off it, and reached around Dimiter. His son (his son!) wormed closer into his arms, and Cliopher held him tight.

“Big day?” asked Cliopher, “Lots of changes?”

Dimiter murmured an affirmative. 

“It's okay to cry,” said Cliopher, “I've got you.” He began to hum, just as his own Mama had when he was small and cried. After a little while he realised his mother wouldn't have hummed Aurora, since it hadn't even been published then. He didn't stop though.

They lay like that, Cliopher's left arm going numb, for some time. He was sure he ignored some half- and quarter-hour bells. When the first bell of the evening rang, there was a knock on the door. Cliopher looked up to see a woman he vaguely recognised in the doorway.

“Lord Conju sent me to remind you to have dinner, Sayo Mdang,” she said with a smile.

Cliopher chuckled, and Dimiter gave him an inquiring look. “Conju is my friend,” he clarified, “And he often despairs that when I am busy I forget to eat.”

“That's because you *do* forget, Sayo Mdang. If Saya Kalikiri wasn't also nagging you, I wouldn't be surprised if you tried to survive only on coffee,” said the woman.

“Well,” said Cliopher defensively, “I'm often very busy and forget the time.”

“I'm not sure how, Sayo Mdang, since the bells can be heard in the entire Palace,” she said, shaking her head, “Now, I'm under instruction to follow you and nag until I see you eating.”

Now it was Dimiter's turn to laugh. “You better eat soon!” he said.

“All right, all right,” grumbled Cliopher. Then winked at Dimiter. “Shall we see if there's anything in the kitchen? If it's empty we can go to the cafeteria, but it can be a bit busy right now.”

Dimiter and Cliopher climbed off the bed, and Dimiter put his sandals back on. Then they walked down the hall and around to the kitchen.

To Cliopher's relief, there was food. He wondered if the Palace staff or Conju had arranged it. Bread, cheese, pickles, fruit. Nothing that he needed to cook. There was also his old coffee brewer, and some coffee.

He had no idea how much Dimiter would eat, so he just put everything on the kitchen table. “You can help yourself,” he said to his some, “But the cheese knife is sharp, so please ask me to cut it for you.”

Cliopher wasn't at all surprised to see Dimiter start with a handful of grapes. He began by tearing off some bread.

“Very good, Sayo Mdang,” said Conju's spy, “I can safely report back. Have a good evening, both of you.”

After dinner, they explored the rooms together. Cliopher found that, as promised, Conju had put his battered old sofa in his bedroom. Dimiter found that the parlour had a toy box. He flapped his hands in excitement as he ran over to see if there was anything inside.

Cliopher hurried after, worried that he would have to explain an empty box to his son. To both their surprise, the box was not at all empty. On top was a folded note, which Dimiter handed to Cliopher.

“What does it say? Are these yours?” he asked, carefully putting his hands behind his back, and peering into the box.

Flipping open the note, Cliopher saw Conju's elegant hand and immediately feared that his friend had gone overboard buying toys.

> Cliopher,
> I only acquired the box. The contents are from your other friends and colleagues. I believe many of them brought in toys their own children no longer played with, knowing that your change in circumstances was very sudden. There is no list of who gave what, as it seems to have been anonymous to stop you refusing. There are also books on the shelf.
> Conju

With a smile, Cliopher said, “They are all for you.” A glance at the wall showed that in addition to his own books, one of the lower shelves had a number of children's books.

“For _me_?” said Dimiter, in disbelief, “But… there's so many...”

“My friends heard you had a hard time,” said Cliopher, trying to frame it in a way his son might understand, “They wanted to do something to help you feel better.”

Dimiter pulled out a knitted shark with one hand, and a wooden elephant with the other. He looked at both of them with amazement. “You must have a lot of friends,” he said, “Do you think the shark and the elephant are friends?”

“Hm,” said Cliopher, “I only know one story with a shark and an elephant in it.” Squatting down next to the toy box with Dimiter, he looked in and pulled out a rag doll. “Do you know about the Shark Queen?” he asked, turning the doll to Dimiter as if it was asking the question.

“No!” said Dimiter, “Tell me! Tell me! Please!”

Cliopher smiled, and did so.

“Dad?” came a small, high voice, breaking into Cliopher's sleep.

“Ngh, yes?” he replied, rolling towards the voice and blinking his eyes.

“I'm sorry, um,” said Dimiter.

Cliopher pushed himself up. “Something wrong?” he asked.

Dimiter nodded, rubbing his arms.

“Did you have a bad dream?” asked Cliopher, and when Dimiter still looked awkward, he realised what must have happened. “Had an accident?” he asked, gently.

Dimiter nodded.

“Thank you for coming to get me,” said Cliopher, some vague memory surfacing of his mother talking with one of his aunts about such an issue. “Let's sort it out.”

He shoved his feet into his slippers, and followed Dimiter back to his room. “It's not your fault,” he said, when Dimiter still looked a little scared, “Everyone has bad dreams sometimes.”

He stripped the bed, and bundled everything into the laundry hamper. Then he turned to his son. “Do you want a wash or a bath? And do you want help?”

“Wash?” said Dimiter, quietly, “Help please.”

Cliopher nodded, “Of course, let's get some nice warm water and a fresh change of clothes.”

When he had Dimiter in clean sleepwear and bundled in a spare blanket, Cliopher realised something.

“I… Don't know where the spare sheets are,” he said, slightly embarrassed, “I'll find them in the morning. Would you like to sleep in the white room, or with me in my bed?”

“With you,” said Dimiter, his head peeking out from the blanket.

“Let's go then,” said Cliopher. Somehow seeing Dimiter follow him, the blanket wrapped around and trailing, reminded him of his Radiancy's most spectacular court costumes, in miniature. It was adorable, and Cliopher's heart sang.

When they reached the bed, Cliopher pulled back the covers and lifted Dimiter up and onto the bed, still wrapped in the blanket. His son didn't laugh, as he had hoped, but he did manage to get a smile out of him.

“You can take the blanket off if you want,” he said, pulling the covers back over Dimiter, “It might be a bit warm, you just looked so cosy.” He walked around the bed, and climbed in on the other side. It really was quite a generous bed, easily the size of three sleeping mats back home.

“I am,” said Dimiter, “Can you… can you tell me a story?” He sounded nervous, like this was an imposition.

“Of course,” said Cliopher, with a smile, “Any time. I'm not sure if I know your favourites though.”

“The one about the princess in the tower? You hummed the song before,” said Dimiter, quietly.

“Aurora?” whispered Cliopher, surprised. How did his son know that? It had been banned thoroughly, and for many years. It was illegal to sing, or to recite, or to own a copy. Cliopher didn't actually know where his had gone, but he had memorised it a long time ago.

Dimiter nodded, “Nana said it's a secret.”

“Nana was right, it's not allowed,” said Cliopher, and Dimiter looked a little disappointed. “But since you already *know* the secret…” and he began to sing softly. He didn't get very far before his son had drifted back to sleep.
