Kip attended the Imperial Apartments at the usual time, nervous about what was coming. Neither of them had been secretary when the Glorious One was acting as Judge of the Court of Last Resort, and as far as Kip knew he hadn't acted as an _investigative_ judge in the time Cliopher had been in the Service. As he was challenged and passed through the many doors, he was wishing fervently it could have been Conju instead.

When he arrived in the Official Study, two senior judges were there already, dressed in their formal robes. Kip bowed low, and said nothing. One of them nodded curtly, the other ignored him entirely. Well, that wasn't exactly a new reaction, and Kip preferred it to being told to leave, or physical aggression.

They weren't left waiting long before the Glorious One made his appearance. All three dropped into the obeisance, and were waved up by the Emperor.

“Good morning, Sayo Mdang,” said the Glorious One.

“Good morning, my lord,” said Kip, carefully keeping his gaze on the Emperor's hands. He was certain the two judges were absolutely furious that the commoner had been addressed before them.

“Lord Chief Justice, Lord Justice, is there anything to bring to our attention before we begin?” said the Glorious One, standing perfectly still and poised.

“No, Radiant and Serene One,” said the more elderly one, who Kip thought was probably the Lord Chief Justice. His voice was soft, but Kip knew from gossip that it could easily command a rowdy courtroom. But then, perhaps that was partially his piercing pale grey eyes.

“Let us remove to the Lesser Audience Room, and begin our summons,” said the Emperor. He didn't wait for a response, but swept out and through the doors. Kip waited for the Justices to proceed him, and dutifully followed where his lord led.

The room had already been prepared, and included a number of pages sitting ready, as well as legal clerks. Kip wondered if he was only here as a witness, but then he noticed the tiny desk beside the throne. While the clerks would take notes for the court, and its record of proceedings, the Private Secretary was needed still. Because of course, his Radiancy would not be allowed to make his own notes. 

When all the bows had been made, and everyone settled in their places, the Emperor turned slightly, and made a gesture to the Lord Chief Justice. “The Glorious One requires the following prisoners be brought,” came the soft voice. He rattled off a list of names and titles, and a number of pages jumped up and ran out of the room.

This seemed wildly inefficient to Kip. Surely they had known yesterday who these people would be, and could have arranged for them to be here in advance. He decided it must be something a previous Emperor had thought was entertaining, or an additional punishment, having the procession of the prisoners through the Palace during one of the busiest times of the day.

Some time later, the prisoners were escorted in. The nobles were finely dressed, and looked clean and hale. To Kip's disappointed, but not his surprise, the two commoners were shackled, barefoot, in plain linen smocks. Both looked like they had already been beaten. They'd been treated worse as official prisoners than Kip had been when kidnapped.

There was also the Commander of the Guard, his deputy, Ser Rhodin, and the other junior guard who had been Ser Rhodin's search partner. A very nervous member of the Service that Kip recalled from the Archives scurried to join a small group of other Service members.

One of the clerks stood, bowed to the Emperor, and began reading the written confessions. They were formulaic, and obviously not written or dictated by those who had signed them. Kip wondered, if he looked at them, how steady the signatures would be.

As they were read, Kip noticed a conspicuous absence. When they were done, the clerk resumed their seat, and the Chief Justice said, coldly, “No confession, Canis?”

The Lord Treasurer looked stunned like he had been slapped. Well, socially, he had been. Kip had never heard anyone use his first name before. Presumably his parents had, but possibly nobody since then.

He stood, and bowed very low. “Glorious and Righteous One,” he said, ignoring the judge, “I have nothing to confess, except my eternal devotion to you and the Empire.” Without waiting, he sat down again.

Kip couldn't imagine what game he was trying to play. How could he be so confident the Emperor would believe whatever excuses he made.

The business of the court moved forward, and Kip tried to concentrate on his notes. He could at least pretend that this discussion of the kidnap of one of the Glorious One's servants was about some other person. This worked perfectly fine until he was summoned as a witness by the Justices.

Using every trick he knew to stay calm, Kip left his desk and walked around to the position for one being questioned. He bowed low, first to the Emperor and then to the Justices. Feeling nearly as tense as the first day in the Glorious One's study, he carefully looked only at his Emperor's jewelled hands.

“What time did you leave the Presence?” asked the Chief Justice, his tone mild.

“At noon, Glorious One,” answered Kip. Everyone had directed their answers to the Emperor, no matter who asked.

“Where did you go?” asked the other judge.

“To the Imperial Archives, Illustrious One, via the back stairs,” said Kip.

“Why?” added the Chief Justice.

Uncertain what he meant exactly, Kip answered, “The main staircase is very busy at that time of day, Glorious One.”

“Why did you go to the Archives?” asked the Emperor, “Why not go to lunch?”

Kip took a deep breath, and hoped he said the right thing. “Glorious One, you had requested I verify the dates of several previous reports. It would not have been appropriate for me to have lunch until the task was completed.”

Watching carefully, he thought he saw the tiniest bit of tension leave the Emperor's hands. That let him relax very slightly as well. He had said the right thing.

“Where were you accosted?” asked the Chief Justice, his tone unchanged.

“In the hallway outside the Imperial Archives, Glorious One,” said Kip. Then unable to help himself added, “A man, who I assumed to be an Imperial Guard from his dress, stopped me and asked a question. Then I heard a crack behind me. That is all I remember before I woke in the dungeons, Your Serenity.”

“Is the man who stopped you in this room? Point to him if he is,” said the second judge.

Aware that his gesture was almost certainly condemning the man to a horrific death, Kip turned slightly so he could see those under arrest. He pointed at the man on the end, and quickly lowered his hand.

“Let the record show that Cliopher Madon identified the prisoner already noted to have confessed to the same,” said the judge.

To his relief, Kip was dismissed as a witness, and the guards were called forward next. He resumed his seat, and took up his pen again. Once again, he was able to pretend they weren't all talking about him.

Finally, the Lord Treasurer was called.

“Do you maintain your earlier statement?” asked the Chief Justice.

“I do, Glorious One. Never did I intend or instruct those involved to commit treason,” the Lord Treasurer said, sounding confident.

The Emperor remained motionless.

“You do not deny that you told them to detain Sayo Mdang?” asked the other judge. 

“I do not. But that is a matter between the Cavalier an Vilius and I, not one for this court, Illustrious One.”

Kip very nearly gasped. Had the Lord Treasurer really just told the Emperor to mind his own business?

“Treason does not require intent,” said his Radiancy, sounding as calm as always, “As established in the Astandalan Law Code, book three, section twenty-seven, paragraph four. Confirmed by Empress Zangora IV in her ruling against the Secoro Uprising and Zangora VII in her ruling against the Nishi Pirates.”

“Glorious One, every action I took was in your service, to the glory of the Empire,” said the Lord Treasurer, his poise still perfect, “I wished to bring your secretary, the son of an old friend, in line with your government.”

Kip remembered the way Conju had nearly had a panic attack after interviewing with the Lord Treasurer. Friendship among the high aristocracy was clearly a different relationship than among commoners. He couldn't think of anything 'friendly' which would result in Conju so scared.

At the side of the room, Kip saw one of the noble prisoners, the man who had made him write to Conju, whisper to one of the guards. The guard then went and talked to the clerks. Had they more to say?

“Your method, whatever your motives, prevented Sayo Mdang carrying out our express instructions,” said the Emperor to the Lord Treasurer, coldly.

“Illustrious one, you did not even know the servant was missing until nearly a full day later, it cannot have been a great inconvenience.”

Stomach churning, Kip waited for the Emperor to respond. Would he truly execute the Lord Treasurer for such a technicality? Wouldn't the rest of the nobility object, possibly making him a martyr? Yet if the Emperor didn't, and only removed the Lord Treasurer from his position, he would be a magnet for every disaffected noble. Would it be best for the Emperor to keep him close, where it was easier to monitor his actions?

“That,” said the Glorious One, “Is both untrue, and immaterial. You are dismissed.”

The Lord Treasurer bowed, and returned to his seat with the other nobles who had been arrested.

One of the clerks stood up. “Glorious One, Lord Eyaris Canis would like to make a further statement.”

The Emperor gestured his acceptance. Nervously, the man who must have been some relative of the Treasurer, stepped forward. Rather than bowing, he dropped into the full formal prostration. “Most merciful lord,” he said to the floor, “Although I have already confessed, and am wholly undeserving of your consideration, I beg you to hear my full repentance, and extend your gracious hand with kindness to my family.”

He paused, but when no further signal was given, he continued. “I followed Sayo Mdang from the Imperial Apartments, on his usual route to the Archives. When I reported his capture to my uncle, I informed him that I had seen no sign that Lord Conju had attended your illustrious self. He said, ‘I don't care if he's in the Emperor's bed or his own, I'm going to whip the an Vilius whelp into line.’”

It seemed everyone in the room froze, except for the lord prostrate on the floor, who was clearly shaking. Of course, courtiers working their way up via the Emperor's bed had been common in Eritanyr's reign. Emperor Artorin, as far as Kip knew, had no concubines. Had never had anyone purified for touch. It was one thing to say Kip had got is position by sleeping with Conju; that was plausible, if incorrect. To slander the Emperor that way was very dangerous. 

“Lord Canis knew, then, that Sayo Mdang had been accosted directly after leaving your Presence,” said the Lord Chief Justice, to the Emperor, “He had ample opportunity then to realise and repent.”

“Did Lord Canis say anything else about either Sayo Mdang or the Cavalier an Vilius?” asked the Emperor.

“Glorious One, on many occasions he has spoken with scorn about the Cavalier an Vilius. I do not know which threats were credible, Gracious Lord,” said the trembling Lord Eyaris, struggling with his words. “He only referred to Sayo Mdang as ‘an Vilius's toy’, Serenity.”

Kip wondered if the Lord Treasurer even knew his name before today. If he had been told it, he had clearly not considered it worth remembering. Some aristocrats were known to simply rename the people who worked for them so they didn't have to bother learning their names. It wouldn't surprise him at all if Lord Canis was that type.

“Your assistance to the court has been noted, Lord Eyaris,” said the Emperor.

This was clearly a dismissal. When Lord Eyaris rose, he mumbled, “My most humble thanks for your unwavering kindness despite my manifold failings, merciful and gracious one.” Kip could see the distress in his eyes, as he looked wildly around the room. But he managed to work his way back to his seat without incident.

Next, the clerks and guards presented the material evidence retrieved from the dungeons and the Lord Treasurer's apartments. One of the clerks pointed out Kip's note to himself regarding the reports he had gone to the Archives to read. They noted Kip's system of task management, and that this had been categorised as 'urgent, before end of day'. Kip hadn't specifically remembered making that note, but of course it would have been. His fingers itched to get the files back, he badly wanted to check that he hadn't lost anything when he'd had to remake his notes from memory.

The evidence continued for some time, and Kip realised it was not only about the plan and results of kidnapping him. Embezzlement, bribery, all kinds of corruption were laid bare before the court. Even extortion. Lord Canis had carefully stopped just short of stealing from the tithes, which *would* have been treason. He clearly knew where the line was, and walked right up to it. It made Kip furious that the man could commit nearly any crime, facing only a fine if he was caught. Now only a technicality would see him face repercussions that were anything more than a minor inconvenience.

“We will retire to make our judgement,” said the Emperor, as the noon bells faded, “We will resume the day after tomorrow.”

Everyone but the guards prostrated themselves before the Glorious One. He left, escorted by his guards. Kip packed away his things, and followed behind at a trot. Without turning, or slowing, the Emperor must have recognised Kip's footsteps. 

“Deliver your notes to us by tomorrow morning, Sayo Mdang,” he said, “Try to take some time for yourself on Silverheart.”

“Thank you, Glorious One,” said Kip, with a deep bow. He accepted the dismissal, and trailed by the guards someone still seemed to think necessary, he returned to the an Vilius—to his—apartment.
