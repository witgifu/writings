You stood, as still as the marble statue of the chained lion beside you. A pair, a reflection, simile and simulacrum. Yellow lion, black plinth; yellow robes, black idol. Your light glinted off the golden chains, one chain reflecting another. 

One of your hands stretched out, three fingers touching your desk. Three points of contact with the world, with the perfect sandalwood that had been smoothed so much you could hardly tell it had ever been a living thing. Another metaphor, perhaps.

Waiting was a skill you had perfected. Stillness, silence, smallness. Now, you waited for a new secretary candidate. Not that they were late, only that you had not bothered eating any more than half of your breakfast.

It was a continuing problem that the Palace did not seem to contain one person who had all the skills you needed in a secretary. Of course, you were not allowed to _ask_ for particular skills. No, this had to be done in the least efficient manner possible, like so many rituals of the Empire. By some method you had never managed to ascertain, the Master of Offices would select and send you a candidate. You would then trial them for a morning.

It was no mock battle, used to test the army recruits. You actually did need to get work done each morning, and this was the person through whom you must do it. A few candidates had not made it through the morning, which really was very irritating. If you had been allowed to write yourself — but no. It wasn't worth thinking about the impossible.

You had tried so very hard to keep the last secretary. Reminding him repeatedly of what should happen with all the work you produced together (or possibly, you produced despite him). But it really was a terrible breech of security to simply lose a letter to one of your admirals.

(You were not supposed to know that the Guard had betting pools on how long each secretary would last. Or that you knew who had won the last round.)

The new candidate arrived, blessedly, slightly early. The guards stamped their spears, and you gestured to admit them.

“The Cavalier Conju enazo Argellian an Vilius, Glorious One,” Sergei announced. The man so named dropped into the elegant obeisance of one who had been trained since childhood. You could see he was well-dressed, even while not looking directly at him.

You hoped that the Master of Offices had not _once again_ sent you someone with more fashion sense than secretarial skills. It must be possible to have both. Surely. Or at least send those with only fashion sense to your dressing room, rather than your study. Perhaps, you wondered, some of your less-skilled Grooms of the Chamber had secretarial skills they had never told you about.

“Rise and take your seat,” you said, with the matching gesture. The Cavalier would be expected to note and remember the gesture for next time. Talking was also supposed to be below your dignity. Sometimes you wondered how your dignity could breathe, surely the air must be thin up there.

As the Cavalier sat at the appointed desk, the bells rang nine o'clock.

(Fourteen years, one month, one day, twenty-one hours)

The way he arranged his tools perfectly, and the lack of use they showed, did not fill you with confidence. Golden skin, aristocratic tattoos, head as bare of hair as your own. Silk robes, finely embroidered, and a silver gilt pen in his hand. Was that apple trees embroidered on his mantle? No, oaks.

The name and the colours of his mantle tickled something in your memory, and you saw a flash of a similar looking young woman presented to you as the family heir. From somewhere not too far away, one of the estates on Ysthar. He was the exact image of a spare child given a place in the Upper Secretariat to keep them busy.

You looked at your coronation portrait, reminding yourself of the oaths you (we) had made. You would try, again. Every day, you would try. With whatever tools you were permitted. 

Start at the beginning. Dictation. You had several letters that urgently needed replies, not least replacing the one that went missing. So, you began.

The Cavalier was perhaps a trifle slow. The rhythm between you and he was not quite in sync. You would adapt, a longer breath here, a gentle pause there. It would not be the first time, and alas, it would probably not be the last.

He made no attempt to look at you, and so you were able to steal glances at him. Focused entirely on the paper before him, the Cavalier seemed to conserve every bit of motion he could, while making his precise marks. His face betrayed no emotion, much like yours. Like the silence of your servants, and the hard marble floors, and the glow of your skin, it was one of the unchangeable features of this place. 

At the end of re-dictating the letter to the admiral, you paused. “We should review that letter so it may be sent immediately,” you said.

The Cavalier pulled out a fresh sheet of paper, and with surprising speed wrote up a fair copy. True, it was a short letter, but the confidence of the writing surprised you. In the past, you had often allowed your secretaries to take away most, if not all, of the work you dictated, and finish it later. It added a day to the process, but it generally resulted in more legible texts. It was only due to the delay caused by the loss of the previous letter that you had asked this time.

By the half-hour bell, you had found the matching rhythm for the dictation. He asked not a single question, and you wondered if that was because he had none, or because he was too afraid. Would you find a pile of documents to seal tomorrow full of errors? It was impossible to know, yet.

At the mid-morning break, you were supplied with refreshments. The Cavalier was obviously surprised to be offered anything while in your presence. He accepted only a glass of water, and placed it on his desk as far away as possible from his completed notes. The water was no doubt perfectly ordinary, but you wondered idly if perhaps he thought it might be enchanted to jump, placing it so far away.

You sipped your tea, and nibbled on a wafer-thin biscuit. The Cavalier ignored the water, and pencilled in additional notes on his completed pages. Was the careful angle of each sheet just a personal quirk, or part of a structure? You wondered if the system he used had a name, even a series of books and an accreditation. Would you someday be presented with the first six-year-old to be able to do this?

At the end of the morning, you had decided he seemed adequate to the task. The bells rang, and as they echoed he packed away his notes and tools.

(Fourteen years, one month, two days)

Seemingly to the empty air, or possibly to the guards, but actually to the Cavalier, you said “Inform the Master of Offices that we accept his choice, and duly appoint the Cavalier an Vilius as our personal secretary.”

With his tools and notes, he slipped elegantly from the chair and into his obeisance once more. For the first time in the entire morning, he spoke. “It is a great honour you do your servant, Glorious One.”

You gestured to dismiss him, and he backed out of the room. It was time for another ritual. Through the inner door of the study, past a chattering crowd that fell down around you. Another day in your life sentence.
