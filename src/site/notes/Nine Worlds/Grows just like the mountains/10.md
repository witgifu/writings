Liliane delivered the mail with breakfast. This wasn't the first post Kip had received since he had moved to the Vilius apartment, but the quantity in one go was a little surprising. However, it could simply be that the mail had been held up somewhere, and this represented a week or more of letters. It didn't have to be that someone had died.

When he opened the first one from his sister, he realised his error. He looked at the rest of the pile with trepidation.

“Is it bad news?” said Conju, sympathetically.

“That depends on your perspective. I suspect I am due to get a letter from every member of my family and every friend on the same topic. The letter I sent to Ghilly must have arrived,” said Kip, trying to hold back his feelings. He put the letters aside for now. Later, in private, he could read them and cry.

Conju gave the stack of letters a disapproving glare. “Well, if you would like some _good_ news, my mother has written back, and is pleased with everything,” he said to Kip, and folded back up the letter he had been reading. “Although she seems to believe I took the position out of political ambition, which… well,” he gave a sympathetic look to Kip, “Our parents will have certain _ideas_ about us.”

Kip's stomach twisted at the thought that somewhere in the stack was very likely a letter from his mother. She would be furious. Oh, it was going to be awful, and he would have to write back and manage to apologise without actually saying he was coming back soon, or going to get back together with Ghilly.

As if he could see right into Kip's plans for the evening, Conju asked, “Will you be willing to come to Court tonight? I know you think it beneath you—”

“I didn't say that, Conju, I said I don't belong there. Not the same thing at all,” replied Kip, taking the bait, “Court is for your set, I would be as out-of-place as a monkey or a donkey.”

“Lady Persimmon did bring an ass on one memorable occasion,” said Conju, with a straight face, “She claimed it was a better dancer than her husband.”

Kip snorted into his coffee. “Well, I don't know any court dances, so you can't use that excuse.”

“You said you play the oboe, so you can't be entirely without rhythm,” said Conju, “Do you have local country dances?”

“I know the dances of home. But Conju, they are nothing like court dances, or even country dances, here. I wouldn't want to embarrass you,” said Kip, quietly. Court dances were much more about being in synch with your partner or set. Kip had never even dared try them.

“I will teach you Korobeiniki,” said Conju, “It starts out so slow even the great-grandmothers can dance it, and gets faster each repeat. People drop out when they get lost or tired.”

Kip could see Conju wasn't going to let this excuse fly. “I don't have a court outfit, I don't know the dances, and the only person I know would be you Conju,” he said, unable to stop himself from letting a note of despair creep into his voice, “Unless you count my old boss who I believe called me a 'lazy, feckless, harlot' to the entire room when he heard I'd left his department without asking.”

“The first two are fixable, and the third is why you *should* go,” said Conju, gently.

“So everyone can see me dancing with you and confirm that I am, in fact, exactly what they assumed?”

“No,” said Conju, “So they can see you have the favour of the Emperor. So they can see you next to me when I talk to the Minister for Health as an equal. Who, after the Emperor, do you think is the most important person at court?”

“The Lord Treasurer? Or perhaps Princess Indrogan,” said Kip, uncertain, “But _I_ don't have the favour of the Emperor, Conju. If either of us does it would be you.”

Conju held up a hand, “His Radiancy said 'very good' to you, not to me. The most powerful person at court is whoever the Emperor listens to. And he reads your summaries every morning.”

“But he reads all the reports! If he didn't, all the memos… Oh my gosh,” said Kip, “Are you suggesting we should tamper with the reports?” He was both surprised and outraged. If they started doing things like that, all it would take would be for one minister to request a meeting or an audience and complain. But… They did have plans to change those processes. So that audience and meetings had at least a topic provided in advance. Which would mean they would know, could prepare…

“No!” said Kip loudly, standing up and dropping his fork, “I would never! This is too much power in one pair of hands.”

“You are aware the Empire is an absolute monarchy, Cliopher?” said Conju, rolling his eyes.

Kip blinked for a moment, and sat down again. “Sorry,” he mumbled, and buried his face in his coffee.

“If you want more accountability in the system, then we need people to know who we are,” said Conju, “Part of the problem with the systems, if you can call them that, in the Offices of State, is that nobody is *responsible*. Things are just done by whoever is available at the time.”

That was certainly true. It made everyone's job harder, unless you were in a position to bribe the Master of Offices. It seemed almost entirely random if your request would be handled or binned.

Kip mumbled, “It still doesn't sound any more fun than writing to my mother.”

“Well, consider it work then,” said Conju with a sigh, “You're attending a networking event to ensure the smooth operation of our planned new systems.”

“Do I get overtime?” asked Kip, with a smile.

“No, Fifth Degree Secretaries get time-in-leu,” said Conju, with a little snark, “See, I have read the Service Regulations!”

“But you forgot I'm only a Fourth Degree,” said Kip, sagely, “You should know that if you looked at my personnel file.”

“*You* should check your last pay stub,” said Conju, with a smile.

Shooting Conju a look of deep suspicion, Kip left the table and walked briskly to his room to fetch his pay-book. He flipped through to the most recent page.

> • IBS LS 4-4 Sal. (Scribbled initials)
> 
> • IBS LS 5-1 Sal. (Scribbled initials)

He turned to the back section, where his various positions were listed. His role in the Department of Health had an end date, and his new position was listed with a start date.

> Undersecretary to Personal Secretary to Lord Emperor. Lower Secretariat 5.

Kip closed the book and put it away carefully. He wanted a moment to think, before he returned to Conju.

It wasn't fair. Promotions should be based on merit, skill, hard work. Not which nobleman you met at an orgy one time.

On the other side, Kip had worked hard the entire time he had been in the Service. He had gone above and beyond repeatedly, and only ever managed to get promotions through luck and the support of the very rare good manager. Most, if not all, the people he had been working alongside had been Fifth Degree Secretaries. So it wasn't as if he felt he didn't deserve it at all.

Beneath that was Kip's undercurrent of fear. That this was too good to be true, too perfect. He hadn't made a single real friend in all his years here. Part of him was still waiting for Conju to show his true colours. To turn around and see a smirk on Conju's face as he said, “Thanks for your help Cliopher,” and kicked him out the door.

The fear part of him said to go back to the dinning room and thank Conju profusely. It said to save every coin of extra salary so he could buy passage home urgently if he needed to. Hold still. Don't flinch, just wait, be quiet, smile politely, and when he lets you go…

There was a knock on the door frame behind him.

“Come in,” said Kip, automatically.

“I should have told you earlier, Cliopher,” said Conju.

Kip whirled around to face him.

“I honestly forgot,” said Conju, looking awkward, “There was so much happening those first few days. When the secretary in the Offices of State fetched your personnel file, they were very reluctant to allow anyone in the Lower Secretariat to take the role. I had to threaten to go back to the Master of Offices. She insisted that if it had to be you, then you must be Fifth Degree. I am sorry I didn't tell you earlier.”

“It's fine,” said Kip, trying to pretend to both himself and Conju that he believed that, “Thank you, I suppose? It feels like a strange way to get a promotion.”

“I know you'd rather a standardised, merit-based process, probably with a double-blind review of the applications,” said Conju, with a small smile, “But please don't march over to Offices of State and demand they demote you.”

Kip sighed, “This whole place is more casually corrupt than my Uncle's dodgy import business. It's less well-organised than my Aunt's weaving co-op. My cousin takes more care in her puppet-theatre than most people do in their work here.”

“Well, sayo-most-important-man-at-court, shall we go and do something about that?” said Conju, as the bells struck the half-hour. “And we can try to find some time to get you court robes and both of us correct uniforms.”

Kip looked down at what he was wearing. Clothes were _expensive_. It was part of the reason he liked having a uniform, nobody could really complain that you wore the same thing every day. He didn't want to think what _court robes_ were going to cost. They'd probably be _silk_.

“Okay,” he said, “But you have to teach me more than once dance. I don't want to be looked down on by someone's ninety-five-year-old grandfather.”

Conju took his arm, and gently pulled Kip out and down the hallway. “I will teach you _any_ dance you want to learn, Cliopher.”

That night, Kip arrived back first. He took the chance, while he was alone, to open the rest of his mail. It was bad, but not quite as bad as he had feared.

Most importantly, Ghilly had said she understood. Not only because his mother would have found it hard to run a campaign for them to get back together if neither wanted to. But almost entirely because he felt terrible for how he had treated her, and didn't want to make her feel like she had been foolish or cheated on. 

So he started, as he felt he must, with a letter to Ghilly. He apologised again, and thanked her for how generous she had been to him. From the postscript, he tried to congratulate her on her own achievements as best he could.

He wrote to Bertie, and Vinyë, and everyone else he had received a letter from. Again and again he tried to explain that yes, it was entirely his fault, and he had been incredibly selfish, but now he was trying to do the right thing. He could only hope that some of them would believe him.

Lastly, he came to the letter from his mother. He sat there, ink drying on his pen, not knowing what to say to her. There was no way he could force her to understand. All he could really do was hope that, in time, she might stop pestering him about it. Well, there was one thing he could ask her. So he wrote a letter asking her, that however angry she was with him (and he admitted, he had been unfair), that she would be kind to Ghilly, and not ask her to take him back.

All the letters felt wholly inadequate. He couldn't face sealing any of them, he wanted to rewrite each one, to somehow make them better, make it better. Make everyone see that he _was_ trying, that he was being honest, that he would never joke about something like this.

Conju found him staring at the letter to his mother. 

“You read them?” Conju asked, from the open door.

Kip nodded. The tears on his face had dried and become itchy.

Walking over, Conju saw the stack of replies. “And you have already replied to them?”

Nodding again, Kip folded the letter from his mother, and put it back on the stack. He sighed, and turned around. “There will probably be more tomorrow.”

“But you are done with your obligations to them for today. So, let's practice dancing. Will you get changed, or will you go into the city in your uniform?” asked Conju, with a twinkle in his eye.

“The city?” squeaked Kip, suddenly imagining hundreds of people watching him fumble through the dances.

Conju rolled his eyes, “We need music to dance to, Cliopher. Unless you can sight-read the music while dancing?”

“Oh,” said Kip, sheepishly, “No, I can't dance and play at the same time.”

“There are plenty of dance halls in the theatre district, and nobody will be watching you, they'll be too busy staring lovingly in to the eyes of their dates. So, change, or uniform?”

Kip could tell which Conju preferred, and agreed to get changed.
