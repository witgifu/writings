You had just sat down to breakfast, when Lord Igel approached. Usually, he brought any issues to your attention as you were dressed for the morning. So this discussion was not going to be about an unexpected change in your schedule, or the introduction of a new assistant.

He dropped into the obeisance, again not incorrect, but unusual. Was he here to beg for his son to be reinstated? To be allowed to stay at court a few months longer? Was four days not long enough time to find an excuse? You waved him up, and braced yourself.

“Yes, Lord Igel?” you said, permitting him to speak.

“Glorious One, I have a few items to bring before you. Firstly, my son has left the Palace this morning for Zangoraville, where he will be watched over closely by the Elector. I apologise unreservedly, and will return his salary.”

He made a bow of first degree apology, with a gesture to indicate a great debt was owed. Your mask was perfect, as you replied only with a gesture of acknowledgement.

Perhaps it was the son that was the problem. Or could be made into a convenient excuse. Surely a wayward son would have at least put in enough of an appearance occasionally to be recognised.

Lord Igel continued. “The oboe has arrived from the Ouranatha after being purified, Glorious One. Would my most generous lord wish to present it, our shall I have it sent to Sayo Mdang?”

Here was the game of courts, played by a master. He was right, of course, you did value this. And you wanted to see Sayo Mdang's reaction, to feel joy in his joy. Your anticipation of this would blunt some of your anger. You were, after all, a man.

“I will present it. This afternoon; we can make time after the petitions.”

You turned back to open your reports. Lord Igel did not move. You carefully did not sigh, and waved for him to continue.

“Would my Glorious and Illustrious Lord wish for Sayo Mdang to be presented at court?”

Well, what scheme had Lord Igel thought up now? Was he wanting to dress Sayo Mdang up in the First Concubine jewels and parade him across the daïs? Did he hope to tempt you into claiming his body publicly and privately?

What did Lord Igel see, when he looked at you? Just a younger version of your uncle? Someone who had been shut away for so many years they didn't understand the desires of the flesh?

“Why would we do that?” you asked, somewhat coldly.

Lord Igel made a third-degree bow of apology. “I know my Glorious Lord would not want to keep Sayo Mdang in the Tower. If his rank and position were not acknowledged publicly, your Radiancy, he would be at risk outside of it. If acknowledged, he would be escorted by at least one of your Imperial Guards, Glorious One.”

He was right. You wanted to swear, and throw the breakfast dishes on the floor. You did not want to parade Sayo Mdang through the Court. But you would, if you had to. You would, to keep him safe, in this nightmare you had put him in. 

How many more times would you be forced to take another step down this road?

You sat, rigid, your posture as perfect as your old tutor could have wanted. You stared at a rose roundel on the wall, to the left of Lord Igel and to the right of your guard.

“How should we present Sayo Mdang, then?” you asked, your voice sounding strangely distant even in your own ears. “As our secretary?” 

You made this last suggestion more in hope than expectation. There was no court introduction for your secretary, any more than there was for any other position in your household, where people did actual work.

“Glorious One, there is an old position, sometimes granted to consorts, or to other favourites. The Hands of the Emperor.”

Relief swamped you. Perhaps you had misjudged Lord Igel, he had clearly done his research before approaching you. He had found a solution that did not have you wanting to scrape your own skin off.

“Yes,” you said, struggling to keep your face calm, “that would be acceptable. See to it with the Master of Ceremonies. I will inform Sayo Mdang.”

You took a deep, counted breath.

Lord Igel *still* did not accept the implied dismissal. You let yourself take two more deep breaths before motioning for Lord Igel to continue.

“Most Gracious and Glorious One, I am sure the Master of Etiquette would happy to teach Sayo Mdang, and the Ouranatha have thoroughly instructed him in the ritual requirements. However, I believe his education in the ways of the court would be greatly aided by arranging a companion.”

You strongly disliked the Master of Etiquette. He was not the same man who had first told you that you were no longer a person. But he was vacuous and obsequious and his greatest joy seemed to be finding fault in others. 

“And I assume you have a candidate for this position?” you asked, wondering if perhaps this was how he wanted to bring his son back to court.

“Yes, Glorious One, the Cavalier Conju an Vilius. He has performed this role before, and comes from an excellent family.”

You did not trust Lord Igel's judgement in who might get along with Sayo Mdang. Would Sayo Mdang feel obligated to accept the first candidate? Would he know what to look for? You did not want to choose on his behalf. Yet, this was not a position where you could interview a list of candidates (as if you ever were allowed to do such a thing).

“We will consult with Sayo Mdang,” you said, and firmly opened the first document on the pile next to you.

Lord Igel, it seemed, was finally done. He bowed himself out, and you were left to your marmalade and tax reports.

Should you discuss Lord Igel's plan with Sayo Mdang first, or wait until the afternoon? You did not want him to think the oboe was somehow a bribe, to accept this an Vilius. Or a reward for doing so. Oboe first then.

The afternoon was spent hearing petitions from your nobles again. Sayo Mdang handed you some prepared reference, and then sat at his desk to take notes.

Leafing through the stack, you realised he had researched those who had notified of their attendance. Names, families, places, likely requests. It was wonderful having someone — or now two people, for surely Sayo Mdang had enlisted the help of the undersecretary — to help you.

You politely refused a grant request that was a thinly-veiled attempt to get you to fund a summer house. Granted a petition to pay death duties over several years. Congratulated someone on a marriage. Referred various people to the correct government minister.

At the end, of course, you were still tired. More tired than usual, from wrestling with all those bickering children. Perhaps less tired than you would have been without the notes.

As Sayo Mdang cleaned his brushes and put away his pens, you said, “If you have time now, Sayo Mdang, I have something for you.”

He looked up, as he put the last items away. “Of course, Glorious One.”

Well, quite. Your requests were never to be denied. And yet, the fiction that they could be helped you feel human.

Lord Igel came through the door to the inner apartments. He bowed to you, and said, “it is in the breakfast room, Glorious One.”

You resisted the urge to rub your hands in glee. Instead, you strode through the doors, which your guards opened for the three of you.

On the small ebony table in the breakfast room was a beautiful leather-wrapped box. A matching leather document wallet sat beside it. Next to the table was a brass music stand, elegant without being ostentatious.

You walked over, and perhaps your steps were a little lighter, a little faster. Turning, you looked at Sayo Mdang, who was eyeing the table in confusion. You made the gesture for a gift offered, and Lord Igel quietly left the room.

Sayo Mdang looked even more confused.

“We were saddened to think that you might be without music on our account, Sayo Mdang. This is a gift, for you,” you said, and somewhere inside I was practically giddy with excitement. 

It was so rare that you were able to offer someone a gift. Of course, the Lord Emperor bestowed blessings on all his people. Things they needed, requested, and sometimes even wanted. But to give a gift, something just for the joy it brought someone else. A gift that (you hoped) was without political repercussions.

Sayo Mdang stepped over slowly, admiring the music stand first. Then, he reached out tentative hands, and popped the catches on the leather-wrapped box. He lifted the lid, and his eyes went wide.

It looked to be a superb instrument. Not ostentatious, covered in gems or silver chasing. Simple, but of the highest quality. It was split into pieces, and nestled into the plush red velvet interior.

His hand floated above it, as if he didn't dare to touch it.

“Go on,” you said, “it's been purified. It's safe. If you break a reed, it will have to be disposed of properly, but it should come with...”

He flipped another catch, and a compartment in the lid opened to show a selection of reeds. His hand shook slightly, as he pulled one out, and examined it closely.

“It's not your original instrument, sorry,” you said, suddenly realising that perhaps he wouldn't want it. There was a bond, you knew, between musician and the instrument they loved and had played a long time. Even a very fine instrument was no substitute for one you knew well.

He bit his lip, and looked at you. “It's beautiful, my lord, thank you so much, I...” he looked back at the case, and fitted the reed back in place. “I don't deserve… I'm not a good enough player, for an oboe like this.”

“If you are about to tell me this instrument is too expensive, Sayo Mdang, please refrain. It was tithed, and no doubt languishing in a store room somewhere. It should be played.”

He turned to face you, and fell in to the full prostration. “Thank you, Glorious One, for your great kindness and generosity. I will try to do it justice.”

You waved him up, and added, “We won't ask you to play, we know it would be cruel to ask when you haven't been able to practice. But we hope you have some time soon to enjoy playing for yourself.”

He rose to his knees, and smiled at you. That smile was the greatest give you had been given since you had become emperor.

“Please get up, Sayo Mdang,” you said. You had never felt comfortable with people kneeling. Not deserving or wanting adoration.

Sayo Mdang rose. He looked once more at the oboe, lightly brushing a proprietary hand over the wood and keys.

“We understand that you have not left the Tower since being admitted, Sayo Mdang?” you asked, now reluctant to break his joy with more requirements.

“Yes, Glorious One. The priest-wizards were very explicit,” he said, uncertainty rising in his eyes.

“It is not required to maintain the purifications. Even we leave, to attend court, and rituals. You may leave if you wish. But… for your safety...” 

You saw the light fall from his eyes, and his face fell from looking at yours to the floor. You hastened to clarify.

“It would best if you had a guard as escort. And if you were presented at court first, so that our courtiers know you have our favour.” You stopped, and breathed slowly.

Sayo Mdang looked up, disappointment clear in his face. He would need to learn to control his expression as well. You would miss seeing his emotions.

You continued, regaining your serenity, “We would appoint you as Hands of the Emperor. It is an old ceremonial position, to touch where the Emperor cannot. It often went to consorts.”

Then his face went blank. “Of course, Glorious One,” he said, and made a bow of apology, somewhere between first and second degree. “I will serve you however you desire.”

Your heart almost shattered. That had been exactly the wrong thing to say. He was now expecting what you did not want, to be paraded as a body. Perhaps even as a conquered 'primitive' culture, in the costume he had been presented to you in.

“Sayo Mdang,” you said, your voice sounding hollow as you tried to push away your feelings and maintain the image of the Emperor. 

“We would place on you no obligation. If you would stay, we would ask nothing more of your service to us than to continue as our secretary. We wish our court to know you have our protection, esteem, and favour.”

You couldn't look him in the eyes, you couldn't face the betrayal that must be there. The surrounding cage was growing smaller. But you would hold open the door if you could.

“If you would rather leave, we would do what we can to see you safely—”

Sayo Mdang interrupted you, “I will stay, Glorious One, as long as you will have me.”

Now you looked at him. Something of the smile had returned to his eyes, if not the rest of his face. You loved that he would dare interrupt you. That he was willing to stay, despite everything.
