Kip washed his face, and changed into his uniform. The guards did not come for him.

He prepared the morning reports, because the undersecretary wouldn't know that needed to be done. But the guards didn't come.

He skipped breakfast. There was no point, couldn't possibly keep anything down. 

Sitting on his bed, he waited for the guards and shook. Were they giving him space to commit suicide first? He contemplated the penknife in his secretarial supplies. Would it be sharp enough? His shaking hands surely would not help.

If he failed to kill himself, they would have him patched up. Then executed when he was strong enough to make a good show.

There were stories of high nobles sent vials of quick-acting poisons, as a mercy. Kip was no high noble, to be eligible for such a gift. He faced a commoner's death for treason.

Was this where his ke'ea had led him? Was the song he heard not the star paths calling, but a siren, calling him to drown in the depths?

He stood up, walking over slowly to his writing kit. Pulling out a fresh sheet of paper, he tried to write a letter. It was hard to see through the tears, but he kept them off the page.

---

Mama,

I hope you receive this letter, and that everyone is well. I have been comforted on many lonely nights here by everyone's wonderful letters from home, and I am sorry I do not now have time to write back to you all. Please pass on my love.

I do not know if the proclamation will make it as far as the Vangaveye-ve, or if it will arrive before or after this letter, if it does. Please understand that I did my very best. As I write, I don't know what the formal charges will be.

It is hard to explain how easy it can be to make a mistake here. You know I have always had such troubles with Astandalan etiquette. I was recently appointed to a much higher position than I ever expected, and this also means I have so much further to fall.

Please do not think I regret coming. I only wish I could have done more while I was here.

Much love,
Kip

---

It was, Kip reflected, wholly inadequate. It was all he had time for now. He folded it up, and addressed it.

He sat there, waiting for the sound of a pair of guards in the hall.

The quarter bell rang. Kip was due at his desk. Leaving the letter, he gathered the rest of his things and made his way to the Imperial Study.

He set out his tools and papers. Perhaps the Emperor would have him write up the proclamation of his own treason. That was the kind of thing Emperor Eritanyr would have done.

Nothing Kip had seen of this Emperor suggested he was much in the mould of Eritanyr. And yet, the cold dismissal, the tear. He had hurt his lord, even if he did not understand how. He had plenty of time, kneeling on that stool, to consider what he had done wrong. 

He had, so far as he could tell, done as he had been taught. Had he missed some signal from the Emperor? Had he worked too fast, or too slow? Had Lord Igel mislead him? Was it something about the medication? Kip wished he could ask someone.

Just before the hour bell, the Emperor arrived. Kip rose swiftly from his chair, and dropped into his well-trained obeisance. The glow of the Emperor for once truly felt like the heat of a midday sun. The Sun-on-Earth, contained within this room, his light glinting off his gems and cloth-of-gold.

Kip held his breath, in fear and awe. The Emperor signalled Kip to rise, and he resumed his seat as the bell rang.

The Emperor did not say “good morning”, and so Kip remained silent. He held is pen in readiness to take dictation. Fearing to look up and see approbation distorting the beautiful face. That he had caressed, only hours ago.

The Emperor began by dictating a formula letter.

Kip's hand was steady, with years of practice. The rhythm of dictation, allowing the words to flow from his ears to his hands, helped calm his heart and breathing. His pen flicked with the curls and dance of scribal shorthand.

This, he knew how to do well. Here, he was comfortable, he understood what the Glorious One required of him.

“A memo to Ports and Sea Trade—”, began the Emperor.

“To the department, my lord, or to the minister personally?” asked Kip, automatically. Interrupting the Emperor. Kip's face went hot. He dared not look up.

“The department,” clarified the Emperor, his robes swirling as he turned.

“Thank you, Glorious and Illustrious One,” whispered Kip to his paper. All his tension and anxiety had returned.

There was nothing Kip could discern in the Emperor's voice, manner, or choice of work, to indicate he was displeased in any way. Had Kip been forgiven for what he had done?

The morning drew to a close. The Emperor paused, slightly, as if he was thinking of adding one last items to the agenda for the day. But then he said only, “That will be all for today, Sayo Mdang.”

The afternoon was uneventful. Kip discussed reports and correspondence with the undersecretary, requested the pages fetch documents from the archives. He returned to his room just before the Emperor would return from Afternoon Court. Kip still felt uneasy about encountering him in the halls.

The letter to his mother was sitting on the desk. Not quite where he had placed it. He would have wondered if the cleaning staff had moved it, except the Palace was cleaned by magic.

He could only assume Lord Igel or one of his team had read it. So they knew he was expecting the worst. Not that he had told his mother why. But Lord Igel had been there this morning, he had seen Kip waiting, and seen the Emperor ignore him.

Lord Igel knew Kip was in disgrace, and knew Kip knew. Kip could only hope Lord Igel would send the letter, when — if — when, Kip failed again.

While Kip sat stewing in his anxiety and fear, a knock came at the door. Surprised, he called, “Come in!”

He should have gone and opened the door, he realised, as it swung open gently. Everyone who had permission to be in the Apartments proper outranked Kip. He stood, and applied his best polite face.

It was Lord Igel.

“My sincerest apologies, my lord,” said Kip, and bowed.

Lord Igel scrutinised him closely. “You will attend the Glorious One tonight. Be in the small dining room well before the seventh bell. Make sure you are washed and in a clean uniform.”

“Yes, my lord,” said Kip, with another bow.

The Groom of the Chamber stalked off, leaving the door open behind him.

Kip tiptoed over and closed it. He slid shaking hands from the doorknob down the silky smooth wood. His mind froze for a moment, unsure which way was up. Was this a second chance, or was Lord Igel trying to force Kip to make another mistake? 

Did it matter which?

Whatever Kip had done last night had been wrong. His training as taná would say that friendship and equality should be the basis of a long-term relationship. Is that what the Emperor wanted?

But of course that was impossible. Nobody could be the equal of the Emperor. Even the Lord Magi bowed before him. 

Perhaps Kip had simply been too forward. It was following his instructions, but maybe they had been misinformed. Kip was, after all, the first concubine the Emperor had ever chosen. The Emperor had not seemed unhappy with his work as a secretary. It was possible he desired service more than seduction. Kip resolved to try his best to be more demure. 

A plan forward identified, Kip prepared for the evening. He stripped, and washed. First to get clean, and then carefully following the ritual bathing he had been taught by the priest-wizards. It used two kinds of soap, a special lotion, and three clean towels. 

They had refused to explain what it did. But it must have done something, because the priest-wizards could tell if someone had not done it fully, and would send them back to try again. Kip hated to think how much laundry it made.

By the time he was done and dressed, it was half a bell before he was due in the dinning room. Not enough time to get anything useful done. If he was going to sit around feeling anxious, he may as well do so in the dinning room, where at least he wouldn't need to be anxious about being late.

The dining room was set for two, at one table. One chair gilt, commanding the room, with the best view of the windows. Opposite it, a smaller, standard chair.

And yet, this was surely as close to equal as anyone dined with the Sun-on-Earth. Only someone purified would be permitted to sit so close. Kip could have been given a lower table. Or if he was here only to entertain the Emperor, he could have been given a stool to sit at his feet.

The table settings were exactly the same, except that the Emperor's eating sticks were gold, the others silver. This was the table settings for a Consort. Kip took a long, shaking breath. Perhaps it was not his seat, perhaps the Glorious One had summoned one of his female relatives.

He had seen nothing in the reports or correspondence to indicate the Emperor was considering marriage. But it could have been discussed in person when Kip wasn't there, or before Kip had been appointed as secretary.

Kip stepped carefully away from the table. He did not have enough information. Instead, he looked out the window. Up close, it was possible to peer through the fretwork and glimpse details of the city beyond.

At this distance, in the evening light, Astandalas truly lived up to the name 'golden'. The stone of the city was a beautiful colour, and the window glass sparkled in the low sun. Kip disappeared into memories of book stores, and cheese shops, and street performers.

Spears stamped outside the door, and it opened.

Kip spun around at the first sound, and fell into the obeisance. The Emperor strode into the room, and paused by the table. As he moved, Kip focused on the Emperor's hands, watching for any signal.

As he waited, his anxiety bubbled. This was surely the Emperor expressing his displeasure with Kip's behaviour. Finally, the Emperor signalled for Kip to rise.

Kip rose, but kept his eyes down at the Emperor's hands. Those beautiful, elegant fingers.

The Emperor took his chair, and signalled to Kip to take the other. This only confused Kip further. He took it, keeping track of the Emperor's hands as he did so, waiting to be corrected. No rebuke came. The Glorious One was displeased, but he was also honouring Kip beyond belief. 

The Emperor signalled, and the meal was served. Kip tried to focus on his table manners. 

 “We have very much appreciated your work these past few days, Sayo Mdang, you are a most excellent secretary,” said the Emperor, as he picked up his eating sticks.

“You are too kind to your servant, Glorious One. I am sure I have much to learn,” said Kip, as the back of his mind tried to discern any extra meaning in the Emperor's words. Was this a subtle further admonishment for his behaviour last night?

Another possibility crossed Kip's mind, as he picked up his own eating sticks. Had the medicine Kip administered effected the Emperor more than it seemed? Had Kip drugged his lord, and then taken advantage of him, and the Emperor had no memory?

Kip carefully picked up the slippery green stems, and chewed them slowly. He could only hope that the Emperor might explain.

Instead, the Emperor changed topic. “You are very dexterous with eating sticks, Sayo Mdang, we thought them not much used on Zunidh.”

“Thank you, Glorious One, I learned the skill here under the tutelage of the priest-wizards,” replied Kip, keep his eyes fixed on the table. 

“That sounds very difficult, the priest-wizards can be quite exacting,” said the Emperor, his tone neutral, his hands relaxed.

“Oh yes, Glorious One, they often were. For this class, however, we made a game of collecting glass marbles from a bowl,” said Kip, remembering the time fondly. “The theoretical classes were much harder, my lord.”

“The priest-wizards are very keen on their theory. It can be tiresome.”

“Yes, and with no magic of my own, Glorious One, I'm afraid I found it much harder to follow.”

“We have studied the philosophy of magic,” said the Emperor, as the greens were taken away and replaced by noodles in a peanut sauce, “and Old Shaian; it does not get much more logical if one does understand. It remains mostly memorisation. There is some logic, such as cooking purifying foods. But for example, a new pair of eating sticks would take the longer purification rite if they were metal, the shorter if they are entirely gold or silver. If they are wooden, it is better that they are destroyed after use.”

Kip looked more closely at the eating sticks. “Wood has to be destroyed after use, Glorious One?” he asked, tentatively. There were so many wooden items in daily life. From his comb, to the handle of his brushes.

“Only if it has entered the mouth, and it is on a specific list, which includes eating sticks. If it included all wooden objects, we would be replacing a lot of chairs,” said the Emperor.

“Of course, Glorious One,” said Kip, ducking his head, ashamed of his ignorance. They had probably covered this in the classes, and he had not remembered.

“The Ouranatha have never explained to us what is taught in the lessons you were given. If you have any questions, Sayo Mdang, please ask.”

There was one question, he was desperate to know the answer to. Dare he ask? He had almost been ordered to, so he would need to ask some question. How could he phrase it so as not to cause offence? 

“They seemed uncertain, Glorious One,” he began, hesitating, “as to what you might require, and so we were trained in a number of skills. Dancing, nursing, massage, card games, assisting with dressing, and so on. I was wondering, Illustrious One, if they did not tell you, if there was any further service I might be able to provide.”

He wanted to look up, to see if there was any reaction on the Emperor's face. But no, he had resolved to behave differently. He kept his eyes firmly on the Emperor's hands instead.

The Emperor put down his eating sticks. Kip held his breath, waiting for the dismissal he knew was coming. He should not have asked what the Glorious One wanted. It was his job to anticipate the Emperor's desire, not to ask. Even if he had already made a hash of that once. Kip put down his own eating sticks.

Feeling the heat rising in his face, Kip said, “I am sorry, Gracious and Merciful Lord, for asking such an impertinent question. I beg, Glorious One, please forget what I said.”

He was about to push his chair back, to prostrate himself. But the Emperor raised his hand in the gesture to wait.

“It had not occurred to us that they might teach you such things,” said the Emperor, “we were surprised.”

Kip folded his hands in his lap, under the table, to hide their shaking. Should he apologise for apologising? That seemed odd. So he waited in silence, his eyes only on the Emperor's hands.

The Emperor signalled for the next course. Only after he began eating, did Kip tentatively pick up his own eating sticks again. He did not feel hungry at all, his stomach twisting with anxiety. But he dutifully chose a small morsel and chewed it slowly.

At the end of the meal, the Emperor finally dismissed Kip. After leaving the presence, he very carefully did not run up to his rooms. He walked quietly and decorously.

He went through his rooms to his washroom. There he sank against the cold tiles and cried. 

Nothing he did was right. He did not belong, and the only skills he had that the Emperor wanted were his secretarial ones. Surely there were hundreds of better candidates for the personal secretary to the Emperor in the Service. None of them would be interrupting the Glorious One, or be forced on him at dinner. Or be used to drug him, or attempting to seduce him.

Why had the Emperor chosen Kip? Was it just a whim he now regretted? Why did he not dismiss Kip from the Palace if he did not want Kip?

There were no answers. All he got was a headache. He drank some water, washed his face, and went to bed.
