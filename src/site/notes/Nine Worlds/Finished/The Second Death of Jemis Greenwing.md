When my secretary, a most useful and competent woman, had looked tense handing over the letter, I noticed. 

“A private letter, my lord,” was all she had said.

I didn't say anything except, “thank you”, but I noticed, and wondered the letter could be. My private mail, from my niece, or other old friends, was usually left in my private study. This must have been assumed to be magical or political. The cover had been removed, so I had no indication how it might have been addressed, or from whom. Unfolding it, I scanned down the page quickly to find the sender.

> Lady Violet, Marchioness Noirell

That explained the mis-categorisation. We most often wrote, or had written, to discuss politics. Since the establishment of the Parliament in Rondé, there had been less need for that sort of correspondence. I wondered idly how long it had been, and decided possibly not since I had visited them both last spring. Or had that been the year before last?

With the memory of the bees of the Woods filling my mind, I turned back to the top of the page.

> Lord Peregrine,
> 
> I hope you are well, and that your work continues to delight and challenge you. As the spring rains fall, I remember fondly of you and all the villagers helping us clean out this old pile of stone to turn it into a home, all those years ago. It's been wonderful having the school here as well, nothing keeps you hopping quite like children.
> 
> I write, although Jemis has begged me not to. He has told me that you have more important things to worry about, and grumbled a lot that he shouldn't even have told me. But both of us can be stubborn if we need to, and even if it is only as Jemis' oldest and greatest friend, I believe you need to know.
>
> Jemis has been unwell for some months. We sent for the healers, of course, but at our age there is only so much they can do. Then two nights ago, Jemis woke in the witching hours from a true dream. I know he hasn't had one in years, not since he had that one of the children and the giant spider. But he has always known the true dreams from just regular dreams.
> 
> Oh, Perry, I don't know how to write this. I wish I could tell you in person first, or more likely make Jemis tell you himself. He saw himself dying, at midwinter. Here, at home, in our bedroom.
>
> I know you'll be very busy. Midwinter is always a tricky time to travel, and it will have political implications for all of Rondé, however much Jemis might bluster that it was all sorted out decades ago. I thought you should know anyway.
>
> Jullanar Maebh promised to come, saying she couldn't very well do less when I was there when she lost Roald. And of course I've written to all the children and told them. So please don't worry that I'll be alone.
>
> Lady Violet, Marchioness Noirell

When I looked up, the room was blurred. At first, I couldn't understand why, until my secretary passed me a handkerchief.

“I will be visiting the Marquis Noirell for Winterturn,” I said, brushing away the tears. There were things I needed to do. So many things I needed to do before then.

“Would you like me to write a reply to the Lady Violet?” my secretary asked, picking up her pen.

“No,” I said, slightly absently, “No, I should write it myself.” I pulled pen and paper out of the drawer. It was official paper, I should find some of my own letterhead to write on. That was in my private study, back at the house. For some reason, that seemed too far.

I scrawled out a reply under the stamped scrollwork title of the 'Lord Wizard of the Conference of Kingdoms and Nations of Alinor'. That title still irked me, thirty years after we'd come up with it. After _Jemis_ had come up with it. I'd forgotten, until that moment, it had been his name.

> Violet (and Jemis)
> Of course I'll come. Expect me no later than the first week of December. I will try and get away earlier if I can. Politics be damned.
> P. Dart

That last sentence was the kind of thing Jemis would have said. Before launching into a plan to reform the systems so that I could take as much time as I wanted, whenever I wanted. Lady and Lord, how was I—

No, I told myself firmly, as I handed the letter off to my secretary, now was not the time to fall apart. The carriage ride from Orio City to Ragnor Bella would be long and boring. I could break down then. For now, I needed to set things up to give myself six weeks of absence. Eight, if I could wrangle it.

My composure lasted until I returned from the formal dinner. That was good, I had managed to present the calm and organised front that the ambassadors needed to see. When I handed my coat to Orin, and caught a glimpse of myself in the hall mirror. It hit me like a physical blow.

Passing off my stumble with a wry smile, I headed directly up to bed. There was nobody else to consult or disturb, although I often kept late hours of my own inclination. Tonight I wanted to climb behind my bed curtains and hide. With only the household staff to judge me, I did.

No light, magical or otherwise. I tugged all the blankets and sheets into a pile, made walls of the pillows, and buried myself like I was seven.

Like _we_ were seven. So many times, at Dart Hall or Arguty, Jemis and I had done this together. Laughing, reading stories by the tiny mage light that Sir Hamish had given me. I pulled my knees up to my face, and sobbed.

Logically, I had known for years. Back when I was first learning about my magic, in that book from Fitzroy Angursell's bag. It had said great mages, especially wild mages, lived much longer. Hadn't the Lord of Ysthar lived something like ten thousand years? Although some of that was the way time had broken in the Fall. 

Then I had seen what it might _really_ mean, when Jemis and I (and Violet, and Roald, and Hal... and everyone) had seen Lord Mdang become frail. He was hundreds of years old, if the Zuni calendar was to be believed. Fitzroy had lived another thirty years. Every time I had seen him, I'd noticed him looking more and more distant. In the end, they said he went sailing to find Sky Ocean again.

I wasn't as great a mage as Fitzroy, and I thought perhaps it wouldn't be quite so different a lifespan. Perhaps, what with the Interim, and me denying my powers for so long, it would be less marked. The hall mirror had reminded me that if anything, it was moreso.

The man looking back at me appeared like he might be sixty. We were eighty-eight, Jemis and I. Even when I had seen him last, Jemis had looked the part. We'd had hard adventures, in our twenties. He'd had that awful wireweed addiction, which the Lady had cured him of. Even thought he'd never stopped running (until, perhaps, this latest illness), grey hair and wrinkles had turned to sagging skin and balding.

This was the logical conclusion. I would once again see my best friend in the nine worlds laid out. Cold, empty of spirit. I would pray to the Lady, oh, how I would pray.

But the door back would not be open a second time.
