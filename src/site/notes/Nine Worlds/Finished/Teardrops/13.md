The evening court after you had given Lord Igel such a dressing down was moderately more interesting than usual. Lord Igel arrived late, of course. He had to, since you were dressed only just in time, and Igel then had to don his own court outfit. He had chosen some of his most formal court robes, displaying the colours of his family to full effect.

You watched him circle through his usual set. He eventually approached a group of young people, which presumably included his useless son. He surely wouldn't send him packing now, in the middle of court? That seemed harsh.

And yet, a young man separated from the group, and followed Lord Igel to a corner. Well, if Lord Igel wanted to add fuel to the rumours, that was the way to do it.

There was a definite air that something had happened. Little knots of people formed, carefully trying to look nonchalantly at other little knots, and dispersed.

The older set did a dance where there was a lot of fancy footwork, that you thought was rather lost in the current court fashions for long robes. The youngest enjoyed a newer dance that seemed to involve skipping across the floor, jumping, and lifts. A whole cluster of conservative parents who would usually stop their children from such a dance, completely ignored it, too involved in their conversation.

You watched Lord Igel's son. He left his father, approached a group of young women, and asked one of them to dance. Lord Igel rejoined the high aristocrats of his own age and rank. He smiled, and bowed, and ignored the eyes watching him.

Perhaps this was not just about you. Sometimes it was hard to think it could be anything else, in this shrine (this mausoleum) of your adoration. But if you tried to imagine that you had not just had your most impressive tantrum in at least three years...

The young lord had just danced three times with the same young lady. Now he escorted her back to her group of young people, to drink champagne and flirt. There he left her, and returned to his father.

Well, that was certainly *one* way to detract from rumours of Lord Igel's disgrace. Sending his son hunting a bride.

You watched two inveterate dualists carefully avoid each other. An elderly man deliver what must have been a scathing insult to a pair of lordlings who had been getting quite intimate for a public setting. Two middle-aged women give the cut direct to a third.

There was an axis in every court session. A time before which it was acceptable to arrive, and strange to leave. A time after which you could stage a departure, but if you arrived then it would be very awkward, and you had probably best just not come.

It wasn't a precise time, but more of a feeling. The right people had arrived, been seen and seen others. Regular attendees could feel the change in the air. You, glued in place, saw it every time. 

It was always interesting to see who left first. Sometimes it was you, although you tried not to make a habit of it. Since your departure finished the evening for everyone else. Part of the point of court was to force your nobles to be polite, and keep them busy, and so to reduce duelling and other violence.

Tonight, the first departure was the young lady who had danced so many times with Lord Igel's son. With her, an impeccably dressed Lord, who was presumably her father. Perhaps he had seen what Lord Igel was up to, and was taking his daughter out of harm's way.

Half a bell later, you left, ending evening court.

The next morning, as you read Sayo Mdang's report summary, you admitted to yourself that you would need to talk with him. You had said you would address the issues from his investigation with the relevant parties. Which included him.

After breakfast, you walked into the official study. Mind set, determined to get this awkwardness over with. 

But first, the only ritual you enjoyed. You signalled for Sayo Mdang to rise, and said “Good morning, Sayo Mdang.”

“Good morning, Glorious One,” he said, and smiled, and looked you in the eyes.

You had been prepared to tell him to keep to the requirements to maintain the purification, without further explanation. But he smiled at you, and saw someone inside you, again. You owed him the choice.

“Sayo Mdang,” you said, and began pacing. You paused, and forced yourself to stop and face him. “Sayo Mdang, we are not concerned with the expense of maintaining your purification. But we are concerned that you are being treated with respect, and not being asked to do anything against your heart.”

He looked ready to deny both points, so I held up my hand to ask him to wait. I took a deep, counted, breath. “We understand that cost may be the only objection you feel you can voice, and so if you insist, we will support you. However.”

You stopped, and took another breath. You needed him to understand the danger. That the purification, his closeness to you, was a protection. The bars of this cage kept others out, as well as keeping you in.

“If you choose to abandon the purifications, that information will not stay within the Tower. It will be seen as a sign of our disfavour, particularly if the story heard is about expenses. You will be shunned, and a target for assassination.”

Sayo Mdang started to look mulish. 

“We say this as a warning, not an endorsement. We would wish that violence was not seen as such a common solution in our court.”

You looked into his eyes, and tried to convey that you despised the way your court worked. To let him know that you did whatever you thought you could to change it. That unable to trust almost anyone, your influence was limited. That you were constrained. Trapped.

In synch, you both took a careful, counted, breath.

“I understand, Glorious One,” he said, and looked down suddenly at his desk. You thought perhaps there was a glint of dampness in his eyes.

“Do you have any questions, Sayo Mdang?” you asked, trying to sound your least imperial self.

He did not look up, you could see he was trying to control deep emotions. You wished you dared to ask him what he was thinking, feeling. What he truly wanted. 

“The medication, Glorious One?” he asked, still looking at his desk.

“You have our consent, if it is deemed needful,” you said, remembering the fear in his face as you asked him what he had done. You could at least assure him *you* would not take exception. 

“But… does it help, my lord?” He finally looked up, seeking your eyes, to see you. To know if you spoke the truth, to know if it was me, or the chains, that spoke.

“I do not know,” you said, honestly, “It may stop us injuring ourselves.”

Sayo Mdang looked down at his desk once more. “Yes, Glorious One,” he said very quietly.

You paced one full loop of the room, trying to clear your head. Giving Sayo Mdang time to compose himself. Letting us both fall back into the patterns of our positions, our responsibilities. 

“Let us begin with the correspondence,” you said. Sayo Mdang pulled forward a sheaf of papers, and we resumed our usual day.
