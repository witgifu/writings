Kip admired the oboe, not daring to touch it. Even in its case, he could tell it was a quality of instrument he would never have aspired to own. If he had become a professional, and had the money to splurge on a special performance instrument at the height of his career, maybe.

It was not the sort of oboe you kept on a shelf to play occasionally to stop your skills from completely atrophying. Such an instrument should be a gift to the best player in the court, not for Kip. It was magnificent.

At the Emperor's prompting, Kip opened the built-in reed compartment. He pulled one out, feeling the shape. A professional would finish their own, but these were lovely. 

Why had the Emperor arranged such a beautiful oboe be purified for Kip? He protested the waste, but the Emperor insisted, like he had with the other purifications, that it was not a waste.

Kip thanked the Emperor for his generosity as well as he knew how. He didn't understand, didn't deserve, but he was determined to practise in case the Emperor ever did want to hear. And to save it from simply moving from one storage to another.

Then, the Emperor asked why he had not left the Tower. He had assumed that the Glorious One knew, had been told by the priest-wizards. They had been very clear to Kip that he must not leave unless told to by the Emperor.

The Emperor seemed to be asking if Kip hadn't left because he was afraid. Perhaps that is true at some deeper level (if he had left, unbidden, would that have been treason?) but he was not afraid of walking around the Palace. 

He had been here over a year, working in the Service, with extra work with the Ouranatha. It wasn't as if he would get lost, walking between the Tower and the offices or archives. The gardens and lower levels were all perfectly safe as well.

Then the Emperor clarified. He did not want Kip as a secretary. 

It had been a fun diversion, and now it was time for the next game. He would give Kip a guard escort, and present him at court. No doubt in that silk and linen mockery of Islander finery.

Kip had been here a year. Had seen what the Palace was like. What aristocratic culture was like. The instructions from the Ouranatha had been clear what would happen if he was chosen.

The past few weeks had let him hope. Let him believe that maybe the Emperor at the centre of it all was different. That *this* Emperor was different. In the letters he had dictated, in the light in his eyes, Kip had thought he had seen a man. But even a great man can only be the product of his culture.

“Of course, Glorious One,” he said, and made a bow of apology, attempting the first-degree, but realising he had probably got it wrong. “I will serve you however you desire.”

What else could he do? He could not simply sail away, as his Buru Tovo had.

He rose from his bow to see the Emperor had on his most regal face. What did it mean? Should he not have apologised? Was that against the rules of this game that Kip didn’t even know they were playing?

Kip's fear seemed to delay understanding what the Emperor was saying. Perhaps it was the incongruity, the words so unexpected from such a countenance. Slowly his mind began processing the Emperor’s words. 

The Emperor *did* want Kip to be his secretary. Nothing more! Oh, he had probably intended this also to be a gift, and now Kip had ruined it by making assumptions.

The Emperor was offering again to help Kip leave, but he couldn't let him continue. He did not want him to think Kip so ungrateful, so dismissive. Kip interrupted, “I will stay, Glorious One, as long as you will have me.”

Kip's words did not cause the Emperor to relax, he stayed perfectly tall and serene. But the light returned to his eyes. Kip did relax, slightly.

“Then it might also be helpful if you had a companion,” added the Emperor.

Kip looked at the Emperor confusedly. “A friend, Glorious One?” he asked.

“Not necessarily, although we would be pleased to hear if you had made friends. A companion would be someone of similar age and rank, who can go where you do. In your case, Sayo Mdang, they would be able to help with court etiquette. We know ourselves that it is peculiar and strange on first encounter.”

Kip wanted to bristle at this reminder that he was not very good at Astandalan etiquette. It was true, however, that having failed that part of the Service exams four times, on his fifth he only just scraped through.

A personal tutor, who knew the world intimately, and of whom he could ask questions without fear of awkwardness, seemed very helpful. He could also see the wisdom in having someone who could also go to court, or wherever else the Emperor needed him to go.

“That sounds useful, Glorious One,” he answered, unsure how one went about finding a companion. And slightly embarrassed at requiring help, and having the Emperor know that.

The Emperor nodded. “Lord Igel has a potential candidate. He will arrange a meeting, and you can let him know if the candidate is acceptable or not.”

“Thank you, Glorious One,” replied Kip, wondering what kind of person this would be.

The interview ended up happening in the small drawing room, where Kip sometimes wrote letters of an evening. Kip was told to wait there after lunch (Lord Igel careful phrased it as a request, it was not). So Kip took some notes to the desk there, to write up into draft documents.

At precisely two in the afternoon, Lord Igel opened the door. After the tiniest pause, when Lord Igel must have been making some kind of mental calculations, he made introductions.

“Sayo Mdang, may I introduce the Cavalier Conju enazo Argellian an Vilius. Lord Conju, Cliopher sayo Mdang.”

He bowed to both of them, and left. Kip wasn't sure what to do next. This was, in fact, the exact type of situation he needed help with. 

Lord Conju was on the short side, but dressed incredibly elegantly. Kip thought Conju was probably a couple of years younger than himself. He wondered why Lord Igel had picked this particular young man.

“Good afternoon, Lord Conju,” said Kip, only slightly before it became awkward. He abandoned his writing materials at the desk, and walked over to the more comfortable chairs.

“Good afternoon, Sayo Mdang,” said Lord Conju, and bowed in what Kip was sure was a specific way that contained some extra meaning. He walked over to stand next to the other chair.

They both paused again. Finally, Kip gave up trying to work out what he was supposed to do and just sat down. He didn't know, he wasn't going to work it out by staring at Lord Conju, and the whole point of this was that he was supposed to learn.

“Okay,” said Kip, “I know I got that wrong, Lord Conju, what should I have done, and why?”

Lord Conju seemed slightly taken aback, but he carefully sat down opposite Kip. “When Lord Igel introduced us, Sayo Mdang, he introduced me to you first. That means he judged you to be the higher ranked person. So, Sayo Mdang, it is up to you to set the level of formality and lead the discussion.”

Kip blinked a few times in honest surprise. “Oh,” he said, thinking it through, “I did know that, I've just never been the higher ranked person before. Um. I'm not actually sure that I *am*.”

Conju artfully raised one eyebrow. “Do you doubt the judgement of one of the leading people of one of the preeminent families amongst the Imperial Court, Sayo Mdang?”

“Oh,” said Kip again, feeling like this might be becoming somewhat of a refrain, “Um, no? But I'm pretty sure he would have said my rank was about equal to one of the pages a few days ago.”

“What are the determents of rank, Sayo Mdang?” asked Conju, relaxing slightly back into his chair.

Here Kip felt he was on stronger ground. He recited from the set exam text, “Titles, family, military rank, ordination by the Ouranatha, higher degrees granted by universities, membership of some professional organisations.”

“And?” said Conju, waving for Kip to continue.

“I suppose, marital status comes into it as well?” said Kip, uncertainly, now trying to gather information from what he had seen in the past year, “And maybe magical power?”

“Position,” said Conju, looking closely at Kip, “Is also important. And its importance is determined by the rank of the one who gave the position. What is your position, Sayo Mdang, and who gave it to you?”

“I'm the private secretary to the Lord Emperor of Astandalas, at his personal request,” said Kip, quietly.

“What does Lord Igel think your position is?” said Conju, rather perceptively.

Kip cringed, “The first concubine of the Lord of Rising Stars, chosen on the occasion of the celebrations of the tenth year of his reign. Soon to be appointed the Hands of the Emperor.”

Conju looked a trifle smug at this. “Quite,” he said, “Which puts you at least equal with an Imperial Duke.”

Kip sighed. “It's not true though, I'm not…” he waved his hand, to try to express what he could not put into words.

“You are not what, Sayo Mdang?” said Conju, testily, “Not chosen by the Lord Emperor? Not the only person in five worlds who can touch him without being burnt alive? Not the only one who dare look in his eyes without being blinded? Not his favourite? If you were only the Emperor's secretary, you would not be living *in the Imperial Apartments*. It does not matter if he takes you to his bed or not.”

Kip blushed furiously. It mattered to *him*, and he suspected it mattered to the Emperor as well. He looked daggers at Lord Conju.

Conju realised his mistake, “I mean to the court, to the people outside the Tower. It isn't their business. Which is not to say that they might not be tactless and *ask*, but you shouldn't tell them.”

Mollified slightly, Kip relaxed. “All right then. Lord Igel said you've done this before, Lord Conju? How does it work?”

Conju snorted. “There is *no* precedent for a companion to the Hands of the Emperor, Sayo Mdang.”

Kip tried to imitate Conju's nonchalant posture and raised eyebrow. “Did Lord Igel give you no instructions, then, Lord Conju?” This got a seemingly genuine small smile from Conju.

“I would spend the first half of every day on ritual purifications and training in the ways of the Tower. Then afternoons and evenings following you around, Sayo Mdang, and making sure you do not embarrass the Emperor by addressing a marchioness incorrectly, or falling drunk into the fountains.” Conju gave Kip a very sceptical look. “Have you ever been prone to climbing fountains, Sayo Mdang?”

“No, Lord Conju,” said Kip, faking taking the question seriously. He had once fallen off a boardwalk in Gorjo City while tipsy, but he didn't think that counted.

“Well then, we should probably start with conversation and dress. And… do you dance, Sayo Mdang?” asked Conju, curiously.

“My dances are *Aōteketētana*,” said Kip, now actually serious, “Yes, I can dance.” He didn't expect Lord Conju to understand what that meant, or how much it meant to Kip to say that. He said it anyway, for himself, for his pride in his home, however much it might be mocked here.

“Is dancing important where you're from, then? Well, good, you should be able to pick up the court dances quickly,” said Conju. 

This was honestly one of the better reactions Kip had heard. Most people in the Palace would have probably said that 'barbarian' dancing didn't count. Kip nodded in reply; it might actually be fun learning new dances.

“When should we start, then?” asked Kip, still uncertain exactly how this was going to work.

Conju went for the elegant raised eyebrow again. “Don't you need to ask Lord Igel or the Glorious One first, Sayo Mdang?”

Kip was mildly surprised, “The Glorious One said it was my choice, Lord Conju.”

“Oh,” said Conju, and it was his turn to look surprised, “You really are his favourite, aren't you?”
