My mother, bless her, managed to send me two attendants before I had to dress for court. I had known both women for some years, and knew they were both efficient and discrete. Of course, until I had appointed a household manager, the two from my mother would be rivals with the two from the seneschal. It could not be helped.

It took all four to dress me for court, and as I did so, I wondered what was supposed to have happened. Had Lord Neirth been trying to embarrass me, by having me be late? Was he expecting that I would simply start dressing early enough that two women would be sufficient? Surely he knew how long it took to dress the Glorious One, and couldn't seriously expect it to take substantially less time to dress me. I was wrapped in silks and dusted with gold and peppered with pearls.

The palanquin down to court was at least easier to get into in this reduced ensemble. There were fewer gawkers, perhaps, although there were still a lot. I processed down after the Glorious One, of course. To everyone's surprise, when he exited his, he must have signalled to the guards carrying mine. For rather than being taken to the general entrance, as I had expected, the journey stopped at the side door reserved for the Lord Emperor.

I stepped out, then made my obeisance. He gestured for me to rise, but I kept my eyes on his hands. This room might be exclusively for his use, but I wanted to be seen as the perfect, submissive Consort. Such a place was not for flirting, not that I had much success so far.

“My lady,” he said, in his regal prepared tones, “Please join us.”

“Thank you, Glorious One, for showing your humble servant such favour,” I said, bowing again. Obediently, I moved to stand a pace behind and to the left. We crossed the dais, to his golden throne. Below us were the throngs of the court, packed tight for this night. I did appreciate being out of the press of bodies on the floor.

Beside the Throne of Glory was another, smaller chair. It was low, and made of blackwood, but still. To grant the Consort a throne of her own, nothing similar had occurred in generations. If you had asked me, I would have expected that I might be granted a cushion or a small rug to kneel on. My years of training held, and I kept my trembles inside, but it was a hard thing.

What would I do for six hours, seated next to my lord, in full view of the court? I had no idea. Shamelessly, I held fast to the rule that the higher ranked person spoke first. So I sat, looking partly at the dancers below, but mostly intently watching for any sign from my lord.

“How do you find the view, my lady?” asked the Glorious One.

“It is like a painting come to life, such movement and colour, Glorious One,” I said. A polite nothing, to match his question. The throne room was designed to carry his voice into every corner, and so I supposed this conversation could only be incredibly public.

“And the music, my lady?”

“Glorious One, the musicians of your court are the finest in the nine worlds,” I replied. Now this did contain a genuine comment, that I thought the choice of music insipid.

“Indeed, my lady. The court composer has prepared a new _étude_ in your honour.”

“I must remember to thank him for his kindness, Glorious One. I have yet done naught to deserve such honours,” I said, hoping the piece would be tolerable. The court composer _was_ good, but he was so afraid of making anything offensive that he tended to just make things dull and derivative instead.

“My lady, surely poets and musicians from all over will be gathering even now try to do your beauty justice.”

They probably were. Not that I was, objectively, particularly beautiful. Of course, I had the second-best cosmetics, jewels, and clothes. Still, if I had been from any other family the best descriptive I could have hoped for was probably 'elegant'.

To be called beautiful by the Lord Emperor, however, was beyond my hopes. I looked up at my lord through my lashes, smiling as sweetly as I knew how. “I hope only to please you, Glorious One,” I said, softly.

He looked, perhaps, very slightly pained by my reply. I did my best not to panic. “That is to say, Illustrious One, that the opinions of poets and musicians mean very little to me; I would be happy to have only your kind regard.”

There was, I hoped, a slight softening in his face. “Thank you,” he said.

“Glorious One, is the viola not particularly fine tonight?” I ventured. To ask his direct opinion on anything while in public was considered crude; I should already have discerned it.

“Of course, my lady,” he said, not looking at me. I hoped for more, but he gave nothing, and the silence lengthened until saying anything would be to begin a new conversation.

So we sat there, in silence, for the better part or six hours. I, watching my lord; he, watching the court. Why had he shown me such favour, but not wanted to talk? Or had he only wished to separate me from any supporters in the court below? I still did not understand what he wanted from me. 

By the end, I thought I would either vomit or faint from nerves. Yet I managed to stand when he did, and departed with him. Safe and hidden in my palanquin, I gripped one of the silk cushions so tightly I thought I might break it in two.

My attendants washed me, and dressed me in a nightgown that covered almost nothing, before wrapping me in a robe that covered far more. Slippers, perfume for my hair. Finally, they presented me with the tonic, and a glass of water.

Before I could change my mind, I drank down the water and walked out past the other glass. Everyone was well enough trained not to show their surprise. It was an unspoken undercurrent.

Beside the bed, I knelt to await my lord. He could reject me, request someone else be sent to warm his bed. Or he could ignore me, or dismiss me. Only on the wedding night was I admitted first.

Not so long later, he entered from his own dressing room. I lowered my head to the floor, hoping he would welcome me, now that I was sober. Without a pause he gestured me up, and reached out a hand. Bobbing a curtsy, I took it, doing my best to appear suitably modest.

It was the first time, so far as I knew, that we had touched. His hand was warm and dry.

“Come, my lady, you must be tired,” he said, and handed me up into the bed.

Perhaps I would have been tired, if my heart hadn't been beating a drum of fear. “My lord is most kind,” I said, as he climbed in behind me. Attendants closed the curtains, and the mage lights were turned off.

I found I was still holding his hand. Would it be too forward of me to kiss it? He slipped it from mine before I had decided.

“Good night, Melissa,” he said, and rolled over to face away from me. As far as I could tell, he went to sleep.

There would be no sleep for me. Staring up at the canopy, I re-examined every detail of my behaviour over the day. All I could think was that I should have offered more conversation at court, better conversation. I ran through comments I could have made, questions I could have asked. It seemed a small thing to be punished over, which was perhaps why he had done it privately, or as privately as possible.

The guards and attendants that waited outside must have heard his words. They would all be asking the same question I was. Why did he invite me into his bed, if he only wanted to sleep? All I could do was wait and see if he woke.

Sometime later, it was hard to tell in the silence, and without windows to even see the stars or clouds, the Glorious One rolled over onto his back. He was not, in fact, asleep. Or at least, was no longer asleep.

Heart in my throat, I slid my hand along under the covers until it bumped against his. He turned his head to look at me.

“Melissa?” he whispered, “Is something wrong?”

“Only,” I whispered back, “That I do not know how to please you, my lord.”

He rolled onto his side, pushing himself up onto his elbow, “Whatever do you mean?”

I looked back at him in confusion. Was this a game, a trick, a test? “My lord husband,” I whispered, “I know my performance at court tonight was unsatisfactory.”

“You were as elegant as anyone could wish in a Consort,” he said, “I am sorry if I was preoccupied.”

The Glorious One, apologising to me? I did not understand. Should I beg that he blame me instead? But he had used the informal first-person. I chose to let it pass by instead, it must have been a slip.

“Why, my lord,” I whispered, “Do you deny yourself, if not to punish me?”

He slumped back down, closing his eyes. “I am not denying myself,” he said, sounding slightly strangled, “There is no self to deny. If you are certain you want to try, please tell me what days you are fertile, I will seek some aids to supply you with what you need.”

“There is no self to deny,” echoed in me, like a bell. He didn't want me, not because I was a failure, but because he could not want. Had something changed between when I had agreed to marry him and our wedding? For surely he would have refused me from the first, if he knew he was impotent. Or perhaps he only desired men? Did, as I had wondered earlier, think procreation only a duty.

“I have no wish to lay any burden on you, my lord,” I whispered.

“Nor I you, Melissa,” he said, now sounding very tired, “If only we could have been born to some other family.”

An echo of my own childhood wish for a brother. One who was not my lord and Emperor, not my lord and husband. With whom I could play music, and read books, and share joy. Had the Glorious One dreamed of a sister, as I had a brother? A dream that had shattered in the reality of what the Imperial Family demanded.

This god, this man, lying beside me, was not Shallyr or Eritanyr. I already had plenty of evidence of that. Not in public, not in private; not in government, not in manners. Perhaps, I could dare to ask.

“Do you *want* an heir and a Marwn, my lord?” I blurted, against all my training. On my dark skin a blush was hard to detect, but I could feel the warmth in my cheeks.

“I will never let the Ouranatha make another Marwn,” he said, full of fierce determination, “Never.”

I knew our mother had suffered, but somehow I had not thought so clearly on what it had done to him. Imprisoned, alone, at the edge of the Empire. Unnamed and forgotten, except by a mother who had taken all the love she had for him and buried it so deep she had claimed it gone. How did he feel about her? Abandoned? Was he jealous of me, who had been allowed to stay with her?

“But an heir, my lord?” I asked again. Eritanyr and Shallyr had both been raised as the heir, as had Anyoë. This Emperor was the first recalled Marwn, and the first for a long time that had not been expected to inherit. It did not seem that being born to the position of heir had a positive effect.

He tensed, very slightly. “Not for myself,” he whispered, “I could never wish this on anyone else.”

Suddenly, it crashed through me, the realisation that I could have said _no_. It had been a genuine question when he had asked me if I wanted to wed him, and I had been too afraid and uncertain. I could have been _free_, and I had been too afraid to fly.

But if I had flown, I would probably have never attended court again. Lived in a backwater somewhere, with only obnoxious letters from my great-aunt. It would have been a hard choice, but oh, it would have been a _choice_.

“I am so sorry, my lord,” I said, trying to keep my voice below the level where anyone except he would hear, “I did not think, I did not know...”

He stretched out, and took my hand, gently, “I didn't know either, before… Or I didn't understand. I accidentally touched a woman, she had tripped and unthinking I went to steady her, and burned her alive. Once I was feeling too ill to eat dinner, and the chef was tortured. And yet I try to tell the armies to pull back to our borders, and they ignore me. I could be replaced with a doll, and everyone would be relieved.”

I had done the same, hadn't I? He was the Sun on Earth to whom we all faced, but we saw him as that shining disk. Not as a man, not as a human. Not as someone who might want to help someone who had stumbled, and be horrified to watch them burn.

Could he and I take something for ourselves? Could we refuse to serve the Empire in this one way? Did I dare suggest it? Nobody with any medical knowledge could be surprised that we had failed to produce a child, and especially not a healthy one. He had been honest with me, he had been gentle, and generous, and kind.

“My lord,” I said, barely more than a breath, “Might we… pretend? It is unlikely to succeed, even if we did try.”

He looked at me, really looked at me. For a second, I was sure I had gone too far, that he would throw me out for my temerity. But then he smiled, and it was unlike any smile I had ever seen from him.

“It would be my honour,” he said, and then much, much more quietly added, “To pretend to do that, dear Melissa. How are your acting skills?”

Safe to say that a long betrothal to Shallyr had made them _very_ good.
