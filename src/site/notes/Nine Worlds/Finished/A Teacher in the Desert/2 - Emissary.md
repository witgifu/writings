I thought that if anything came from my letter, it would be an admonition that it was the duty of the Prince to sort out such issues, and I should at least ask there first. What I expected was silence. What I got, was Sayo Mdang, the Hands of the Emperor.

The desert in the centre of Jilkano was never a place I expected to see the hero of the Indrogan Estates. I had passed on my vastly out-of-date copies of the Cisven Flyer to others in the pub, in an effort to sponsor adult reading. So I wasn't even alone in my surprise, when he arrived on a day with no school lessons.

“Do you think your wife will come out for this? Even though I'm here?” I whispered to Ted, as Sayo Mdang climbed down from the camel. His underling and guard looking sweaty and tired.

“Maybe,” said Ted, staring openly, “Maybe.”

Suddenly I realised there wasn't anywhere in the village for guests to stay. “Oh no,” I said, “Do you have any spare blankets, Ted? I can sleep in the store room, and they can have upstairs.”

I felt terrible. Here was the Hands of the Emperor, in fine linen robes, surely used to all the luxuries of Solaara. All I had to offer him was my little bedroom and parlour.

Ted nodded. “I've got some stuff in storage from when we were building. Seemed a shame to throw it away, even if nobody needed it. You say hello, and I'll pull it out.”

“Thank you,” I said, as he disappeared inside.

I turned back to the caravan, and took a deep breath. Then I strode forwards, and bowed deeply to the man who had made my education free and my life possible. “Welcome to Loe, Sayo Mdang,” I said, trying not to blurt anything entirely stupid, “I had no idea my little letter would ever reach so high, much less that anyone might come all the way out here. Thank you.”

Sayo Mdang smiled, in a way that invited you in to share his joy. “His Radiancy is interested in the welfare of everyone on Zunidh, I am honoured to be able to help,” he said, and then he bowed to _me_.

I admit I might have stood there looking like I had been hit on the head. Sayo Mdang was kind enough not to mention it, when I finally gathered my wits again. “Please,” I said, “Come into the cool of the shade.” I opened the door to the schoolroom, and the three of them followed me.

“I'm afraid all the accommodations we have are somewhat rustic,” I pleaded, as they came in, with the guard carrying the bags.

“Is this the school?” asked Sayo Mdang, looking around, “I hope we will not interrupt anyone's learning?”

“Yes it is, sir,” I said, “I am the schoolmistress. Today is a day off, so do not worry that there is nobody here. Please, follow me upstairs.”

They nodded, and smiled, and followed me up the stairs. “I'm afraid that since we received no news of your plans, I will need to prepare the bedroom,” I said, trying my best to imitate the way of speaking of my upper-class colleagues from Cisven. “But please make yourselves at home in the parlour. The building across the way will be able to supply meals. I apologise again in advance, our fare is very basic, as everything comes in on the caravans.”

Sayo Mdang smiled that infectious smile at me, and said, “Not at all, not at all, schoolmistress. This looks lovely, thank you. I assure you, I have lived in far more strained circumstances than this for many years.”

I bowed again, and hurried into the bedroom. Thankfully, I did have two sets of sheets, if only due to the accident of not knowing they would be provided and bringing my own. I stripped the bed, and remade it. It was really only meant for two, so I hoped the guard would not mind sleeping in the parlour. Then I shoved as much of my things as possible into either a bag, if I would need it soon, or the trunk in the corner, if I would not.

Blushing furiously, I wrapped the bag in the used sheets. Only stopping briefly in the washroom to grab my brush and wash cloth, I carried it downstairs. I opened the door to the store room, and dumped the bundle just inside. Then I dashed into the kitchen to make drinks. I had a very little coffee left, but it was quite poor quality, and I would have been embarrassed to serve it. Instead, I embraced that were in Jilkano, and prepared barley tea.

I had to run across and ask Ted to borrow another cup, since I only had two. When you live alone, and everything has to come by caravan, keeping spares in case of guests is hard to justify. Thankfully, the pub had plenty of cups. He also gave me a big bundle of padding, that he assured me rolled out into a bed.

Nervously, I returned upstairs with the tea. “The bedroom is ready, my apologies again for the delay,” I said, putting out the tea things. Looking up, I saw a quickly suppressed look of surprise on the face of Sayo Mdang's assistant. “Oh,” I said, looking back down at the teapot, “I am sorry, this is not true tea from Ysthar. I am sure none has reached here since the Fall. It is only barley tea I'm afraid.”

I didn't look up to see the reaction. In some places I knew barley was considered peasant fare, and barley tea was presumably unheard of. Since arriving in Jilkano I had acquired a taste for it, which was handy as I hadn't been able to get the caravan to bring coffee often. But I had nothing better to offer, and so I offered what I had.

“Only three cups?” said Sayo Mdang, “Won't you be joining us, schoolmistress?”

“I thought you might want to rest from your journey, sir,” I said, keeping my hands behind my back so he couldn't see me picking at my nails with nerves.

“We have come in response to your letter, I would like nothing better than to get to know you,” said Sayo Mdang, gesturing at the chairs, “Please, find another cup if you would like, and join us.”

I nodded, and dashed back down and across to Ted to borrow another cup. He lent me two, claiming it was in case I broke the first one from anxiety.

Sayo Mdang was incredibly kind. He asked about my students, where they came from, and how they were to teach. He asked about me, and my education, and why I had chosen to come all the way out here to work. I couldn't believe such an important man could be interested in these things, but he seemed to be genuine.

After the tea was finished, Sayo Mdang asked where and when was best to meet people. I explained about the pub, and how the days here usually worked. Then he said, “And do you know how I might be introduced to the desert peoples?”

“Aioru and his… actually, I never asked if they were related. His friends, anyway,” (I was babbling, that was nerves again) “They will come to school tomorrow. He will know the best way to get in touch with his people. He's been translating for his elders, you know, he's really fluent now, you wouldn't know he had not a word of Shaian when I came.” I stopped myself, and took a deep breath.

Another disarming smile from Sayo Mdang, “I look forward to meeting the young emissary.”

I cleaned away the tea things, and managed to escape. The guard and the assistant said nothing, but all of them had very politely drunk the tea.

---

That evening, Sayo Mdang and his colleagues were the centre of attention at the pub. Even if they had only come from the coast, they would have been a nine-days-wonder. But they had come all the way from Solaara to listen to the miners. The people of Loe had never felt so important, so connected to the world.

Miners talked proudly of their homes, built of the stone they had dug. Of their children, and the hopes they had for their future. Of work in the mine itself, and the dangers it posed. Sayo Mdang listened, and asked questions that were kind but clever. Even those children who were there got to answer questions, and ask them in turn.

Had Sayo Mdang sailed over on a boat? No, he had taken the Sea Train, but he had sailed from Western Dair to Jilkano before the Sea Train was built. Did he live in a glorious palace in Solaara? Well, yes, the Palace of Stars was magnificent, but he lived in two rooms right on the edge, probably smaller than their homes here. Did Sayo Mdang have any children? No, but he did have a big family, back home in the Vangaveye-ve.

I, needing to be up before dawn the next day, did not stay to hear everything that was said. Waving goodnight to Ted, I slipped out and back to the schoolhouse. I washed in the kitchen, and then took my small mage light to the storeroom. As promised, unbuckling the straps from the bundle Ted had given me, it unrolled into a sort of mattress. Without much regard for the organisation system of the space, I moved things around until I had enough room to lay the bed out all the way. Then I stripped down to my under-things, and crawled inside.

After such an intense day, I managed to fall asleep quite quickly. I didn't notice Sayo Mdang or anyone else return.

---

Thankfully my body was trained to wake up after a certain amount of sleep, rather than with the morning light, as the cupboard didn't have a window. I pieced myself together by the mage light, and then went into the kitchen to make some porridge.

To my surprise, I caught Sayo Mdang there.

“I'm terribly sorry sir, I did not know you would rise so early. The pub doesn't open until a bit later. I could make you some porridge if you like?” I said, feeling awkward.

“I was just going to make some coffee,” he said, holding up a filter paper, “If you would like some? And if you could help me work the stove?”

I smiled. “You have to push the knob in, slide it had to the left until it clicks, and then slide it right. I've never seen another like it, it took me a week to work it out.”

“A week of cold breakfasts?” said Sayo Mdang, “Oh dear.” He operated the stove controls, and got it going.

I put on another pot on for my porridge, which was just ground oats, a little dried fruit, and water. I got out sugar, salt, and the tin of condensed milk. Some of the miners had goats for fresh milk, but goats were too smart for me.

Somehow a slightly sleepy Sayo Mdang seemed less intimidating, as I stirred my breakfast. He did make me a cup of coffee, although I hadn't asked for one. Pushing the cup towards me, he sat down at the little kitchen table, and then put his nose in his cup like he could inhale it. He relaxed noticeably after the first sip.

I joined him with my bowl. “There's more in the pot, if you want some, sir. Or the pub will have flatbread later,” I said. I didn't expect him to be interested in porridge, but I had made extra anyway.

I took a small sip of the coffee to decide if it would need milk. As soon as the aroma and taste hit, I groaned involuntarily. I hadn't had coffee this good since I'd left Cisven. It tasted like the tiny café next to my student housing. Unreasonably amazing. It did not, at all, need condensed milk to hide the flavour.

Sayo Mdang smiled at me. “Oh my stars,” I said, “It's been years since I've had coffee this good. Where are the beans from?”

“The Vangaveye-ve,” he replied, with clear pride.

I savoured the coffee between spoonfuls of porridge, happy in a little bubble of flavour.

After he had finished his coffee, Sayo Mdang had another cup, and then did take me up on the offer or porridge. Unfortunately, I couldn't just sit there all morning.

“Just throw any dishes in the sink,” I said, as I did so with mine and the empty pot, “I'll clean them at lunch.” Then I went outside to ring the bell.

The students filed into the schoolroom, and Sayo Mdang came out from the kitchen to see them. A few of them stared, and one of the braver ones waved. Aioru and the other locals looked suspicious.

“Good morning,” I said, and we did the usual blessing. Sayo Mdang joined us, with a twinkle in his eye. Perhaps the prayer to the Sun on Earth felt a little different when you saw him every day.

“Would you like to introduce yourself, Sayo Mdang? I'm sure everyone is excited to meet someone new,” I said, putting him on the spot.

He grinned, and walked up to the front to stand next to me. “Good morning,” he said, “I'm Cliopher Mdang, and I work in the Imperial Bureaucratic Service. Does anyone know what that is?”

A couple of hands shot up from the older kids. He then proceeded to teach the best civics class I'd ever heard, somehow managing to bring in things appropriate to every age group. Even I laughed at the story of the Shark Queen.

When he was finished, he took the local children aside, while I assigned the scheduled work to everyone else. Sayo Mdang's companions came down, and then the three of them went across to the pub.

At the end of the morning session, the children were usually itching to be released. Today, Aioru hung back. “Schoolmistress?” he asked, waiting by his desk.

“Yes, Aioru?” I said with a smile.

“Do you trust Sayo Mdang?” he asked, “Did you ask him to come?”

“I asked someone to come, yes. I must admit I never thought someone as important as Sayo Mdang would come. As to your first question, that's complicated,” I said.

Aioru sighed, but he was smiling. “You always say that!”

“Well, it's often true,” I retorted, “I trusted Sayo Mdang not to poison me and took the drink he made me this morning. I trusted him to be kind when he was talking to you. But I only know him by public reputation. I don't know if I would trust him with my greatest secret, or a lot of money. Also, just because you know you can't trust someone doesn't mean you don't like them. The smallest children can't be trusted with ink, but that's fine.”

Aioru thought about this, and nodded. “I might not be back for the afternoon,” he said, with a sigh, “He wants to talk to the elders, and they'll need me to translate.”

“Thank you, Aioru,” I said, “Without you, I don't know what we'd do.”

He rolled his eyes at me. “Probably way more boring maths classes,” he said with a snicker. Then he went across to the pub to find Sayo Mdang.

Indeed, he was gone that afternoon, and Sayo Mdang and his colleagues were gone all evening, only returning very late. The next day Sayo Mdang visited the mine, and we managed to have a fairly normal school day. In the evening, they came to the pub for supper and talked some more.

As Sayo Mdang made coffee the next morning he asked me, “Would it be possible to talk to the children today?”

“Of course, sir,” I said automatically. Then after a beat I asked, “Did you want to talk to them all together again?”

“Smaller groups, if that would be possible, schoolmistress?” he said, as I stirred the porridge.

“Oh, that would probably be easier, sir. They usual work in small groups by age,” I said, then added, “What did you want to talk to them about?”

“The same thing as everyone else. Their lives here, what they value, their dreams,” he said, with a smile.

I nodded, not really understanding. What would the children know about mining problems, or local gods? If they knew anything, it would just be copying what their adults said. But then I thought maybe there was something in that, perhaps the children would be willing to say what the adults said behind closed doors, but wouldn't say to an important person from the capital.

“Who would you like to talk to first, sir?” I said, as I sat down opposite him.

“Whoever would be most convenient,” he replied with a laugh, “I'm sure I'm disrupting you and your students enough.”

After talking to the children in the morning, he spent another afternoon and evening with the locals. Another morning with the miners. Six days of going back and forth, talking with different people, at different times and places. He must have talked to every person in Loe, and every local, at least once if not twice.

At the next day off, he gathered together both groups and the shrine, and made a proposal. I didn't really understand all of it, the details of how the mine actually worked were not of particular interest to me. My general impression, however, was that the locals were getting nearly everything they wanted (with the notable exception of the end of Loe). The miners were getting a few things they wanted, such as new places they could dump waste. The main concession he proposed for the miners was that they should be paid a daily wage by the Prince, rather than by the amount of gold they extracted. Apparently, under the labour laws, that should have been happening already.

The entire time, Aioru was translating for his people. He was incredible, and I could see how much his people valued him and his skills.

When everyone was in agreement, there was a bit of a party. And then the next day, the caravan returned, and Sayo Mdang left.
