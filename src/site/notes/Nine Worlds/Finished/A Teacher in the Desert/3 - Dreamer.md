Perhaps a month later, I was sitting in the kitchen after morning session. I had finished my lunch, and was just staring out the window. It was time to start thinking about what I would do next, where I would go next. Would I return to Cisven? Or to another market town in Western Dair, perhaps? Maybe the Vangaveye-ve needed teachers? I could survive living almost anywhere with such wonderful coffee.

Aioru knocked on the door frame.

“Come in,” I said, warmly, “Didn't want to go home and nap?”

He shook his head. “I've been thinking, and it's too hard to sleep.”

I nodded in sympathy, “I know the problem. What's on your mind?”

Aioru focused all his fierce courage on me, and said, “How do you join the Imperial Bureaucratic Service?”

I frowned, trying to remember. I thought I heard one of my fellow students at the Teachers College mention a sibling who had applied.

“Don't say I'm too young, or I don't know enough, or they'll never let in a desert tribesman!” said Aioru, angrily.

“I wasn't going to!” I protested, “I was just trying to remember, I think there's an exam you have to sit, but I'm not sure how, or where, or even what's in it. I'll write to some of my friends, and find out.”

He looked at me suspiciously.

“I'm not trying to put you off,” I promised, “I will find out, and as soon as I know, I'll do everything I can to help you apply.”

Aioru walked over to the table, and sat down with a big sigh. “The elders don't understand why I'd want to leave,” he said softly, “And the village kids, they're nice about it, but they said they didn't think they'd be allowed to join so why should I be.”

“I'll get my stationary now, and write to my old lecturer at the Teachers College. If anyone knows, they will,” I said, “I had to beg to get my parents to let me go to Teachers College, you know. They thought I should work in a factory for a few years, save up, and marry a tradesman and run his shop. But I wanted to see the world. What do you want to do in the Service?”

“I want to be like Sayo Mdang,” said Aioru, not looking at me, “I want to solve hard problems, and bring people together, and help them.”

“That,” I said, “Sounds like an excellent reason.” I smiled at him, and then I did exactly as I said I would. I got my writing things, and wrote to everyone I could think of for information on joining the Service.

---

The next mail delivery after my letters went out didn't have any answers. When Aioru looked a little disappointed, I set him an algebra exercise of calculating how long a letter from Loe would take to get to Cisven, and a reply to make it back. He agreed it would be tight, relying on the mail sorting at the Lights being very efficient. I then gave him a sample of my mail, so he could see how his estimate compared to what I had experienced. The look on his face when he found out it once took three months for a reply to reach me was precious.

“When I join the Service, I'm going to visit the central post office in Jilkano-Lozoi, and fix it,” he said.

“I believe you,” I replied, with a smile.

Two months after my letters had left Loe, I was inundated with responses. I had been sent _two_ complete sets of the set books for the exams. One a very worn set that my friend swore had been her great-uncle's. The other a fresh set, that my lecturer had got from a government office in Cisven. There was also reams of advice. Anyone who had even heard a rumour of a neighbour or relation who had taken the exam or joined the Service sent me everything they could gather. I would certainly owe many people long thank-you letters!

I invited Aioru to come and look at the books with me one afternoon session. He immediately picked up the first volume of the old set, opening it up and looking down the contents page. “Classical literature? Poetry? What does this have to do with anything?” he said, running a finger down the list.

The volume on mathematics lay open in front of me, and I was giving a slightly tense look at the words 'advanced calculus' and 'linear algebra'. The books, I had to hope, were very thorough, or I would be hard-pressed to teach this content. It was the sort of thing I had only just scraped by at to get into Teachers College.

“This isn't going to be something you'll learn in a couple of months,” I said, looking over the rest of the books, “It's going to take years. You'll probably be at least fifteen by the time you're ready to sit the exam.”

Another five years. I could give Aioru the books, the addresses of people who had offered advice. Return to Cisven, or anywhere really. Teachers were needed all over, that's part of why I'd wanted this career. Staying for five years in one place had been about saving up, so I could pick where I went next.

When I looked at Aioru, valiantly trying to read and understand the start of “History of Literary forms in Shaian,” I knew where I was going next.

---

I wrote to the Lozoi Department of Education, requesting another five-year contract. It was possibly the first time it had ever happened, as the response seemed to imply that I could write back if I required any help escaping a possible 'unwanted relationship entanglement'. I replied, thanking them, and saying I wanted to see my youngest students through to graduation.

Surely some of the adult miners knew I was on a five-year contract. There had been teachers before, who each presumably stayed the five years and then moved on. But I didn't tell anyone I was staying. After all, what was there to say? It was just a continuation of normal.

The set books were on my mind all the time. It was reasonably clear to me that they expected someone who sat the exams to have undergone further education after school. Perhaps only a two-year course in bookkeeping, or secretarial work, but to be tackling this work when most of it was already familiar. It was possible that if very rich or even noble children wanted to get in, they could find special tutors who prepared people for the exam. Out here in the Hutabarrah, Aioru only had me.

Thankfully the newer books were well-organised and came with everything we needed. I did notice that the older books assumed you would have copies of the epic poems already, and was thankful they had been added to the newer editions. There was so much to cover, did I have time to tutor Aioru on all this in addition to teaching all the other students? It wouldn't be right to focus just on Airou; even children who would leave school at fourteen-and-a-half to enter the mine were owed a full education. There were many students I could see who had a spark of interest in something; even if they couldn't or wouldn't make a career out of it, I wanted to foster their joy as well.

So I pulled out my syllabus from the Lozoi Department of Education, and compared notes. Unsurprisingly, thankfully, the mathematics and science material lined up well. The books went further, but that was fine. It would be good for Aioru to stretch himself, and for the other children to have the chance to try as well. For literature, I juggled the texts and topics around to match, like a sort of puzzle. 

History and Geography were understandably focused on Zunidh and Jilkano in particular in the Lozoi guidelines. The older set books were Astandalan Imperial history and geography, the newer ones were that with a bit appended about the Fall and it's aftermath. That last chapter of the set book was several key topics in the Lozoi books. I sighed. Perhaps we could do some comparative history between Jilkano and the Empire? The set books even managed to brush over the role Sayo Mdang played in the Littleridge Treaty, presumably whatever noble organised them had a grudge against the commoner.

Curiously there was nothing about magic or religion, I had rather expected a lot about the Imperial Cult. Perhaps it wasn't as strong in Solaara itself. Even our little village had a shrine with a picture of the Emperor in it. Instead, there was a strange book on etiquette. The new edition was about twice the size of the old one, with a lot more detailed explanations, but it was still baffling. Why were there so _many_ types of bow? And so many types of _fork_? The old book had a section on orgies, which was… educational… but could probably wait a couple of years. The exclusion of that topic in the newer book made me hope that they had fallen from favour.

Over many, many naptimes and evenings I worked on breaking down everything Aioru would need to learn. Starting with what we could do first, I embedded it into the regular lessons. But then sketching out the months and years into the future.

One evening, when I was pouring over notes while eating dinner, Ted came and sat down beside me.

“The caravan should arrive tomorrow,” he said, “Are you all packed?”

“Packed?” I said, my mouth half-full of stew and my mind full of the _Deeds of Olor_.

“To go home. Or, wherever you're going,” he said, with a small smile.

“I'm not going,” I said, swallowing, and probably blushing.

“Oh, are you leaving next month? I thought you had come at the end of winter, perhaps I mis-remembered,” he said, looking confused.

“No,” I said, “You're right about when I came. But I signed another contract, I'm here for five more years.”

Ted looked at me like I had grown an extra head. “Why would you do that?” he asked, “You've got no family here; many of the mine families try and convince their kids to take apprenticeships in the city. Surely you can do better? You're a good teacher.”

“Thank you,” I said with a smile, “I'm glad you think so. The Lozoi Department of Education was worried I'd been trapped in an abusive relationship.”

Ted snorted. “Not unless you count your relationship with your work.”

I laughed back, and flipped the book closed. “I'm staying for the children, Ted. I don't want them to have a just-qualified teacher who does the bare minimum to pass inspection. I want to see more of them graduate; go to the coast and get apprenticeships, and then come back and make Loe a place that's about more than the Prince's gold. And the Hutabarrah seen as a beautiful place in its own right.”

With a piercing gaze, Ted said, “And you think Aioru's part of that?”

“I'm sure he is,” I said.

Ted nodded, and walked back behind the bar. I watched him go around to the taps, and start pulling extra jacks out from behind the bar. Then he banged his fist on the counter.

“Hey ho!” yelled Ted, and everyone else went silent, carefully putting down spoons and drinks. “You all know our Schoolmistress what been here five years. Well, and usually she'd be off about now. But she tells me she's signed on for another five years, 'cause she likes the kids so much, or some such nonsense. So, here's to her, and all the kids. A round on the Prince!”

There were some claps, and some cheers, and someone brought me a jack of small beer too. Plus many 'thanks' and 'what a good lass'. And not at all subtle looks from me to Ted and back, with dramatic winks.

---

Later that night, I was sitting outside, enjoying the cool night breeze. I saw, like a mirror of five years ago, a local woman standing beside the shrine. Smiling, I got up this time and walked over to her.

Beside her were Aioru, and the other woman. “Hello,” I said, and bowed to the Emperor's portrait.

“The miners say you aren't going home?” said the woman, who I now knew must be one of the local elders.

“Not for another five years,” I said, and both women nodded. Aioru looked surprised.

“Well,” I said to him, “At least five years. We'll see how long it takes you to get into the Service.”

“You think he can?” said the elder.

“Yes,” I said, “For the same reason you sent him to school in the first place, I imagine. And maybe one of the other local children might go to Teachers College, and you could have a school in your own language.”

They looked at me thoughtfully, but Aioru didn't translate, so I assumed they understood.

“My name is Odierne,” I said, “But everyone just calls me Schoolmistress, I don't mind.”

“You can call me Aude,” said the woman, then indicated to the other, “And this is Lotem.”

I bowed to both of them. “Thank you, Aude and Lotem, for letting me teach your children.”

“As if they would let us stop them!” said Aude, and we both smiled.
