He walked down towards the harbour, familiar paths, pavers warm through his sandals. “Hey Kip!” called various people, old friends, family. He nodded, and smiled, and kept walking. It could have been yesterday, or last week, or any day before he had left for Astandalas. The city here hadn't changed. Only he had.

He felt like he was watching himself, walking beside himself. Judging himself. Too much, he was always too much. Too ambitious, too different, too loud, too angry, until he came back. Now everyone asked why he was quiet, too quiet. But what could he say? He had tried to explain about the Fall, about the places outside the Ring that were ravaged by famine and natural disasters.

He was in old clothes, from his uni days. That wasn't who he was, not anymore. Ghilly had made that *quite* clear. 

But the grass skirt he'd arrived in had also been wrong, Vinyë had chided him and sent him to dress. “You're not the wild boy Mama sent to Uncle Tovo,” she had said. And not wanting to remind his mother of her grief for his father while her own mother lay dying, he scurried off to change.

He had nothing else to wear, nowhere else to be, no one else to be.

“Hi Kip!”

Smile, nod. Keep walking, look busy.

Before he was really aware, he had made it to the harbour. Should he keep going around the harbour edge, and walk out to the lighthouse? (And would he turn around at the end, to walk back?)

Now that he had stopped, someone caught him. 

“Sayo Mdang!”

He turned. Nobody called him that here. That was a name from another place (another place he didn't belong, another place he'd abandoned). It was a *velio*.

She had a job for him, helping navigate their ship through the storms. He tried to explain how he didn't really know, that all he'd done was survive (and for what?). She didn't listen, of course. He took the job, if nothing else, he did know how to sail.

By the warehouses, he spotted an aunt. He waved to her, and she waved back, as he boarded the *velio* ship. He had nothing to pack, nothing to take, nobody he could face explaining why he was leaving again.

It was easier on the ship. Follow orders, lie in his hammock and pretend to sleep, eat. Days and nights, storms and hot sun, blended together.

If there was extra work, risky work, he would be first to join. He didn't talk, he didn't sing. That was okay. Nobody else on the ship knew him. He could be 'crazy Mdang' here.

It was a bad typhoon. He had seen it, and warned the helm. That was what he was here for. They were doing their best to skirt the edge, but still rain and waves lashed the decks. Everyone was tied to railings and posts, as the ship rolled precipitously. He held to his post by the mainsail.

And then beside him, the stern lantern flickered once. And went out.

It was impossible to see if it had broken, or somehow the wind or water had put it out. Every sailor was making a sign to ward off evil. There was only bad luck from such an event. But there was no more than a moment to spare, as they held the ship over the next swell.

Someone came to relieve him. In the storm it was hard to tell time, moments were hours and hours were moments. He made his way back to the hatch.

But there waited the quartermaster. She handed him a lit storm lantern, and pointed to the stern. Talking, in this weather, was pointless. He nodded, and turned back.

Hand over hand, he pulled himself along the railing. Starboard side, up the stairs, along to the stern. Stupidly, the lantern couldn't be reached without going over the railing. He tied himself carefully, before vaulting over. Then he shimmied along the iron bar that led to the lantern. 

Right next to it now, he could see the glass in the lantern had shattered. Careful of sharp edges, he opened it. Then he tied the storm lantern inside the shell. If they made it to a port, it could be repaired properly.

He was given an extra ration of rum after. But the true reward, he thought, was that he was so exhausted he slept without dreaming.

Some unmeasurable amount of time later, they arrived back in port. The captain told him to write a report about what he had done, that the Palace was interested in any word of the Vangaveye-ve. He didn't have the heart to tell her that had just been him. Nobody else was interested in knowing anything more than that it hadn't sunk into the sea.

In a pub where the floor stuck to his feet, he drank cheep rum. It was hard to die, he thought, as he admired the graffiti, when you had so many survival skills. He could swim, he could forage and fish, find water, even make his own clothes.

And he had. He survived all the way home, with it shining as a beacon in his heart. He could still feel the thread, the pull of his island. But he turned away from it. He didn't belong there now.

He thought Ani would claim him on the *velio* ship. Every chance he could, he had given her. But she didn't want him either.

Maybe he would go for a walk in the fens. He finished his rum, and left the pub. It was not the kind of place where anyone talked.

In the evening light, the fens were surprisingly beautiful. They smelled like damp rot, but after months spent in ship's quarters it was at least not the smell of under-washed sailors.

He wandered along the first path he found. As the twilight turned to true dark, little lights blinked on. Some stayed steady. Some wavered like lanterns held in walker's hands.

It was a dance, he thought. The music provided by insects, buzzing. They bit him mercilessly, and he scratched absently at the bites as he walked.

At some point after the insects slept, the fire of the rum ran out. He kept walking, until his legs, more used to climbing than walking, gave out. Then he curled up on the path where he fell, and slept.

Dawn came again. He woke, and got up, and walked. He wondered vaguely how time ran in the fens. When the path forked, he made a choice seemingly at random.

His body remembered walking. Remembered the long trek he had made overland. He tried to keep his mind away from the same memories, but they crowded in. And what good had it done him? None. He had been useless and unwanted every step there, and when he arrived home.

The sun reached its zenith, and the smell and humidity of the fens was becoming oppressive. Then the path he had been following abruptly left the fens.

He stopped at the edge, and looked around. The path continued up the ridge, although path was perhaps a strong word. It more resembled a wild animal trail. In every other direction all he could see was the fens, fading into a humid haze of heat.

“Is this where you stop?” asked the voice of his Baru Tovo in his head. He wanted to say yes, he wanted to walk off the path into the sucking mud and be swallowed up. Perhaps then he could be useful.

He stumbled up the track instead. Numbly, he knew his body would need fuel if he continued like this. Perhaps there would be clean water, at least, at the top.

At the top was grass. From it emerged a line of mounds, running off into the distance. Each with a stone door set in, to face the rising sun. He continued on the track, until it petered out when he long grass ended. Around the mounds, the grass was kept in check. Someone must be doing that, to make such neat edges.

He went from one mound to the next, not really looking, not really thinking. Just going on, as he had always done. East, always east.

At the last mound he stopped. Where were the mound-keepers? All he could hear were insects. He looked around.

In the distance, incongruously, was the Palace of Stars. As it had fallen, on the edge of the town of Solaara. He sighed, and continued what he now realised was the walk back to his old room.
