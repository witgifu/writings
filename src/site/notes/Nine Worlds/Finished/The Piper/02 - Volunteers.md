The student union building was packed. Toucan had got himself a spot near the secretary, and Kip wormed his way to join them. There was a lot of angry grumbling, and it sounded to Kip like some people were in the mood for throwing stones and defacing property.

Toucan spotted Kip, and grabbed his arm. “It was this guy's idea to evacuate the high-school,” said Toucan, pulling him into the circle, “I just delivered the message.”

The secretary, a distant cousin of Bertie's, nicknamed Ginger, nodded.

“The elders don't want anyone volunteering,” said Kip, trying to keep his voice down. He wanted Ginger to hear, but not the rowdier groups. “I think they just want to hide the teens and hope the Navy sails away when they can't find them.”

Ginger rolled their eyes, “I know you two respect them,” they started.

Toucan interrupted. “I respect them, but this time they're wrong.” Kip nodded in agreement. Maybe, maybe, if Buru Tovo had told him not to, he would have listened. But Tovo wasn't here, and Kip wasn't sure he'd say not to go either.

“You remember the full list of criteria, Kip?” asked Toucan, knowing the answer already.

“Of course,” said Kip, grinning back at him.

“Excellent!” said Ginger, “Let's do this.” They turned around, and climbed up onto a table. 

“Hey!” they yelled, and slowly the chatter died down. “If nobody qualifies, this whole discussion is moot. Kip Mdang knows the full list of criteria. He's going to call them out. If you don't qualify, sit down. We'll see how many are left.”

Reluctantly, pushed by Toucan, Kip climbed up next to Ginger. They clapped him on the back, and jumped down.

“Right,” said Kip, “This list is pretty discriminatory, and they used a bunch of words I know a lot of us will find offensive, but I'm just going to recite it, okay?”

“You can all write angry letters in the student paper tomorrow,” called out Ginger, and there was a smattering of laughter.

Kip started reciting the list in order. There *were* a lot of angry faces, and commiserating shoulder slaps. The grade requirement cut out a lot of people, and when he got to the musical skills there were some embarrassed shrugs and laughs.

At the end, he looked around the room, and there were just three of them left. Him, a slightly terrified looking man, and a furious looking woman. Kip stepped down off the table, and Ginger climbed back up.

“Can you two come up? Then we can discuss what happens next,” said Ginger, waving at the other two. They both climbed over all the various people in between, with people randomly cheering and clapping them.

“I knew you played oboe,” said Toucan, “I didn't know you sang.”

Kip shrugged, “I'm not sure if I'm good enough for them, but I'm okay. Not as good as Mama, say.”

The other two made it over, and Ginger crouched down to talk to them. “Do any of you want to go?” they asked, gently.

“I'd rather go than send all the teenagers, or try to hide them,” said Kip. The woman nodded enthusiastically, the man reluctantly.

“I know,” said Ginger, “But I don't want any of you feeling like you have to give up your education or family.”

Kip looked at the other two, “I'll be there tomorrow, but I won't judge you if you aren't.”

 “I'll be there too,” said the woman, looking ready to fight anyone who told her otherwise, “My brother is sixteen.”

They all winced. “I'm sorry,” said the man, “I'm… my parents are in the Isolates, I'm the first in the family to come to uni… I don't….”

Kip and Ginger both clapped him on the shoulder from opposite sides. “Not everyone has to do this,” said Ginger.

“There's more than one kind of courage,” said Kip, “And admitting when you can't do something is one of them.” Toucan snorted from behind Kip, who chose to ignore it.

The young man looked relieved. “Thanks,” he said, “To all of you.”

Ginger stood back up, and the chatter that had picked up died away again. “We have two volunteers,” said Ginger, and a cheer went up from the crowd. They raised a hand for silence again, “But we need to be pragmatic, the Admiral could still turn them down. We need to co-ordinate with the elders, teachers, and teens to make it as hard as possible for them to get anyone else.”

There was a general rumble of approval. “Every club secretary, I want you to gather your members and think about what you can do. Donate supplies, make food, visit parents. Start finding a space for yourselves. I know lots of you are in multiple clubs,” there was a small round of laughter, “Just pick one, okay. The union committee will be here until we find out what happens tomorrow.”

Toucan smiled at Kip, and went to gather the Traditional Culture Club. They would probably be good at talking with the elders. Kip wasn't sure he wanted to do that right now, he suspected he knew what they'd tell him. It wasn't what he wanted to hear.

Nor did Kip want to go home and tell his mother about what he was going to do. Or his sisters. Neither, by the looks of it, did the angry woman. “Kip,” he said, offering his arms to her.

“Nieleni,” she said, and grasped his forearms, “my island is Linaroa.”

“Mine is Loaloa,” Kip added, with a smile, “Yes, another Mdang.”

Nieleni smiled, “Well, I guess we're in competition now.”

“Is it a competition you want to win?” asked Kip, “I'm not sure that I do.”

“Not really,” said Nieleni, “I would actually rather take my statistics exam, whatever I told my friends.”

Kip smiled. “Same,” he said, “Want to get a drink and try to pretend we're doing anything else tomorrow?”

“Are you propositioning me for a one-night-stand?” asked Nieleni, laughing.

Kip bit his lip, “No,” he said, “I actually just want a big glass of rum and to hide from my family.”

“*That*, I can work with,” she said with a smile.

Bertie and Cora came and found him, as soon as they came out of their Anthropology class. 

“Do we have to lock you up, Kip, to stop you being such an idiot?” snarled Bertie, already clearly angry.

Kip held up his hands, “Oh, and you'd let them take the teens instead?”

Cora snorted, “They're just posturing, Kip, there's no way—”

This was what Kip had expected from Uncle Lazo and his Mama, not his friends. “There are two more ships outside the Ring,” said Kip, bitterly, “And the zamà sent their apprentices away, they can feel there are strong mages.”

Bertie still looked irate. “There's got to be another way,” he said, “Rather than just throwing your life away.”

“You've both done Astandalan History, how well has rebellion gone since Aurelius Magnus?” countered Kip, “How many provinces have seceded? It's an Imperial Proclamation, if we ignored it, we'd be crushed.” He tried to emphasise the point by swatting at a mosquito, but missed.

“Kip,” said Cora gently, “Nobody's talking about rebellion, just maybe trying to negotiate. Perhaps a bigger pearl tithe.”

“Negotiate with who?” said Kip, “The Poz? How many more days until his liver gives out? He wasn't even at the announcement. With the Admiral?”

They both shook their heads, as if he was clearly being unreasonable. Kip stared at them. How could they not see? “Would you petition the Sun-on-Earth himself, then?” he asked, “Or will you build a fleet of parahë tonight and leave the Empire?”

“You're not listening, Kip,” said Bertie.

At those words, Kip shut his mouth in a tight line, and gestured for them to continue. He had thought his friends would understand, but instead they had cut right to the quick.

Cora went first, “What are they even going to do with, what, three hundred sixteen-year-olds, if they manage to catch them all, which is unlikely? Try to force them to sail the ships? Send them all to the Palace of Stars as some kind of choir?”

Bertie added, “I'm sure the city council is up at the Palace now trying to offer them something else instead.”

There was a silence, which Kip let unspool. “Obviously, if we get word that the terms had changed, I would reconsider,” he said, trying to keep his voice calm. “But the easiest thing to do with three hundred teens would be to sell them to the farms of Kavanor, or the mines of Ysthar.”

“Imperial citizens can't be enslaved,” said Bertie, dismissively.

“A life sentence to hard labour for rebellion and treason,” said Kip gently, “Would generally be considered a mercy. Go talk to Ginger, or Toucan and the elders, maybe they have a clever plan. Or maybe pray to Mama Ituri, and ask her to smite the Navy.”

Bertie huffed. “We will. Well, not the Mama Ituri bit, I don't want all of Gorjo City wiped out with the Navy.”

Kip hugged them both. “I *do* hope you find another answer,” he said, “I don't *want* to do this.” They both hugged him back, and then left to find Toucan.

With a two-hour lecture followed by a three-hour lab, Ghilly was the last to find him.

“Of course it's you,” she said, with a sigh, “I knew all that hard study would land you in trouble.” She hugged him, and lifted her chin up for a kiss. 

Kip obliged, but he couldn't help but wonder what she meant by it. “Do you want to…?” he asked, softly.

“No,” she said, “I'm such a bundle of nerves, I couldn't relax. If you're still here tomorrow evening, you can ravish me under the boardwalk.”

“Sorry,” said Kip, and began kneading her shoulders instead. “I can feel you've been hunched over a lab bench all morning.”

Ghilly relaxed into the massage. “Kip,” she said, “If they do take you…”

“You find someone else, Ghilly,” he said, with a peck on her hair. “There's no word I'd ever be able to come back.”

She tucked her head under his chin. “I didn't realise until today I'd managed to snag one of the best in the whole Wide Seas.”

“According to an arbitrary and discriminatory list drawn up by some fop at the Palace of Stars whose probably never worked a day in his life,” said Kip, with another little kiss on her hair.

They sat together for a while, just holding hands and listening to the sounds of the university. Students arguing, birds singing, somewhere a lecturer getting impassioned and thumping on a desk. They got noodles together at the cafeteria, and ate them, squished against each other on the bench.

Then Ghilly gave Kip another kiss, and left for her afternoon classes.

Over the afternoon and evening, most of the student clubs formed plans and disappeared. Toucan came by with a message from Lazo, asking Kip to come to the shop. 

Kip sighed. “I don't want to make trouble for you,” he said, awkwardly.

“But you're not going to talk to him,” finished Toucan.

“No,” said Kip, firmly.

“They've been really helpful, you know, organising things, sending the word out to the Ring,” said Toucan.

“I know,” said Kip, “They're good people. Uncle Lazo is good. If it was anyone else, he'd be thinking with his head.”

Toucan nodded, “I'll say I didn't see you, but I passed the word on.”

“Thanks,” said Kip.
