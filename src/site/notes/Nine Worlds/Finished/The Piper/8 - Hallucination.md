After the trip to Master Triébert's shop, Cliopher treasured the time he was allowed to spend playing the oboe. When he made mistakes in his lessons with Lady Mariel, it was certainly harder to play, but he persevered. As he played, he could pretend he was somewhere else, anywhere else. He could forget how tired and sore the rituals made him, and just lose himself in the feeling of his hands moving across the keys, and the joy of the sound.

It was during his precious hour of practice time, on the last full day they were in Kavanor, that there came a knock. Not at his door, which he might have expected, but from behind the wall. Cliopher assumed it was simply someone accidentally bumping the wall on the room behind, perhaps cleaning the suite next door.

But it came again, more insistently, and sounding exactly like someone knocking on a door. “Come in?” he whispered, not wanting to be heard by Lady Mariel in the parlour.

A panel on the wall opened, to reveal itself to be a door. Cliopher startled, gripping the oboe tightly, but knocking the sheet music off the reading stand with his elbow.

“Hello!” said a man, with an infectious grin. His skin was so dark it was nearly black, and he was dressed in blue, with a scarlet mantle, and sword was strapped to his belt.

“Please,” whispered Cliopher, “Keep your voice down.”

“Oh?” whispered the other man, whose clothing and manner were tickling a thought in the back of Cliopher's head. “Why? Are you trying to get a baby to sleep? Because I'd pick a happier tune for that if I were you.”

“No,” whispered Cliopher, “But my guardian is in the next room, and I have no idea what she will do if she sees you.”

The man looked at him for a good long moment. “You can't possibly be a child,” he said, and Cliopher's eyes went wide, and he urgently tapped a finger to his lips to signal for quiet.

“Sorry,” whispered the man, looking sheepish.

“I'm twenty-one,” whispered Cliopher, “I don't know what else to call her. My tutor? My escort?”

“Escort to where?” whispered the man.

“Astandalas the Golden,” Cliopher whispered back, “Why are you in my bedroom?”

“Oh!” said the man, and the infectious grin came back. Then he winced, and whispered, “I heard you playing, and I couldn't resist. I'm a bard, you know.”

Suddenly, the pieces dropped together. “Fitzroy?” mouthed Cliopher, not daring even to whisper. His whole body shivered. The man looked nothing like the 'wanted' poster in his bedroom at home. That was probably good news for the bard himself.

The man, Fitzroy Angursell, nodded once, sharply. “The one and only. So,” he whispered, “Want to sneak out and come play with me?”

Cliopher closed his eyes. It was his dream come true, his actual daydream. How many times had he imagined himself just _seeing_ the Red Company, or hearing Fitzroy play. And here was Fitzroy Angursell, appearing in his room and inviting him on an adventure. 

There was no way he could say yes. Two tears leaked out his eyes, as he shook his head.

“Why not?” whispered Fitzroy.

“I'm a tithe from the province of the Wide Seas, being delivered to the Emperor,” said Cliopher bitterly, not looking at the other man, but fiddling with the keys of the oboe. “I don't want to know the beating I would get if I snuck out.”

“A tithe?” whispered the bard, “I thought people were _invited_ to play in the Palace of Stars, not forced?”

“I wish—” footsteps approached the door, and suddenly frantic, Cliopher waved Fitzroy back into the service hall and shut the door.

“Have you fallen asleep, Cliopher?” came Lady Mariel's voice through the door.

“No, my lady,” said Cliopher, moving to open the door. He did so and bowed, the oboe still in his hand. “Did you need me for anything?”

“Why were you silent?” she asked, suspiciously, looking around the room, “And why is your music on the floor?”

“Sorry, my lady, I must have knocked it off as I was coming to the door,” said Cliopher, blushing, “I was practicing the fingering only in the next section, my lady, I apologise if I caused you any concern.”

She gave him a suspicious glare, and said, “Pack it away, you have another ritual in five minutes.”

“Of course, my lady,” said Cliopher quietly. He didn't dare close the door again. Instead, he left it open as if he was unconcerned about her seeing what he was doing. Cleaning the oboe, he wondered what she thought he had been up to. It was a mystery. He stripped down to his underthings, and went to Exa for the ritual.

This one left him tired and shaking. They seemed to vary a bit, to what schedule or logic Cliopher had no idea. He cleaned up at the washstand in his room, braving closing the door again. Looking at himself in the mirror, he wondered if the appearance of Fitzroy Angursell had been a dream. Probably he had, as Lady Mariel suggested, fallen asleep. It really did make more sense.

Sleep now would be nice, in fact. But soon he would be called to luncheon, and with Exa there he would have to be alert and careful with his table manners.

Despite his efforts, he received two strikes after the meal. Then Lady Mariel, perhaps detecting something of his weakness, declared that she was going out shopping without him. Once she had gone, Cliopher shamelessly hid in his room from Exa.

A dance manual and a book about the major Imperial titles had joined the etiquette book. He tried to read through the book on these important families, which did include some interesting history. It even had a short and incredibly biased account of Aurelius Magnus. But he found himself slipping and dozing off.

So it was, that despite his best efforts, Exa came to find him when the clock chimed four.

“You know, there's plenty of space in here,” said Exa, leaning casually against the door post.

Cliopher sat up from where he had slumped over. “I'm sorry, sir,” he said, “I shouldn't have fallen asleep like that.” Without thinking carefully, he added, “Please don't tell Lady Mariel.”

Exa grinned, “Nothing is free, boy,” he said. “I don't have any of the accoutrement, so you better come to my room.”

As always, Cliopher stripped to his underwear, and hurried across to Exa's room. As he entered, he carefully stepped around Exa's outstretched arm, and into his place in the chalk diagram.

“See, that's what I mean,” said Exa, stepping up close, “Do you want me to tell Lady Mariel you haven't been cooperative?”

“No sir, sorry sir,” said Cliopher, quietly. He tried not to flinch, when Exa laid both hands on Cliopher's waist, adjusting him slightly in the circle. As Exa drew the oil designs, he let his fingers hang loose and trail along Cliopher's body beside the oil. Cliopher did his very best to ignore it.

When they were done, Cliopher was feeling even more sore and tired.

“I'm hungry,” said Exa softly, behind him, “I'll just be heading out for a bit to get something.” 

Cliopher nodded absently, and then stumbled back towards his room to clean off.

Once he was clean, he collapsed into bed again. Part of him wanted to take advantage of having the suite to himself, maybe have a proper bath rather than just a wash. Or see if any of the novels on the bookshelves in the parlour looked interesting. But he was sore, and tired, and it was just so much _effort_ to get up.

Closing his eyes, he fell asleep on top of the covers. He dreamed he was Aurora, and his sister Vinyë had come to rescue him from the tower. When he woke from his nap it was dark, and he cried into his pillow, missing his friends and family like nothing else. He hadn't even dared ask if he could write to his mother. Would they even be told if he was sick, or died?

After drying his eyes, and dressing again, he crept into the dinning room to see if he had been left anything to eat. 

There he found the Red Company, having dinner.

He stopped in the doorway, unable to prevent himself from simply gaping. True, it wasn't _all_ the Red Company, not even half. But he'd managed to convince himself that Fitzroy had been a dream. It was harder to disbelieve Jullanar and Pali, as well as Fitzroy. Sitting down to share out a huge box of steamed buns.

“Oh hello again,” said Fitzroy, casually. “I hope you don't mind. Would you like some?”

“Thank you,” said Cliopher. Now that he saw his face in better lighting, it was very clear that Fitzroy was at least a bastard of the Imperial Family. The book of family lineages had pictures for many of them. He could see why having a 'wanted' poster that actually looked like Fitzroy could cause problems, given it would also look like many high nobles. Or, in fact, Prince Shallyr.

Cliopher sat down at the table with them, and Jullanar passed him a bun. “You look a bit shocked,” she said, “Don't worry, his effect usually wears off in a bit.”

“Sorry, but, um…” said Cliopher, “Why are you here?”

“To rescue you, of course,” said Fitzroy, with the madcap grin that had made him famous. He bit into a bun, and said to Pali, “I do believe these might be *the* best onion buns.” Pali only rolled her eyes.

Cliopher put his uneaten bun down on the tablecloth. “Oh,” said Cliopher, softly, looking at the table, “I thought I told you earlier? I have to go to Astandalas.”

Pali's hand snaked out to grab a bun from the box, and Cliopher flinched and looked up. “Sorry, my lady,” he said reflexively, then realised what had happened. Blushing and stomach rolling with embarrassment, he looked down again at the bun he had been given.

“Did your family sell you?” asked Pali, looking him over.

“No,” said Cliopher, not looking up at her, “I volunteered.”

Fitzroy reached out a hand towards Cliopher, as if to grip his shoulder sympathetically. Then he stopped, pulled his hand back, shaking it out. “You're dripping with schooled magic, did you know that?”

“There's four to six rituals every day,” said Cliopher, suddenly feeling like he was dirty, “I don't have any magic, so I can't feel it myself. I *am* sorry if it hurt you though.”

“Why did you volunteer?” asked Jullanar, gently.

“Admiral Longren said he would take all the sixteen-year-olds from the province if a suitable candidate didn't volunteer. Another young woman who met the criteria, and I, offered. I was chosen,” said Cliopher, “I don't know what would happen if I didn't arrive at the palace, but I made a promise to my community.”

Not feeling very hungry anymore, Cliopher started just tearing off tiny fragments of bun.

“There's no other way?” asked Pali, sharply.

“Unless you could ask the Sun to change the Glorious One's mind,” said Cliopher with a sigh. 

“Mortals can only go once to the Court of the Sun,” said Fitzroy, with a shrug and a sad smile.

Cliopher had been trying to convince himself he was okay with his selection, that he'd accepted it. Somehow seeing such folk heroes unable to do anything made him realise, more than ever, that he was destroying everything that had he had wanted himself to be.

“I'm sorry,” said Cliopher, trying desperately to control his face, “I should probably go.”

“Wait!” said Jullanar, as Cliopher stood up, “What's your name? Fitzroy's just been calling you The Piper.”

Cliopher hunched his shoulders. “It's an oboe, not a pipe. A pipe has a fipple, an oboe has a double-reed,” he said, softly, “I was Kip Mdang, now it's Cliopher Madon.”

“Green Lady watch over you, Kip,” said Jullanar.

“Thank you,” said Cliopher, bowing deeply to Fitzroy. Then he slunk out of the room.

Cliopher undressed, climbed into bed, and tried to tell himself that the Red Company was some kind of hallucination. Probably brought on by the rituals, and trying to do too much circular breathing in one day. He pulled the covers right up under his chin, and clapped to turn the mage light off. Then, as he had every night since he left the Vangaveye-ve, he cried himself to sleep, remembering his mother singing.
