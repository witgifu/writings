Cliopher had done it. He had saved his people. Would they ever know? Perhaps word might come down via the Poz that Cliopher Madon had been presented at court. Maybe somebody would connect that with Kip Mdang.

He had been gifted to the soon-to-be Consort. Cliopher was too tired to think what that might mean, practically. When the Emperor had mocked him, Princess Melissa had stood up for him. It could have meant his failure, if the Glorious One had rejected him. Cliopher owed Princess Melissa his life, and by extension the lives, or at least wellbeing, of all the Vangaveye-ve.

A few people had gathered around the Princess, complementing her or making requests. Cliopher moved to stand at the edge, waiting to be told what he should do. He did not expect to talk to the Princess, no, the etiquette books made it clear that she would have ladies in waiting who might approach him with instructions.

Eventually, the Princess extracted herself. With a signal to a guard, she walked towards the doors to the audience chamber. Cliopher, somewhat perplexed, followed.

She walked quickly, determinedly. A guard on either side. Cliopher followed, without paying particular attention to where they were going. Only when she reached an impressively grand door, did she stop and look around.

“Yes?” said Princess Melissa, looking at him. It was much firmer and clearer than when she had spoken to the Emperor.

Cliopher dropped into the second-degree obeisance due to the Consort, adding a gesture for a great debt owed. “Your highness,” he said softly.

“Well?” she said, impatiently gesturing for him to rise.

He was confused. Had he done the wrong thing? Should he have gone back to Lady Mairel? Or followed the Emperor? Something of that confusion might have shown on his face, despite his best efforts, because she said, “Come in.”

Cliopher bowed again, “Thank you, your highness.”

Three women waited inside for the Princess. One held the wax note-taking tablet of a secretary, one had the chatelaine of a majordomo, and the last had no obvious markers of role, but seemed older. 

Princess Melissa turned to the secretary, “He ended the session early, what can I fit in instead?”

“Lunch,” said the older woman, before the secretary could speak.

“We could perhaps add in a visit to the army convalescent hospital, your grace?” the secretary offered. Then at the glare the other woman gave her added, “After lunch.”

Princess Melissa nodded, firmly. To the majordomo she said, “The Glorious One gave me a gift.” She turned her head very slightly, to indicate Cliopher, and her tone was studiously neutral.

“Looks like a young man,” said the older woman, “Unless he's a horse under a very good illusion?”

There was a shadow of a smile on Princess Melissa's face, “No, Jivane, he is a special coronation tithe.”

All four women turned to look fully at Cliopher. Nervously, he bowed to the group, with no idea what rank the new three women held. If they were senior members of the nearly-Consort's household, it was likely they held high titles in their own right.

“I will take care of him, your grace,” said the majordomo, “Do you have any specific instructions?”

“No,” said the Princess, “I trust you.” Then she swept down the hallway, and into another room, followed by Lady Jivane, Lady Magus of Zunidh.

The secretary also departed at a brisk pace, into a different room. Then it was just Cliopher and the majordomo. “Can you talk?” she asked.

“Yes, my… lady?” he ventured.

She raised one eyebrow. “I'm no lady,” she said briskly, “My name is Saya Auletes. You are?”

“My apologies, Saya Auletes. I am Cliopher… Madon,” he said, unable to stop himself from pausing at having to mispronounce his family name.

The other eyebrow went up on Saya Auletes's face, “Did they change your name?”

Cliopher found this perceptiveness so surprising he startled. “Yes, Saya Auletes. I was born Cliopher Mdang, although my family usually called me Kip.”

Saya Auletes sniffed. “They do that, nobles. Can't be bothered learning how to say someone's name so they just change it. Right, let's find you a bedroom, Cliopher Mdang, and get you out of that full court costume and into something you can move in.”

“Thank you, Saya Auletes,” said Cliopher, now more confidently making the bow to a superior in position but not rank.

She led him down a corridor, through an antechamber, and then another corridor. Stopping near the end, she looked him up and down. “Wide Seas, you said?”

Cliopher felt for his island, but there was nothing there. Could he still claim to belong to Loaloa if he didn't know his island in his very soul? “Yes, Saya Auletes, I was from the Vangaveye-ve, in the Wide Seas,” said Cliopher.

“_Was_?” said Saya Auletes, surprised, “Did they change your official place of birth or something?”

“Not as far as I am aware, ma'am,” he said, but he couldn't face explaining further.

She opened the left door, which showed a room in light woods, soft blues, and greens. It was easily three times the size of the one he had been given by Lady Mairel. 

What role was she expecting him to fill, that he was given such a sumptuous room? A cosy sitting area, a reading nook, a large desk. Just behind a screen, he could see a large bed as well. “Are you sure, Saya Auletes?” he asked, frightened. 

Saya Auletes rolled her eyes. “Yes,” she said, “It is my job to know. I also know that there's probably clothes in this palace made to fit you, other than that confection. While I find what has happened to them, we can make do. Now, do you have any preferences about who helps you undress?”

“No, ma'am,” said Cliopher, feeling awkward. It didn't seem likely she would send Exa, and that was the only person he knew of he _wouldn't_ want.

“Easy,” she said, “Feel free to stand awkwardly in the middle of the room then, I'll send someone as soon as I can.”

He wasn't entirely sure if she was being serious or sarcastic. Perhaps Kip would have looked around the room and picked out a book to read. Or examined the art, or the view out of the windows. Cliopher went and stood in the middle of the room as instructed.

Before the next quarter-hour bell rang, a man about the same age as Cliopher opened the door, carrying a bundle of clothes. “I'm Faleron,” he said, with a smile, “Not 'the Blue', just Faleron. Let's get you into something more comfortable, Sayo Mdang.”

“Thank you, Sayo Faleron,” said Cliopher, quietly.

“Oh, I'm just Faleron, Sayo Mdang,” he said brightly, heading towards a door Cliopher hadn't even noticed, “Your dressing room is through here, sir. The washroom is beyond it.”

Taking the hint, Cliopher followed Faleron. The young man had put down his burden, and already begun separating it. When Cliopher joined him, he turned and smiled. “Now, these are just some spare sleep clothes. Although, these court costumes are so intricate, the rest of your things might turn up before we get it all off.”

Cliopher went and stood in the middle of the room, on a rug that was presumably there for that purpose. Faleron kept up a gentle stream of chatter, leaving spaces for Cliopher to say something, but continuing happily when he didn't. It was soothing, he thought. The young man was also deft at removing the court costume, although it still took a long time.

“There we go,” said Faleron, as he pulled off the final under-robe, and hung it on a stand. Then he shook out a simple, undyed linen robe with ties. It reminded Cliopher of the clothes he had worn on the ship, but in the plain beige, even more basic.

“Would you like help with dressing, sir?” said Faleron, warmly.

Cliopher's heart leapt at being offered what seemed to be a genuine _choice_. “No, thank you,” he said, quietly.

Faleron smiled again, “I'll just go and fetch you a lunch then, Sayo Mdang. It should be waiting for you when you're ready.” He put the robe down, and left, closing the door behind him.

Cliopher looked at the robe, and then the closed door. There was nothing he had to do, no lessons or study or practice. He hadn't been summoned, or told an hour he needed to be anywhere. He was standing, barefoot now, on the plush rug. Rubbing his feet in it, enjoying the softness, he finally relaxed. Without really thinking, he folded himself down, rolled onto his side, and tucked up his knees. Then he drifted off to sleep.

---

The water was endless, above and below. He was past the point of buoyancy, being pulled along by the dark current. There was no rock to hold to, nothing in any direction, as he tumbled. Even if there had been, would he grasp it? He had always been afraid of drowning, fighting to learn to dive, but perhaps he didn't need to.

Ani, the mother of the oceans, knew where he belonged.

A shining sparkle. Was it a pearl, dropped by another diver? No, as the water spun him around it, he could see it was larger. A toy vaha, like the one his father had made him. Was it the same one? Had Ani brought them back together?

There were two tiny people on the vaha. One an Islander in a grass skirt, the other with ink-dark skin, in red and blue. Cliopher tried to swim closer, to see better. Was this Elonoa'a and Aurelius Magnus? But then shouldn't the Emperor be in yellow?

The boat grew, or he swam closer, it was hard to know. Then he saw the taller man was Fitzroy Angursell. Or at least, it was the man he had hallucinated eating onion buns who claimed to be the outlaw bard. And him, Cliopher. On a vaha, sailing, singing, laughing.

Cliopher wanted to see more, to hear what they were saying. He started swimming properly, pushing across the current. Two voices rang out, singing together, “the unassuming magnificence of / the common and ordinary good.”

It was hard to keep swimming, he was tired and sore, and surely he should be running out of air. With that last thought, he gasped, and awoke with a start.

---

There was carpet under him, not sand. He could breathe. Slowly, his memories fluttered back into place. Where he was, why he was there.

Cliopher staggered up to his feet, and found the robe. He pulled it on, and the matching over-robe. There were even a pair of slippers. Then he shuffled out of the dressing room, to see what was to become of him next. How maybe he might repay even some of what he owed.

A covered tray sat on the table. Cliopher walked over to it, and lifted the lacy cloth to reveal a bowl of thick stew, flatbread, and a glass of watered wine. The smell made him realise he hadn't eaten since dinner the night before. Suddenly ravenous, he fell on the food, scooping the stew with the bread.

Just as he was wiping up the last of the delicious thick gravy, there was a knock at the door. He shoved the last bit of bread into his mouth, and went to see who it was.

Faleron was pushing a trunk on a trolley. Vaguely, Cliopher recognised it as the trunk he had used earlier. He stood back to let Faleron come in. 

“I'll do the clothes first sir, they're on top. Then I'll get your books and things,” said Faleron, pushing the trolley over to the dressing room.

Cliopher returned to the table, and sat down to sip the watered wine. Faleron was finished in the dressing room before Cliopher finished the drink.

“Would you like tea or coffee, sir?” he asked, as he pushed the trunk over to the shelves.

“Coffee, please, if you have it,” said Cliopher, shyly. With Lady Mairel so keen to teach him how to serve tea correctly, he hadn't had much opportunity for coffee.

“Of course, sir, no trouble at all, we always have both. Many of the staff here are from Damara, you know, and prefer coffee,” said Faleron, happily. “Oh, you play oboe, Sayo Mdang? Such a beautiful instrument,” he said, as he extracted the instrument case and accessories, and arranged them on the shelves.

“Yes,” said Cliopher quietly.

“Not many books,” said Faleron, “But I suppose you've been very busy, and travelling from so far! The library is the other door off the room that joins the private and public rooms, you will have passed it on your way in, but if you get lost at any time don't hesitate to ask anyone. I know I did when I first came here.” Faleron smiled, and pulled out the sheet music as well.

When Cliopher said nothing, he continued. “Now, you can take any books out of the library and bring them back to your room to read, there's just a little card in the front of each one to put in the box by the door, just so if someone needs a particular book they know who to ask.”

“Thank you,” said Cliopher.

“Not at all, sir. There should be pens and paper and so on in the desk,” said Faleron, and wandered over to check it. “Yes, indeed there is. And stamps, I see, good. Post usually arrives twice per day, sir so we put it out at breakfast and lunch, although if you're sending messages within the Palace those can be at any time.”

Faleron went around the room, explaining the various features with more of the gentle patter of chatter. Then he took away the trunk and tray, promising to return with coffee.

Cliopher looked at the desk. Could he write a letter? Faleron made it sound like that was expected. It would be good to let people know he was… alive. That he had done what he had set out to do.
