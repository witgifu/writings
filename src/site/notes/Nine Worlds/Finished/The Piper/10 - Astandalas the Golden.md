“You're late,” Exa growled, waking Cliopher up. He scrambled off the bed, and then out of his clothes.

Exa grabbed him by the upper arm, and pulled him along the hallway. They didn't stop at the door Cliopher thought was Exa's but at what looked more like a cupboard door. When Exa opened it, Cliopher realised it probably had been a cupboard once. Now it was a tiny magic workroom.

Shoving him into the centre of the circle, Exa rushed through setting up the spell as dramatic bells began to ring. Just as they finished, he began chanting.

Perhaps it was being woken so suddenly, or Exa's bad mood, or being on a different world. But this ritual didn't feel like it was scraping his skin, so much as peeling it off. Only by biting on his lip so hard that it bled did Cliopher managed to stop himself from crying out. The whimpers and tears he could do nothing about. Something about the spell stopped him from falling over, held him in place. When it finished, whatever it was released him, and Kip crumpled to the floor.

Crouching down, Exa gently stroked a hand over Cliopher's scalp. “You will be on time in future, yes?” he asked.

“Y-yes, sir,” said Cliopher, shaking. Was it that the change in the ritual was somehow a punishment for his tardiness? The mage was not explicit, and Cliopher dared not ask. Perhaps there was some kind of clock or alarm in his room he could use.

“Good boy,” said Exa, and kissed Cliopher on the top of his head. “Soon I'll be showing you to the priest-wizards, and they aren't as forgiving as I am.”

This was being forgiving? He didn't dare say anything. Instead, he gathered all his strength, and pushed himself up. Not looking at Exa, Cliopher left the cupboard, and staggered back to his room, stopping a few times to lean on the wall.

“Back here at ten,” called Exa, from the doorway.

“Yes, sir,” said Cliopher, not looking back.

Back in his own room, Cliopher dragged the chair over to the washstand. There was no way he was going to be able to clean up while standing, his legs felt like strands of kelp. Carefully, he cleaned himself up, and shaved his head. The trunk must have arrived sometime in the night, for the few things from it had been set out. The three study books, the oboe, and sheet music. The shoes, including the strange boots, and the coat behind the door. More must be in the cupboards.

Yes, there was a sort of magical alarm clock by his bed. He poked it until he worked out how to set it for ten minutes before ten. Then he stripped and did a full wash, wishing he dared go looking for a bath. Cliopher was rather afraid that he might have been assigned to share a bathroom with Exa, however, and did not want to spend any extra time with the man.

He also briefly considered trying to find the dinning room, and perhaps breakfast. But that room would also possibly have Exa, and might well have Lady Mairel. He missed coffee more than the food, but it was easier to spend as much time as possible alone.

Looking at the shelf of things he had been given, that he supposed he should think of as his, although he could not quite do so, he sighed. One chapter of each book, and then he would play the oboe. Hopefully, by then, his arms would feel more sturdy as well.

Whether by virtue of Kip being on time, or some other reason, the ten o'clock ritual only left Cliopher feeling scrubbed raw. 

Shortly before the one o'clock bell rang, a knock came at his door. He answered it, expecting Exa or Lady Mairel, but finding to his relief the maid from yesterday. “My lady requests you in the parlour, my lord,” she said, with a bow.

“Is that next to the dinning room?” Cliopher asked, having not actually had time, or the desire, to explore. 

“Yes, my lord,” said the maid, “The library is the other side, closer to the bedrooms.”

“Thank you,” said Cliopher, “But I'm not a lord. Just a commoner.”

The maid said softly, and with another bow, “My lady says you are, my lord, and I do not like to cross her.”

Something in her eyes perhaps, suggested to him that she knew exactly what disobedience cost him. And perhaps that she might face the same as he did, or worse.

“Neither do I,” said Cliopher, “Sorry.”

“You need not apologise to me, my lord,” she said, and turned to lead him down the hallway.

He let the matter drop. Was he to be made into a lord? Was that what the proclamation had meant when it said 'ornaments to his court and person'? Cliopher had vaguely assumed it meant some kind of attendant, or perhaps performers. He knew, from Saya Dorn, that the very best musicians were invited to perform for the Lord Emperor.

He assumed the summons was for more lessons with Lady Mariel. Instead, he opened the door to see two strangers. A wiry looking young woman paced the centre of the room, and a very old woman with thick spectacles sat at the pianoforte.

He bowed to them both as if they were minor nobility with much uncertainty. There were no obvious family badges or any other of the tells the book mentioned, and both of them had medium brown skin, similar to his own. They both bowed back, and looked at him, expectantly. 

“Good afternoon,” he said, nervously, “I am Cliopher Madon.”

“I am your dance instructor, Guild master Shaiangani Sandushdika,” said the woman who had been pacing. “You may call me Mistress Shani.”

Lady Mairel had arranged for him to me taught by a *master dancer*? He bowed again, and said, “Thank you, Mistress Shani.”

“Come here,” she said, pointing to a spot on the floor, “We begin with stretching.”

Cliopher had to admit he was impressed with how flexible she was. Perhaps it shouldn't have been surprising, she must have been dancing from a young age to already have her mastery. She poked and prodded him to get him into the exact positions she wanted, showing him where he should feel tension. It was not at all like the way Exa behaved, Mistress Shani was efficient and businesslike.

“You claimed to have some dance skill already, and I can see you've stretched before. Do you know any Astandalan court or country dances?” she asked.

“No, Mistress Shani,” he said quietly.

“Are your people's dances for performance or participation?”

Cliopher considered. His dances were far more than simply *entertainment*. However, he answered the question, “Both, Mistress Shani.”

“All the dances I will teach you are done in pairs, sometimes in rows or squares. The parts are called 'man' and 'woman' but you will learn both. Let us start with how to walk.”

Cliopher blinked in confusion. He'd been walking for years, she had seen him walk over. Shrugging internally he assumed this must be the name of a particular dance or move. He looked attentively at his teacher.

She held out her left hand, and after a small delay Cliopher gave her his left. “Other hand,” she said with a smile.

“Sorry, Mistress,” he said softly, and switched hands.

She turned to face the end wall, and the pianist began a slow, dreary march. Mistress Shani began to walk in time, and Cliopher copied her. She walked toe first, like the outer-ring folk who never wore sandals. There was also the slightest hint of bounce. Slowly, the music got faster, as she led him around the armchairs and tables.

Cliopher was surprised when she started going backwards, but managed not to miss any steps. When she switched again to forwards, one of the diagrams in the dance manual suddenly clicked in his head. He had tried to read it, but could tell he was missing a lot of information, with just descriptions and stylised diagrams.

His body was still sore from the rituals, and the lessons with Lady Mairel. It did not come easy, this new form of dance. But it was good to move, not to think too hard, just let the music and Mistress Shani lead him. Like the oboe, this was something where he didn't have to be anyone, he could just be.

She showed him two actual dances, correcting him as she went. Lifting a hand higher, correcting a foot placement. When the bells chimed ten o'clock, she stopped, and told him to do the same stretches again. 

“Make sure you stretch at least twice per day,” she said, as she watched him finish the last one.

“Yes, Mistress Shani,” he said. Then he stood straight and waited to be asked what mistakes he had made.

Mistress Shani looked at him quizzically. “Are you waiting for something, Cliopher?”

Flushing, Cliopher bowed, “My apologies, Mistress Shani, I had thought—,” then he stopped himself. Lady Mairel was always clear there were to be no excuses. “It is not important, I am sorry for keeping you,” he bowed again, and walked slowly towards the door, waiting to be told to stop.

“Cliopher?” she asked. The pianist had stood up and closed the instrument cover, but when Cliopher turned back Mistress Shani hadn't moved.

His stomach twisting, Cliopher bowed again. Should he offer a second-degree apology? Should he say something? He seemed to just be compounding his mistakes.

“What were you expecting to happen, before?” she asked, still sounding confused.

“That you would list my failures this evening and,” he wasn't sure what the polite term would be, so he tried, “chastise me.”

“That's what Lady Mairel does?” asked Mistress Shani, one eyebrow raised.

“Yes, Mistress Shani,” said Cliopher quietly, looking at her feet.

“Idiot,” muttered Mistress Shani.

Cliopher looked up, startled, and opened his mouth to apologise again.

“Not you, Cliopher,” said Mistress Shani gently, “Your etiquette teacher. Probably thinks *she* came out perfectly fine.”

Cliopher didn't know what to say to that. The etiquette book said that he should defend his patron or family against slander. Was Lady Mairel his patron? He decided the safest thing was to say nothing.

“You can go, Cliopher, I'm sure you have a busy schedule,” she said.

Finally, something where he knew what was expected. “Thank you, Mistress Shani,” he said with a bow, and left the room.

That evening, Cliopher _was_ summoned to Lady Mairel for another lesson. It turned out to be a crash-course on behaviour in The Presence. He was sure she said it with capitals. This began with three hours of practicing different forms of the obeisance. The full formal prostration, through to a deep genuflection that was suitable when one was departing and would soon return. In addition, there were a number of gestures to rise, indicating how far one could go, such as being allowed to rise to stand, or only to kneel.

Cliopher's knees and ankles hurt. Lady Mairel poked him with her foot to get him in the exact position she wanted. Then she would make him stand up, and drop again, until he could drop at the right speed, with the right expression, into the exact correct posture.

As Cliopher was getting sore, and making more mistakes, he feared that Lady Mairel was becoming more upset with him.

“If you think I am being too particular, Cliopher,” she said dryly, clearly noticing his attitude, “Remember that Eritanyr once had three people executed for poorly performed obeisances.”

“My apologies, my lady,” he said softly, but did not bother to offer any further reason in case that might be considered 'talking back'. He also didn't know what would happen to his province, to his people, if he was executed by the Emperor. Would that count as failing to provide an adequate tithe? His arms shook as he tried again, and it wasn't only from exhaustion and pain.

“I know you are sore, and tired,” said Lady Mairel, in a highly unusual display of sympathy, “But the Emperor will never be concerned about such things. You must be perfect in every circumstance.”

“I understand, my lady,” said Cliopher, into the floor. It seemed cruel to demand constant perfection, nobody was perfect. Except, he supposed, the Emperor was by _definition_ perfect. Eventually she signalled for him to rise. Sensing that perhaps Lady Mairel was in a good mood, as she hadn't mentioned anything he had done wrong all day, Cliopher decided to dare to ask a question.

“My lady,” he said, looking down, and trying to drag the correct phrasing from the etiquette book, “Would you be so kind as to tell me what will happen?”

She sighed, “You will be presented to the Glorious One, in court. The representatives of the Upper Ten Thousand all stand around trying not to look bored as a queue of young nobles are announced and make their bows to the Emperor.”

“Thank you, my lady,” said Kip, his heart speeding up. “And… and after that, my lady? What will become of me?” He had an image in his mind of being taken and filed on a shelf next to boxes of pearls and bolts of ahalo cloth. That was probably optimistic.

“That will depend on the Glorious One,” she said, “Of those presented so far, one was gifted to a general, one sent to a monastery. Most of the tithes have been added to the inner court of his Radiancy's household. Two have subsequently been executed.”

Cliopher bowed, “Thank you, my lady.” He paused, wondering if the inner court of Emperor Shallyr was much like his Buru Tovo's tales of meeting Prince Eritanyr. It was widely known the son wanted to outdo his father. In a 'backwater' province they might not get timely reports, but even they knew what kind of person the new Emperor was.
