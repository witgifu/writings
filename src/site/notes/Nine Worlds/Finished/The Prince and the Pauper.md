It was getting dark. Fitz stretched as he put down the hoe, and turned to the others. "Time to head in?" he said, hopefully.

“Eye,” said the old man, “Will be storming tonight.”

Everyone else nodded, and shouldered their tools. “Holly!” called out the stout mother named Essie, “Get them pigs in!”

A teenage boy ran out of the house, “I about to, ma!”

Essie shook her head, “A ways about to, never do.” They all smiled though. 

“Ho there!” called Holly, at someone behind them. They all turned around. 

At the main farm gate stood a middle-aged man, in travel-worn clothes. He was medium-brown, and his dark hair was flopping into his eyes. In his right hand were the reigns of a horse, of indeterminate quality under a layer of dust.

“We ain't got naught for trade,” said the old man, “Less you want a place to sleep out the storm.”

“Dried fruit and fresh fish for a place to sleep and supper?” offered the stranger, “I don't mind camping, but Pen doesn't like thunder.”

“Y'been poaching the lord's fish eh?” said Essie, with a grin. “What'll we call you then?”

“Just Kip is fine for me,” said the stranger.

Fitz dropped his hoe, narrowly missing his own foot. “Kip?” he said softly. There were probably hundreds of people called Kip, he thought, as the man turned towards him.

“My—,” said Kip, and swallowed hard, “Do you remember me?”

“Eh, what's this Fitz? Do you got family?” asked the old man, looking between them. “He ain't look much like you, but. Look like the hill folk, as you look like the islands.”

“He was my… friend,” said Fitz, awkwardly, “Before.”

Essie made the sign against evil spirits. “Get inside, all of you, before the rain wash us away, eh.”

Nervous, Fitz cocked his head at Kip, and waved at the barn door. “Does Pen mind goats?” he asked.

“No,” said Kip, with a tentative smile, “She's good with most animals except geese.”

“Well, fair,” said Fitz, “Geese don't like anyone either.”

They went together into the barn, and Kip hitched Pen to a ring in the main post. Then he got her a bucket of water, and took off the packs. All the while muttering to her. Finally, he took a twist of hay, and began rubbing the road dirt off her.

“I didn't know you knew how to take care of horses, Kip,” said Fitz.

“I didn't, when you last saw me,” said Kip, keeping his eyes on Pen. “I've learnt a few things since then.” They lapsed into silence, as Kip continued to take care of his horse.

When Kip was satisfied, he turned to Fitz, who had been filling his time mucking out the goats. The goats seemed to object, although Fitz was never sure if it was just that they didn't like him in particular.

“You didn't know how to muck out animals, when I last saw you,” said Kip, picking up his packs.

“I did,” said Fitz, putting away the hay fork, “I just didn't have any chance to use the skill. Like so many things I knew how to do.”

“You remember?” said Kip, his voice catching.

“Yes,” said Fitz, “I think I remember everything. I can't talk about it with most people. But I guess since you already know.” Fitz shrugged, and sighed.

“I'm the only one in Solaara,” said Kip, and bit his lip, “Conju, Ludvic, Rhodin, Kiri… any time I tried to mention you, it was like my mouth was stuck.”

“I see the proclamations in the town hall sometimes,” said Fitz, “You've been doing great things.”

Kip snorted. “Barely holding things together,” he said, wryly, “I haven't seen Ludvic in two years, he's been so busy commanding the army in the field. Conju practically lives on a sky ship now, travelling between the Princes. But, we can talk about that later, maybe.”

There was a tense pause.

“I'm sure everyone wants to see about those fish,” said Fitz.

Kip nodded, and tugged on a leather bucket. Peering over, Fitz saw at least three good-size cod. He picked it up, and Kip took his packs, and they headed through the inner door to the main room.

“Cod, eh?” said Essie, when she peered in the bucket, “Will you gut, scale, and bone them for me? Only I don't want Da choking, and I don't trust any of mine to do it right.”

“Of course, mistress,” said Kip, politely. “Where should I put the bits?”

She pointed at the bucket, and then went to tell her brother off for how he was cutting the onions.

Kip squatted over the bucket, in the doorway, to do the messy job. Fitz came and sat beside him, with a bowl of dried split peas to pick through.

“Did you come looking for me?” said Fitz, eyes carefully scanning the bowl as he sifted through the peas with his fingers.

“Yes,” said Kip, softly, “You know I'd planned six different speeches to make when I saw you? But when the moment came, all of them seemed stupid. You're happy here, aren't you?”

“Happier than… than I had been since before you knew me,” said Fitz, throwing a small bit of rock out the door.

“These people seem very kind,” offered Kip.

“They've been unbelievably generous, sharing everything they have with me,” said Fitz, his eyes misting up. “I've done my best to return the favour with work, but there are some gifts it's impossible to pay back.”

“I know,” said Kip, checking with his fingers to make sure he hadn't missed any bones.

“Did you come here to ask me to go back?” said Fitz, his hands paused, and trembling slightly.

Kip sighed, and stood up. “I came looking for the Emperor, but he's gone, isn't he?”

“For you, Kip—” said Fitz, but Kip interrupted.

“No,” said Kip, and carried the fish over to Essie.
