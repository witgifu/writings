The midnight bell rang, and Cliopher thought it was probably time he headed back to his own rooms. If he stayed much longer, Conju's excellent brandy was likely to give him a headache.

Conju must have seen the turn of Cliopher's thoughts. He smiled, and refilled his friend's water glass instead.

“I did want to pass on my thanks,” said Conju, looking at the crystal water jug as he put it down, “To your past self for ensuring *he* got to bed on time yesterday.”

Cliopher blinked. He couldn't think how, but waited for Conju to explain.

“You know he's been searing for evidence of the borders, of course, I assume he had you hunting through the Archives as well,” said Conju.

Nodding, Cliopher wondered what ancient reports the Archivist had managed to find. His Radiancy had mentioned it this morning he'd found evidence, but they had been mostly absorbed in the audit Cliopher had done of the Department of Fisheries.

“He found it in your report on flood prevention,” said Conju.

Cliopher's blood ran cold. It would have, of course. He remembered that report now. He also remembered what else it contained. Unlike anyone he had worked with at the time, his Radiancy would have read the entire thing.

“Oh, good,” said Cliopher, absently. Then he rapidly applied his court neutral smile. It wouldn't do for Conju to catch his anxiety. “Speaking of going to bed on time...”

If Conju noticed, he was too polite to mention it. Instead, he sent his friend off with a gentle patter of other gossip.

The walk back to his rooms seemed somehow both longer and shorter than usual. Why had he not already been arrested? Was his Radiancy pulling up every other report he had written, checking for further evidence? Cliopher was thankful he had only needed to use that trick once. 

He hadn't seen Rhodin or Commander Omo since yesterday. They were probably both involved in the investigation. Would they ask his family? There was still a box of his and Basil's writings in his room back home.

It was the first time that Cliopher had reason to be relieved that Basil had not replied to any of his letters since the Fall. He could hardly be arrested for treason if he never so much as wrote to Zunidh.

By the time he arrived back in his rooms, Cliopher was resigned to his fate. He spent a couple of bells updating his letters to his family and friends, which he kept in case of his death. To them he added notes for Kiri, and last and most painfully, a letter of confession and apology to his Radiancy.

If he confessed, they could avoid a public trial. He made sure to word it carefully to make sure nobody else was implicated. If he could cut short the investigation, he hoped nobody else would be charged. 

He wanted to lie, and say it was the only thing of that kind he had ever written. Of course if they found his writings from home, that would only make things worse. And, of course, every reform he had mad to the Zunidh government was inspired by the works of Fitzroy Angursell, and the Lays. He would not deny that.

Sometimes in the quiet hours without bells, he finished. Then he did his ablutions, and went to bed.

When he woke the next morning, it took him a few moments to remember why he felt so anxious. Then the previous night returned in full clarity. So it was with a looming sense of doom that he put on his best uniform, and made his way into the Offices of State.

It wouldn't do to be yawning as he made his obeisance, so he did make sure he had two cups of coffee. That perhaps didn't help with his stomach feeling unsettled, but he wasn't sure he could face eating anything. Kiri came in as he was staring at a banana in the kitchen.

“Did it offend you, sir?” she asked, as she got herself a cup of herbal tea.

It was a few moments before Cliopher even realised she'd been talking to him. “Sorry,” he said, “Did what offend me?”

“The banana,” she replied, “You were looking at it the way you look at dispatch boxes you suspect contain bad news.”

“I didn't realise I did that,” said Cliopher, “I should probably try to stop. No, I was just looking in the direction of the banana, not at it.”

“Ah, then has something happened overnight? Something I should be preparing people for?” asked Kiri, bracing herself.

It wasn't, Cliopher reflected, either untrue or an unreasonable assumption. Still, he couldn't quite face telling her. Anyway, it would be poor etiquette to confess to someone else _before_ his Radiancy.

“Not for certain,” said Cliopher.

“Your hunches are generally good,” said Kiri, “I don't suppose you could give me a hint?”

Cliopher sighed. “If something happens, you will probably hear about it from Commander Omo,” he said, and turned to leave.

“Oh,” said Kiri, “Ah. Right. That kind of problem. I'll wait to hear first then.”

Cliopher nodded, and went back to his office. There he did his best to clear off every piece of work he could find. It wouldn't do to leave anything for Kiri to have to finish off that was half-done. Nor would it be good to abandon any of his regular work. It was _because_ of his love of Fitzroy Angursell that he was here, doing what he could for Zunidh.

Following his usual routine, he made his way up to the Imperial Apartments, and made small-talk with the guards as he passed through the many layers. He tried to act as much as possible like this was an ordinary day. It wouldn't do to worry anyone else.

The final doors were opened, and Cliopher performed his obeisance. He hadn't been so worried since his second day serving his Radiancy. He was gestured to rise as usual, noting that today's guards were Rhodin and Oginu. There was no movement from either towards him, and no sign from the spymaster that he was watching Cliopher any more than usual.

But then, there wouldn't be.

“Good morning, Sayo Mdang,” said his lord.

“Good morning, my lord,” said Cliopher quietly. Unable to make eye contact for fear he would burst into tears.

He put his writing kit down on his desk, but did not take his seat as usual. Instead, he removed the letter of confession, his heart beating so hard he thought it must be audible to everyone else. Carefully, he put it into the place on his Radiancy's desk where he usually placed documents for his lord's reference or seal.

Then he stepped away, and folded down into the full formal prostration. Forehead resting against the cool marble, eyes closed. He didn't want to see the look of betrayal from his lord, didn't want to see him turn to his guards. If he could have shut his ears as well, he would have.

Instead, he heard the soft steps as his lord moved to pick up the page, and the paper bending slightly as he picked it up to read. Cliopher's heart twisted inside him. Had he done enough to protect his family? Was —

Much faster footsteps, and the sound of a door opening. That must have been the door to the Private Study, for his Radiancy opened no other for himself. The paper fluttered, the door snicked shut, and his Radiancy's robes swooshed against the marble floor.

“Cliopher,” said his Radiancy, perfectly calmly.

“Yes, my lord,” said Cliopher, into the floor, not moving.

“You may rise. There was nothing to be ashamed of in that report, it showed the kind of clear insight I have always appreciated from you,” said his Radiancy.

Slowly, nervously, Cliopher moved first to his knees, and then to standing. He was not being arrested, and his lord had addressed him with praise, with informal pronouns. Usually he would treasure such a statement, turning it over again and again.

Had the report somehow _not_ contained the letter? But he had gone down and copied it out, so it would have had to have been removed between then and when his Radiancy had requested it. Had someone found it shortly after the fall, when the spells had been behaving erratically? But why would anyone be looking at that report at all? Especially then when everyone had been much more concerned with the immediate problems of food and medical supplies.

“Thank you, my lord,” said Cliopher, baffled by his luck.

Yet, his letter of confession had been explicit, and it wasn't as if his Radiancy seemed confused.

“Do you write any others in the same series?” asked his Radiancy, “I would be interested to read them.”

Cliopher paused, as he moved back to his desk. “I wrote many reports as a junior secretary, my lord,” he replied, prevaricating. Was his Radiancy asking what Cliopher thought he was asking? Was he requesting Cliopher hand him _more_ treasonous writing?

“Of course,” said his Radiancy, moving into his usual pacing. Then in the same tone he would usually use when changing topic, he said, “You have often asked, Cliopher, if we might reconsider the ban on the works of Fitzroy Angursell. Perhaps we might allow private reading, and academic study.”

With slightly shaking hands, Cliopher drew out his pens and papers. “Thank you, my lord,” said Cliopher, carefully, “I shall draft something and have it to you by this evening.”

Later, as Cliopher sat staring at his lunch, he finally allowed himself to think about what had happened that morning. There was now no doubt in his mind that his Radiancy had read every page that had been in the file. He had then seen Cliopher's confession, and his response had been to carve out an exemption in the law which could possibly be used to allow Cliopher's crime to be ignored.

Next time he went home, he would have to see if anything else of that story had survived. In the box, in the cupboard, in his childhood bedroom. Perhaps it had.
