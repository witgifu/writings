Cliopher woke up, and as always before coffee, it took him a little while to orient himself. He was in the vacation house, on Lesuia. Last night it had been the lunar eclipse party. And then.

He rolled over, his mind completely unwilling to finish that thought. There, beside him, was the Lord of Rising Stars. His breath, his chest, falling and rising gently. Kip looked further, to the door. Two guards, Ato and Ludvic were standing there now, keeping watch.

The light suggested it was early still, just past first light. Had his Radiancy being doing the dawn rituals on his own here? Cliopher didn't dare wake him, he looked so beautiful asleep. Serene in truth, rather than careful composure learnt through years of fear.

A bird greeted the dawn, not far from the window. His Radiancy jerked awake. He was rigid, looking at the ceiling, breathing rapidly.

“Did you have a bad dream?” said Cliopher, softly.

“I—” said his Radiancy, and Cliopher watched as his lord put on the serenity, and disappeared. “Was it a dream, Cliopher? What happened last night?”

“The Moon Lady came in answer to your prayer,” said Cliopher, now starting to feel nervous, “She… awoke something in you? And we came back here, and kissed and...”

His Radiancy closed his eyes, lying perfectly still. Cliopher had a flash of memory, of visiting the bier of the sleeping Emperor. Cliopher reached out his golden hand, to reassure himself that his lord is still there, but stopped just short of touching.

“My l-lord?” he said, his voice shaking, “I—I regret nothing, except that now I am afraid I have hurt you, somehow.”

“Cliopher,” said his Radiancy, “It is I who hurt you, I who took advantage, who abused my position for my own selfish—”

“No!” said Cliopher, loudly, unable to let his lord go on berating himself, “I promised I would tell you if I felt uncomfortable, what I liked, and disliked. And I did. I love you, Tor.”

“We should leave,” said his Radiancy, still looking at the ceiling, “Return to Solaara; you need not come with us. You can retire, here, to your home. We will make you Duke of Ilkano, and we will give you the next sky ship. I am sorry, I know it cannot possibly make right what I have done—”

Cliopher rolled over onto his front, and slid feet-first off the edge and onto the floor. His mind was a freezing wasteland. It was all his worst fears come true. His lord was sending him away. He couldn't think what to say, he just lay, shaking, in the formal prostration. Tears ran unchecked down his face. His Radiancy wasn't listening, didn't understand, and Cliopher didn't know how to make him.

“Oh, Cliopher,” said his Radiancy, sitting up, “Please, get up. I—” he swallowed, and then slipped off the other side of the bed. “I'll send Conju,” he whispered, and fled the room. The guards followed.

Slowly, alone now, Cliopher got to his feet. He was still shaking. What was he going to do? Go back to his mother's house? How could he explain what had happened to any of his friends and family? When he didn't even understand? What had he done _wrong_?

He looked out the window, into the trees. It felt like when he had arrived back after the Fall, he didn't belong anywhere. Not in Solaara, not in the Vangaveye-ve.

Well, maybe there was one place he did belong. He could still feel the pull of his island. Gardening, fishing. Perhaps if he needed to, Buru Tovo would let him dive for pearls again.

Something in him seemed to break, like a worn rope that finally snapped. He pulled out a clean pair of trews and put them on. Looking around the room, there wasn't much he would need. Stashing his pen knife in the back of his waistband for want of a bilium, he slipped out of the room. As if he was going to the bath house, he walked out the back door.

He didn't stop at the bath, but kept going. Along the firm sand of the beach near the tide line, and up towards the headland. After half an hour, he paused by a rock pool. There he pried open a couple of oysters, picking out the sweet meat.

Perhaps two or three hours or more along, it didn't really matter, he saw a stream running down to the beach. Kip turned, and followed it up into the jungle. Vaguely, he was thinking to find an abandoned village. Any village required fresh water, and even if people had moved away there might be good wood trees around.

He arrived at a village only minutes later, small but clearly occupied. A woman looked up from her garden, and smiled at him. “I bring a fire for your hearth! I bring news of the Wide Seas! Have you any problems for me?” he said. Kip didn't question why he had chosen that greeting.

She grinned back at him. “The young tanà comes, eh? Here, help me with the water spinach.”

So Kip did, weeding around the garden, turning the compost pile, feeding the pigs. At about noon, the woman said, “Why did you come to this corner of the island?”

“I was looking for wood to make a vaha, I'm going home,” he said, with a smile, “But I am glad to help.” And it was true. There was something soothing about physical labour. Especially when he got to eat the results.

After lunch, she introduced him to the rest of the tiny village. Just five families, and the yam house. The children, she explained, were at school in another village, up the path. Kip smiled, and said hello to everyone. They didn't really have any issues for the tanà, but they were happy to share their wood with him, in exchange for helping repair one of the homes. It hadn't been lived in for a while, but there was an older couple who could do with a space away from their grandchildren sometimes.

Kip agreed of course. Sometimes the work of the tanà, building the community, was more literal than other times. That was perfectly fine with him.

The people of the village had metal tools to lend him, which certainly made the tasks faster. The roof was repaired in very little time, only cross-struts needed replacing. The walls were in surprisingly good condition, but Kip also helped repair some of the floor boards which had bent out of shape over time.

By the evening, he was looking at the seasoned wood, trying to decide what piece might be best for his main keel. He only knew one pattern, but he knew it back-to-front and inside-out, after so many repairs. The village had a vaha in a local pattern, which Kip admired, but didn't dare risk copying. Maybe when he got to Loaloa he could ask the elders to teach him their patterns. Perhaps it would take a month to build this vaha. That was fine, he would work on his vaha when nobody had anything else for him to help with in the village. 

The children came back from school just as night really fell. Everyone was happy, sharing food and stories of their day. There was a somewhat confused story from the children about a velio man who had gone missing, or perhaps a wontok, they weren't quite sure. It was somewhat lost in the excitement of getting to meet the young tanà, Kip of Loaloa. Maybe if the other village couldn't find the lost man, Kip should go and help them? Well, if the search reached that far, he would get involved.

Kip sang tales from the _Lays_ to the children as the stars came out. Asking them in turn what their ke'a was, what their favourite star-stories were. Two families offered for Kip to come and sleep in their houses, but he took himself off to an unused outbuilding, an old style of shelter now used for drying plants.

Some days later, Kip wasn't bothering to keep track, a speaker of a neighbouring village came to visit. Jiano seemed pleasant, but Kip was busy replacing the thatch on the out-of-use house. He watched Jiano talk, but didn't really listen. He didn't want to fall off the roof because he wasn't paying attention, that could have been fatal.

After a while, one of the men, Mian, called up to him, “Feeling hungry, Kip? There's some roast plantain and fresh papaya salad.”

“Sounds lovely,” Kip called back, and carefully manoeuvred himself down to the house platform.

Jiano was standing with Mian, and Kip waved at them both. “Are you staying for dinner, Jiano Speaker?” asked Kip, with a smile.

Mian laughed, and said something to Jiano that Kip didn't quite understand. Perhaps there was a local dialect that was quite different? Kip knew some Isolate ones were nearly unintelligible, but surely everyone in the Ring, particularly so close to Gorjo city, were reasonably similar. Certainly everyone had been able to understand and be understood by Kip before. Jiano replied in the same, but turned to Kip, as if to include him in a joke.

Kip looked between them, confused, “I'm sorry, I don't think I know that dialect,” he said, “It seems familiar though? Is it from the Western Ring somewhere?”

Jiano looked startled. “What?” he said, “Is this some kind of joke Cliopher?”

Now Kip was even more lost. Why would they have expected him to know this dialect, was it one his father's family spoke? Also, why had Jiano call him _Cliopher_? That was his uncle's name, nobody called *him* that.

“Only my mother calls me Cliopher,” said Kip, frowning, “And only when she's very angry. I don't think she's done it since….” He paused, unable to remember when exactly, “I was a wild child, before I was sent to learn from Buru Tovo.”

Mian and Jiano shared another startled look. “Where are you planning on going when you finish the vaha?” asked Mian.

“Oh, back to Diana on Loaloa, of course,” said Kip, “You've all been very hospitable, thank you, but I will probably wear out my welcome before I finish the boat.”

“Build carefully!” said Jiano, “I can't stay, I should head back.”

Mian nodded, and Kip shrugged. “Of course, Jiano Speaker,” he said, “I've been hurt by poor repairs too often to build shoddy work.”

Kip had dinner, and then worked on the vaha for a while. The next morning, when someone else was ready to work on the roof again, he dropped the boat construction, and helped them instead. In the afternoon, he was helping weed another garden.

Looking up, hands full of young grasses, he was surprised to see Jiano had returned. With him was a woman, and two men who were very obviously velioi. One of them was short, and looked like a walking painting, so perfect were his posture and clothes. The other man was tall, in a kilt, with a spear. Kip squatted back on his haunches to wait and see what this was all about. Was the short man the one who was lost? He certainly didn't look like he had been living rough, neither of them did.

“Kip?” called Jiano, as they entered the centre of the village.

Kip put the weeds into the compost, dusted off his hands on his trousers, and called, “Coming, Jiano.” He walked over casually to join the four of them.

The short man looked exasperated. He said something which sounded scathing, but which Kip didn't understand at all. In fact, it sounded like the language Mian and Jiano were speaking the day before. Perhaps it was a language out of Jilkano then? Maybe that's why it sounded familiar, he heard some of it on his way back after the Fall.

“I'm sorry, I don't understand,” said Kip, looking from the short man to Jiano, “Would you mind translating? Unless you speak this language perio?”

Now the short man looked angry. The tall man with the spear placed a gentle hand on his shoulder, and said something soft and calming. The short man snapped back at him, and shook the hand off.

“You don't recognise them, Kip?” asked Jiano, confused.

“No…?” said Kip, trying to rack his brain. The short man had tattoos, which reminded him of the old Imperial high nobles. But what would one of them possibly have been doing on an island in the Vangaveye-ve?

Jiano and the woman looked worried. The short man went very still, and the man with the spear looked alert but calm. The short man asked a question, looking between Kip and Jiano.

“Lord Conju was wondering if you remember where you live?” asked Jiano.

“My island is Loaloa,” said Kip, confidently, “Although I haven't lived there in a while. I did when I was a boy, but then I moved back to live with my mother.”

“Not in Astandalas?” asked Jiano, “Or Solaara?”

“No,” said Kip, with a sigh, “I did, when I worked in the Imperial Bureaucratic Service.”

Jiano translated for the two velioi, and then probed further, “When was that?”

“Oh, a long time ago now, surely,” said Kip, with a smile.

There was a pause after Jiano translated this latest statement. The short man asked another question, his face devoid of emotion, but he stared at Kip.

“Why did you leave?” translated Jiano.

“I was fired,” said Kip, ruefully, “Although I was probably lucky I wasn't executed, I was always awful at etiquette, which was very serious in Astandalas.”

Once Jiano translated that, the two velioi looked confused. They chatted a bit to each other. This was, Kip thought, an extremely bizarre situation. Why had two velioi come all the way out here to grill him about his old job?

By this point, the rest of the village had gathered around to listen. That made Kip feel even more awkward, that they should all be listening in. It seemed even the kids knew this other language, and Kip felt a bit strange. Was this a new change in the education system? Was everyone learning another language? Why had nobody thought to tell Kip?

One of the kids went running of to their home, and then came back with one of their school books. They flipped through a few pages, and then handed it proudly to Jiano.

“Do you recognise this person?” Jiano asked.

Kip laughed, “Of course!” he said, “That's Emperor Artorin Damara, everyone knows that picture. It's the one people put in their shrines. Although, he probably looks a bit older now, that was his coronation portrait.”

“Correct!” said the kid, and they took their schoolbook back.

Mian was now standing on the other side of Jiano. “Would you mind going back with Jiano?” he said, cautiously, “I know you want to finish the vaha, we'll keep all your work out of the rain so it's ready when you come back. But we think you should talk to a shaman, and the only one on Lesuia is closer to their village.”

“Thank you, but why?” asked Kip, puzzled, “I don't have any magic.”

“You should be able to understand the other language,” said Jiano, “And you should recognise these two velioi. We're worried something has happened to mess with your memories, maybe magical, maybe divine. There was a lunar eclipse not long ago.”

If that was true, it explained a lot about what was going on, and why everyone seemed confused and distressed by him today. If he'd somehow tripped into a stray bit of magic that had messed with his memories, that was very worrying. They should see if they could get the shamans to check the island for any more.

“That does sound very bad. I don't have any magic myself, I don't suppose anyone here has enough to tell if I'm… contagious or anything?” asked Kip, suddenly worried that he might have hurt the little village.

Mian smiled, “You're not, there's a lot of magic wrapped around you, but it's very tidy. I'm sure the shaman will be able to tell more.”

Kip sighed with relief. “All right,” he said, “Shall we go then? And thank you, Jiano, for offering to help me.”

“No problem, Kip,” said Jiano, “Let's go, before it gets too late.”

The five of them walked back to Jiano's village. It was called Ikialo, which was also something Kip should have known. He also should have recognised the woman, Aya, who was Jiano's wife, but more worryingly was also his own second-cousin once removed. If he was starting to forget family members, this was serious.

“We'll send word to the shaman today,” said Jiano, “But they might not be able to come until tomorrow.”

“Of course,” said Kip, “I'm sure they're very busy.” He noticed dinner preparations were well underway in Ikialo. “Is there anything I can do to help?”

“Don't worry, hani,” said Aya, with a smile, “Come join us and trade stories for food.”

The two velioi waved at them, and took a different path out of Ikialo. Kip joined Jiano and Aya for dinner, and tried not to worry about the strange issue of his memory, and the potential magic. He couldn't do anything about it at the moment, couldn't even sense there was anything wrong. Tomorrow would be soon enough.
