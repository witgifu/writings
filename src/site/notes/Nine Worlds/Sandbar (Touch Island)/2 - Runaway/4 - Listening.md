There was a knock on the door. There hadn't been the stamp of spears, but it sounded like a guard. Instinctively, Cliopher stood, and backed away from the door. Conju looked at him, and then went to open the door. He let Ludvic in.

“He asked to see you, if you are feeling up to it,” said Ludvic, quietly.

“Of course,” said Cliopher, quickly wiping his face with a damp cloth from the washstand. He was still in what he had worn to dinner, and a quick glance showed he hadn't spilt anything on it.

Ludvic led him back to the main room, with Conju trailing them. As he had before dinner, Cliopher walked into the room the correct distance, and then dropped into the obeisance. His Radiancy was not reading this time, and gestured him up before he had done more than bend, but Cliopher completed it anyway. Then he returned to standing, and waited for his lord to speak.

“Do you remember what happened, the morning after the lunar eclipse?” asked his Radiancy. His voice was measured, serene, perfectly controlled. It sounded like his voice had when they both looked over the beautiful map of the nine worlds, all those weeks ago.

“Yes, my lord,” said Cliopher, carefully keeping his eyes on his lord's hands. They were still beautiful. Looking at them now, Cliopher was reminded how they had looked, gleaming in the afterglow of the Moon Lady. How soft they had seemed, held in Cliopher's hands. How they had— no. He should not remember that.

“What do you remember?” his Radiancy asked.

“I disagreed with you, my lord. You dismissed me from your service, and so I left,” said Cliopher, trying to remain factual. Trying _not_ to cry again, nor to apologise, nor to restart the argument.

His Radiancy's hands briefly tensed, and Cliopher quickly added, “My eternal thanks, most merciful lord, for allowing me to return.” It wasn't, strictly speaking, an apology, although it veered close to one.

“Of course, Cliopher, I—” his Radiancy paused, and Cliopher worried that he had been expecting an apology, and was now uncertain what to say. 

“I am most grateful…” continued his Radiancy, just as Cliopher said, “I apologise, my lord, it was not my place to disagree with you.”

“Cliopher,” said his Radiancy, and there was a note of pleading there, “It is _always_ your place to disagree with me. At any time, about anything. But most especially when I am denying that your feelings are true.”

This was so unexpected, so outside the bounds of any of the things Cliopher had thought his Radiancy might say, that he startled. In doing so, he looked up into the eyes of his Radiancy. Perhaps, he thought slightly inanely, if anything had enchanted him, it was those beautiful eyes, nine hundred years ago. There was the man to whom Cliopher could deny nothing, would do anything for.

“I love you,” he said, to the surprise of everyone in the room, including himself.

Cliopher hastened to add, “But I know I can be a lot, too much, often,” he looked back down, “I don't expect anything, my lord.”

His Radiancy's hands slid from his lap, to the arms of the chair, and he stood. Gracefully as always. Two steps forward, his Radiancy made, before Cliopher realised. Quickly, he stepped sideways and back, to make way for his lord.

“Cliopher,” said his Radiancy, softly, that same note of pleading.

Nine hundred years together told him to take another step back, his lord was too close. He almost did, but his fingertips brushed his golden palm, reminding him that he didn't _have_ to. That if his lord wanted to, it was safe.

“Yes, my lord,” said Cliopher, unable to resist looking up again. Those eyes seemed to reel him in, a crab in a pot, rising to the surface and the sunlight. He took a step forward, instead of back.

“What do you want?” whispered his Radiancy.

“I—” began Cliopher, his mind spinning. Did he dare be honest? Would he be sent away again? But it was impossible to lie, now that he was caught in those eyes. “I want to stand with you, and reform the government until it doesn't need you, and help you find the next Lord Magus, and burn down the taboos. I want to set you free,” he whispered back.

“What do *you* want?” whispered his Radiancy again.

“I want you to be happy,” said Cliopher, barely more than a breath.

His Radiancy shook his head, “No, Cliopher. What do you want for yourself?”

Cliopher tried to look away, to hide himself, his feelings. This wasn't professional, it wasn't correct, he was not just skirting close to the line but running across it at full speed and heading towards the cliff. He wasn't sure he could pull back this time.

“You,” he mouthed, but now there was no sound. He gripped his hands together, pressing his thumb hard into his gold palm.

His Radiancy stepped forward again. Gently, so gently, like Cliopher was a bird that might fly away, his lord reached out one hand to rest gently on top of Cliopher's clenched ones.

“I'm here,” said his Radiancy. Only no, it wasn't. That was Tor. 

Cliopher tried to calm his heart, to slow his breathing that was too fast and too shallow. “Thank you,” he whispered, biting his lips, “But please, if you only took me back out of pity, or fear of what I might do, please, I do not want to hurt you, my lord. I will go, I would be fine.”

“As soon as Conju reported you were missing, I regretted what I had said. That I had listened to my own fears first, rather than you,” said Tor, gently, “I am so very sorry, Kip.”

Cliopher wiped his eyes on his sleeve, even though he was sure Conju was judging him somewhere for doing so. “I forgive you,” he said.

“Thank you,” said Tor, “I'm not sure if I deserve it.” He looked down at their hands for a long time.

“That's why it's not up to you,” said Cliopher, with a small smile.

Tor looked up, and laughed. Just a small chuckle, such as Cliopher might have gotten many times over the years. But when emotions were this high, and this charged, it felt like a weir opening after a flood.

Then he looked back down at their hands. “When we get back, the Ouranatha are going to want to execute you, or make you a saint, I'm not sure which.”

Cliopher rolled his eyes. “I think I'd rather the death threats, I'm used to those. They've been trying to get a reason to execute me since you appointed me your secretary, I'm sure they were behind at least three of the assassination attempts,” said Cliopher, dryly.

“Five,” said Ser Rhodin, “That I managed to confirm. For two others they have plausible deniability.”

Cliopher looked mildly surprised. “When were those?”

“Oh, various times,” said Ser Rhodin, “They never got close enough that you were a witness, so I didn't bother you.”

“Still,” said Tor, “I, and I am sure Ser Rhodin as well, would rather you faced *fewer* violent threats, rather than more. I am not entirely sure that we will be able to convince them that there hasn't been divine action, the stamp on you is quite clear.”

Cliopher grimaced. “And they don't really like that there are gods outside their own set, so if I told them it was Vou'a, I suppose they would ignore it and claim it was your miracle.”

Tor inclined his head, “It is likely.” He looked just as put out by this as Cliopher felt. Cliopher knew that Tor didn't like, or agree with, the Imperial Cult, but was forced to accept it. Having any evidence of his divinity, rather than just his impressive magical power, would be pouring oil on the fire of the Cult.

“Could we perhaps claim the Moon Lady was involved?” asked Cliopher tentatively, “It is not entirely untrue, and I am sure some reports of unusual astronomical activity will make to Solaara from the University of the Wide Seas.”

Tor looked slightly taken aback. Then thoughtful, then worried. He grasped Cliopher's hands ever so slightly tighter.

“My lord,” ventured Conju, “If it were assumed to be the work of the Moon Lady, the conclusion would be that she had acted to provide you a lover.”

Cliopher realised that while Conju had addressed this statement at their lord, he had actually intended it for Cliopher. This was the source of worry for Tor.

“I did not mean to be so presumptuous,” said Cliopher, looking from Conju to Tor in anxiety.

Tor closed his eyes, and sighed. “Cliopher,” he said, and then stopped. He breathed slowly, but Cliopher could feel his pulse rising.

“Tor,” said Cliopher, “Are you trying to decide which I would prefer, or which *you* would prefer? You can ask me, although I hope you could guess.”

It had been an evening, a day, full of surprises. Yet one more suddenly appeared before Cliopher. Tor dropped to one knee in front of him, still gripping to Cliopher's hands. “Cliopher,” he said, his voice creaking slightly, “If you have any doubt at all, please say no. But I would rather make an honest man of my beloved. Will you marry me?”

Cliopher's eyes went wide, and he stopped breathing. In contrast to his heart, which seemed determined to set some kind of record. “Yes, my, my beloved,” he gasped. The room was going slightly grey and sparkly.

“My lord, I think Cliopher better sit before he faints,” said Conju, sounding unusually anxious.

Was he going to faint? Cliopher wasn't sure. He staggered back slightly, but as Tor's hand started to slip off of his, he turned his hands to grab both of Tor's. This caused him to over balance further, and Tor to follow. They fell in and awkward tumble onto a couch.

Conju hovered with a glass of water, not wanting to risk touching his lord, but hoping to reach Cliopher. Tor put out an open, flat palm, in the pose he used when it was not possible to pass something more safely. Conju gently placed the glass on it, holding only the rim.

Tor grasped it firmly, and held it up for Cliopher to sip. “Are you sure Kip?” he asked, “Please don't make a hasty call. Consider it carefully over the next couple of days. Once we tell the Ouranatha that the moon has brought us together as equals, it will be near impossible to change, and I do not want to trap you with me.”

Cliopher sipped thankfully, and the room regained some of its colour and sound. Once he had regained some of his more usual breathing and pulse, he said, “Yes, my answer will still be yes. I choose you, my Tor, if you will have me.”

*Equals*. Tor had said it, not Cliopher. Could it be true, was it possible? He had hoped, at most, to be some comfort to his lord. A friend, perhaps, which already seemed very daring. *Fanoa* said Cliopher's heart, the word he had cut from his vocabulary, smothered even in his thoughts, for so many years.

“I am going to ask you again every day until we arrive in Solaara,” said Tor, but now with affection. “Not because I am not listening now, Cliopher, but in case you change your mind. And yes, because I love you, and I am afraid.”

Cliopher tucked his head under Tor's chin. “And I will say yes, because I love you, and I am not afraid.”
