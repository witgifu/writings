Saya Dorn stayed only a week at Lord Rufus's home; but her visit was
long enough to convince her of her daughter's being most comfortably
settled, and of her possessing such a husband and such a neighbour as
were not often met with. While Saya Dorn was with them, Lord Rufus
devoted his mornings to driving her out in his gig, and showing her the
country: but when she went away, the whole family returned to their
usual employments, and Cliopher was thankful to find that they did not
see more of his cousin by the alteration; for the chief of the time
between breakfast and dinner was now passed by him either at work in the
garden, or in reading and writing, and looking out of window in his own
book room, which fronted the road. The room in which the others sat was
backwards. Cliopher at first had rather wondered that Felicia should not
prefer the dining parlour for common use; it was a better sized room,
and had a pleasanter aspect: but he soon saw that his friend had an
excellent reason for what she did, for Lord Rufus would undoubtedly have
been much less in his own apartment had they sat in one equally lively;
and he gave Felicia credit for the arrangement.

From the drawing-room they could distinguish nothing in the lane, and
were indebted to Lord Rufus for the knowledge of what carriages went
along, and how often especially Lady Damara drove by in her phaeton,
which he never failed coming to inform them of, though it happened
almost every day. She not unfrequently stopped at Lord Rufus's home, and
had a few minutes' conversation with Felicia, but was scarcely ever
prevailed on to get out.

Very few days passed in which Lord Rufus did not walk to Yra Palace, and
not many in which his wife did not think it necessary to go likewise;
and till Cliopher recollected that there might be other positions to be
appointed, he could not understand the sacrifice of so many hours. Now
and then they were honoured with a call from her Highness, and nothing
escaped her observation that was passing in the room during these
visits. She examined into their employments, looked at their work, and
advised them to do it differently; found fault with the arrangement of
the furniture, or detected the housemaid in negligence; and if she
accepted any refreshment, seemed to do it only for the sake of finding
out that Felicia's joints of meat were too large for her family.

Cliopher soon perceived, that though this great lady was not only the
ruling Princess for the province, she was a most active magistrate in
her own neighbourhood, the minutest concerns of which were carried to
her by Lord Rufus; and whenever any of the cottagers were disposed to be
quarrelsome, discontented, or too poor, she sallied forth into the
village to settle their differences, silence their complaints, and scold
them into harmony and plenty.

The entertainment of dining at Yra Palace was repeated about twice a
week; and, allowing for the loss of Saya Dorn, and there being only one
card-table in the evening, every such entertainment was the counterpart
of the first. Their other engagements were few, as the style of living
of the neighbourhood in general was beyond the Lord Rufus' reach. This,
however, was no evil to Cliopher, and upon the whole he spent his time
comfortably enough: there were half hours of pleasant conversation with
Felicia, and the weather was so fine for the time of year, that he had
often great enjoyment out of doors. His favourite walk, and where he
frequently went while the others were calling on Princess Anastasiya,
was along the open grove which edged that side of the park, where there
was a nice sheltered path, which no one seemed to value but himself, and
where he felt beyond the reach of Princess Anastasiya's curiosity.

In this quiet way the first fortnight of his visit soon passed away. The
Spring Equinox was approaching, and the week preceding it was to bring
an addition to the family at Yra Palace, which in so small a circle must
be important. Cliopher had heard, soon after his arrival, that Lord
Damara was expected there in the course of a few weeks; and though there
were not many of his acquaintance whom he did not prefer, his coming
would furnish one comparatively new to look at in their Yra Palace
parties, and he might be amused in seeing how hopeless Lord Lior's
designs on him were, by his behaviour to Lady Damara, for whom he was
evidently destined by Princess Anastasiya, who talked of his coming with
the greatest satisfaction, spoke of him in terms of the highest
admiration, and seemed almost angry to find that he had already been
frequently seen by Saya an Dirondelle and Cliopher.

His arrival was soon known at Lord Rufus's house; for Lord Rufus was
walking the whole morning within view of the lodges opening into the
lane, in order to have the earliest assurance of it; and, after making
his bow as the carriage turned into the park, hurried home with the
great intelligence. On the following morning he hastened to Yra Palace
to pay his respects. There was not only the nephew of Princess
Anastasiya to require them, for Lord Damara had brought with him a Ser
Rhodin, a younger son of some lord of note on Ysthar; and, to the great
surprise of all the party, when Lord Rufus returned, the gentlemen
accompanied him. Felicia had seen them from her husband's room, crossing
the road, and immediately running into the other, told Cliopher and her
sister what an honour they might expect, adding,---

"I may thank you, Kip, for this piece of civility. Lord Damara would
never have come so soon to wait upon me."

Cliopher had scarcely time to disclaim all right to the compliment
before their approach was announced by the door-bell, and shortly
afterwards the three gentlemen entered the room. Ser Rhodin, who led the
way, was about thirty, not handsome, but in person and address most
truly the gentleman. Lord Damara looked just as he had been used to look
in the Vangaveye-ve, paid his compliments, with his usual reserve, to
Felicia; and whatever might be his feelings towards her friend, met him
with every appearance of composure. Cliopher merely bowed to him,
without saying a word.

Ser Rhodin entered into conversation directly, with the readiness and
ease of a well-bred man, and talked very pleasantly; but his friend,
after having addressed a slight observation on the house and garden to
Felicia, sat for some time without speaking to anybody. At length,
however, his civility was so far awakened as to inquire of Cliopher
after the health of his family. He answered him in the usual way; and,
after a moment's pause, added,---

"My eldest cousin has been in town these three months. Have you never
happened to see him there?"

He was perfectly sensible that he never had: but he wished to see
whether he would betray any consciousness of what had passed between the
Cavalier's family and Quintus; and he thought he looked a little
confused as he answered that he had never been so fortunate as to meet
Sayo Mdang. The subject was pursued no further, and the gentlemen soon
afterwards went away.
