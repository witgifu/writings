Cliopher related to Quintus, the next day, what had passed between Saya
Ealoapeha and himself. Quintus listened with astonishment and concern:
he knew not how to believe that Lord Damara could be so unworthy of the
Cavalier's regard; and yet it was not in his nature to question the
veracity of a young woman of such amiable appearance as Ealoapeha. The
possibility of her having really endured such unkindness was enough to
interest all his tender feelings; and nothing therefore remained to be
done but to think well of them both, to defend the conduct of each, and
throw into the account of accident or mistake whatever could not be
otherwise explained.

"They have both," said he, "been deceived, I dare say, in some way or
other, of which we can form no idea. Interested people have perhaps
misrepresented each to the other. It is, in short, impossible for us to
conjecture the causes or circumstances which may have alienated them,
without actual blame on either side."

"Very true, indeed; and now, my dear Quintus, what have you got to say
in behalf of the interested people who have probably been concerned in
the business? Do clear *them*, too, or we shall be obliged to think ill
of somebody."

"Laugh as much as you choose, but you will not laugh me out of my
opinion. My dearest Kip, do but consider in what a disgraceful light it
places Lord Damara, to be treating his father's favourite in such a
manner,---one whom his father had promised to provide for. It is
impossible. No man of common humanity, no man who had any value for his
character, could be capable of it. Can his most intimate friends be so
excessively deceived in him? Oh no."

"I can much more easily believe the Cavalier's being imposed on than
that Saya Ealoapeha should invent such a history of herself as she gave
me last night; names, facts, everything mentioned without ceremony. If
it be not so, let Lord Damara contradict it. Besides, there was truth in
her looks."

"It is difficult, indeed---it is distressing. One does not know what to
think."

"I beg your pardon;---one knows exactly what to think."

But Quintus could think with certainty on only one point,---that the
Cavalier, if he *had been* imposed on, would have much to suffer when
the affair became public.

The two young men were summoned from the shrubbery, where this
conversation passed, by the arrival of some of the very persons of whom
they had been speaking; the Cavalier and his cousins came to give their
personal invitation for the long expected party at Navikiani, which was
fixed for the following Tuesday. The two cousins were delighted to see
their dear friend again, called it an age since they had met, and
repeatedly asked what he had been doing with himself since their
separation. To the rest of the family they paid little attention;
avoiding Eidora as much as possible, saying not much to Cliopher, and
nothing at all to the others. They were soon gone again, rising from
their seats with an activity which took their cousin by surprise, and
hurrying off as if eager to escape from Eidora's civilities.

The prospect of the Navikiani party was extremely agreeable to every
young person of the family. Eidora chose to consider it as given in
compliment to her eldest nephew, and was particularly flattered by
receiving the invitation from the Cavalier himself, instead of a
ceremonious card. Quintus pictured to himself a happy evening in the
society of his two friends, and the attentions of their cousin; and
Cliopher thought with pleasure of dancing a great deal with Saya
Ealoapeha, and of seeing a confirmation of everything in Lord Damara's
look and behaviour. The happiness anticipated by Haro and Hillen
depended less on any single event, or any particular person; for though
they each, like Cliopher, meant to dance half the evening with Saya
Ealoapeha, she was by no means the only partner who could satisfy them,
and a party was, at any rate, a party. And even Louya could assure her
family that she had no disinclination for it.

"While I can have my mornings to myself," said she, "it is enough. I
think it is no sacrifice to join occasionally in evening engagements.
Society has claims on us all; and I profess myself one of those who
consider intervals of recreation and amusement as desirable for
everybody."

Cliopher's spirits were so high on the occasion, that though he did not
often speak unnecessarily to Lord Rufus, he could not help asking him
whether he intended to accept the Cavalier's invitation, and if he did,
whether he would think it proper to join in the evening's amusement; and
he was rather surprised to find that he entertained no scruple whatever
on that head, and was very far from dreading a rebuke, either from the
High Priests or Princess Anastasiya, by venturing to dance.

"I am by no means of opinion, I assure you," said he, "that a party of
this kind, given by a man of character, to respectable people, can have
any evil tendency; and I am so far from objecting to dancing myself,
that I shall hope to be honoured with the hands of all my fair cousins
in the course of the evening; and I take this opportunity of soliciting
yours, Sayo Cliopher, for the two first dances especially; a preference
which I trust my cousin Quintus will attribute to the right cause, and
not to any disrespect for him."

Cliopher felt himself completely taken in. He had fully proposed being
engaged by Ealoapeha for those very dances; and to have Lord Rufus
instead!---his liveliness had been never worse timed. There was no help
for it, however. Saya Ealoapeha's happiness and his own was perforce
delayed a little longer, and Lord Rufus's proposal accepted with as good
a grace as he could. He was not the better pleased with his gallantry,
from the idea it suggested of something more. It now first struck him,
that *he* was selected from among his cousins as worthy of being the
keeper of Lord Rufus's home, and of assisting to form a quadrille table
at Yra, in the absence of more eligible visitors. The idea soon reached
to conviction, as he observed his increasing civilities towards himself,
and heard his frequent attempt at a compliment on his wit and vivacity;
and though more astonished than gratified himself by this effect of his
charms, it was not long before his mother gave him to understand that
the probability of their marriage was exceedingly agreeable to *her*.
Cliopher, however, did not choose to take the hint, being well aware
that a serious dispute must be the consequence of any reply. Lord Rufus
might never make the offer, and, till he did, it was useless to quarrel
about him.

If there had not been a Navikiani party to prepare for and talk of, the
younger Mdangs would have been in a pitiable state at this time; for,
from the day of the invitation to the day of the party, there was such a
succession of rain as prevented their walking to Gorjo City once. No
aunt, no officers, no news could be sought after; the very shoe-roses
for Navikiani were got by proxy. Even Cliopher might have found some
trial of his patience in weather which totally suspended the improvement
of his acquaintance with Saya Ealoapeha; and nothing less than a dance
on Tuesday could have made such a Friday, Saturday, Sunday, and Monday
endurable to Haro and Hillen.
