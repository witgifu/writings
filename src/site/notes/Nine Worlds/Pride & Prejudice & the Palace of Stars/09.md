Cliopher passed the chief of the night in his cousin's room, and in the
morning had the pleasure of being able to send a tolerable answer to the
inquiries which he very early received from the Cavalier by a housemaid,
and some time afterwards from the two elegant gentlemen who waited on
his cousins. In spite of this amendment, however, he requested to have a
note sent to Tahivoa, desiring her mother to visit Quintus, and form her
own judgment of her situation. The note was immediately despatched, and
its contents as quickly complied with. Eidora Mdang, accompanied by her
two youngest nephews, reached Navikiani soon after the family breakfast.

Had she found Quintus in any apparent danger, Eidora would have been
very miserable; but being satisfied on seeing him that his illness was
not alarming, she had no wish of him recovering immediately, as his
restoration to health would probably remove him from Navikiani. She
would not listen, therefore, to her son's proposal of being carried
home; neither did the apothecary, who arrived about the same time, think
it at all advisable. After sitting a little while with Quintus, on Lord
Lior's appearance and invitation, the matriarch and three relations all
attended him into the breakfast parlour. The Cavalier met them with
hopes that Eidora Mdang had not found Quintus sayo Mdang worse than she
expected.

"Indeed I have, sir," was her answer. "He is a great deal too ill to be
moved. Sayo Jones says we must not think of moving him. We must trespass
a little longer on your kindness."

"Removed!" cried the Cavalier. "It must not be thought of. My cousin, I
am sure, will not hear of his removal."

"You may depend upon it, madam," said Lord Lior, with cold civility,
"that Sayo Mdang shall receive every possible attention while he remains
with us."

Eidora Mdang was profuse in her acknowledgments.

"I am sure," she added, "if it was not for such good friends, I do not
know what would become of him, for he is very ill indeed, and suffers a
vast deal, though with the greatest patience in the world, which is
always the way with him, for he has, without exception, the sweetest
temper I ever met with. I often tell my other nephews and nieces they
are nothing to *him*. You have a sweet room here, Cavalier an Vilius,
and a charming prospect over the beach. I do not know a place in the
Ring that is equal to Navikiani. You will not think of quitting it in a
hurry, I hope, though you have but a short lease."

"Whatever I do is done in a hurry," replied he; "and therefore if I
should resolve to quit Navikiani, I should probably be off in five
minutes. At present, however, I consider myself as quite fixed here."

"That is exactly what I should have supposed of you," said Cliopher.

"You begin to comprehend me, do you?" cried he, turning towards him.

"Oh yes---I understand you perfectly."

"I wish I might take this for a compliment; but to be so easily seen
through, I am afraid, is pitiful."

"That is as it happens. It does not necessarily follow that a deep,
intricate character is more or less estimable than such a one as yours."

"Kip," cried her mother, "remember where you are, and do not run on in
the wild manner that you are suffered to do at home."

"I did not know before," continued the Cavalier, immediately, "that you
were a studier of character. It must be an amusing study."

"Yes; but intricate characters are the *most* amusing. They have at
least that advantage."

"The provinces," said Lord Damara, "can in general supply but few
subjects for such a study. In a provincial neighbourhood you move in a
very confined and unvarying society."

"But people themselves alter so much, that there is something new to be
observed in them for ever."

"Yes, indeed," cried Eidora, offended by his manner of mentioning a
provincial neighbourhood. "I assure you there is quite as much of *that*
going on in the country as in town."

Everybody was surprised; and Lord Damara, after looking at her for a
moment, turned silently away. Eidora, who fancied she had gained a
complete victory over him, continued her triumph,---

"I cannot see that Solaara has any great advantage over the provinces,
for my part, except the shops and public places. The provinces are a
vast deal pleasanter, is not it, Cavalier an Vilius?"

"When I am in the provinces," he replied, "I never wish to leave; and
when I am in town, it is pretty much the same. They have each their
advantages, and I can be equally happy in either."

"Ay, that is because you have the right disposition. But that
gentleman," looking at Lord Damara, "seemed to think the provinces were
nothing at all."

"Indeed, mamma, you are mistaken," said Cliopher, blushing for his
mother. "You quite mistook my lord. He only meant that there was not
such a variety of people to be met with in the provinces as in town,
which you must acknowledge to be true."

"Certainly, my dear, nobody said there were; but as to not meeting with
many people in this neighbourhood, I believe there are few
neighbourhoods larger. I know we dine with four-and-twenty families."

Nothing but concern for Cliopher could enable the Cavalier to keep his
countenance. His cousin was less delicate, and directed her eye towards
Lord Damara with a very expressive smile. Cliopher, for the sake of
saying something that might turn his mother's thoughts, now asked her if
Felicia an Dirondelle had been at Tahivoa since *his* coming away.

"Yes, she called yesterday with her mother. What an agreeable woman Saya
Dorn is, Cavalier an Vilius---is not she? so much the woman of fashion!
so genteel and so easy! She has always something to say to everybody.
*That* is my idea of good breeding; and those persons who fancy
themselves very important and never open their mouths quite mistake the
matter."

"Did Felicia dine with you?"

"No, she would go home. I fancy she was wanted about the mince-pies. For
my part, Cavalier an Vilius, *I* always keep people that can do their
own work; *my* family are brought up differently. But everybody is to
judge for themselves, and the an Dirondelle family are a very good sort,
I assure you. It is a pity they are not handsome! Not that *I* think
Felicia so *very* plain; but then she is our particular friend."

"She seems a very pleasant woman," said the Cavalier.

"Oh dear, yes; but you must own she is very plain. Lady an Dirondelle
herself has often said so, and envied me Quintus's beauty. I do not like
to boast of my own family; but to be sure, Quintus---one does not often
see anybody better looking. It is what everybody says. I do not trust my
own partiality. When he was only fifteen there was a gentleman at my
sister Oura Mdang's in town so much in love with him, that my
brother-in-law was sure he would make her an offer before we came away.
But, however, he did not. Perhaps he thought him too young. However, he
wrote some verses on him, and very pretty they were."

"And so ended his affection," said Cliopher, impatiently. "There has
been many a one, I fancy, overcome in the same way. I wonder who first
discovered the efficacy of poetry in driving away love!"

"I have been used to consider poetry as the *food* of love," said Lord
Damara.

"Of a fine, stout, healthy love it may. Everything nourishes what is
strong already. But if it be only a slight, thin sort of inclination, I
am convinced that one good sonnet will starve it entirely away."

Lord Damara only smiled; and the general pause which ensued made
Cliopher tremble lest his mother should be exposing herself again. He
longed to speak, but could think of nothing to say; and after a short
silence Eidora Mdang began repeating her thanks to the Cavalier for his
kindness to Quintus, with an apology for troubling him also with Kip.
The Cavalier was unaffectedly civil in his answer, and forced his
younger cousin to be civil also, and say what the occasion required. He
performed his part, indeed, without much graciousness, but Eidora Mdang
was satisfied, and soon afterwards ordered her yacht. Upon this signal,
the youngest of her nephews put himself forward. The two boys had been
whispering to each other during the whole visit; and the result of it
was, that the youngest should tax the Cavalier with having promised on
his first coming into the country to give a party at Navikiani.

Hillen was a stout, well-grown boy of fifteen, with a fine complexion
and good-humoured countenance; a favourite with his aunt and mother,
whose affection had brought him into public at an early age. He had high
animal spirits, and a sort of natural self-consequence, which the
attentions of the officers, to whom his uncle's good dinners and his own
easy manners recommended him, had increased into assurance. He was very
equal, therefore, to address the Cavalier on the subject of the party,
and abruptly reminded him of his promise; adding, that it would be the
most shameful thing in the world if he did not keep it. His answer to
this sudden attack was delightful to his aunt's ear.

"I am perfectly ready, I assure you, to keep my engagement; and, when
your cousin is recovered, you shall, if you please, name the very day of
the party. But you would not wish to be dancing while he is ill?"

Hillen declared himself satisfied. "Oh yes---it would be much better to
wait till Quintus was well; and by that time, most likely, Lieutenant
Carter would be at Gorjo City again. And when you have given *your*
party," he added, "I shall insist on their giving one also. I shall tell
Captain Forster it will be quite a shame if she does not."

Eidora Mdang and her nephews then departed, and Cliopher returned
instantly to Quintus, leaving his own and his relations' behaviour to
the remarks of the two cousins and Lord Damara; the latter of whom,
however, could not be prevailed on to join in their censure of *him*, in
spite of all Lord Lior's witticisms on *fine eyes*.
