Aunt Oura's caution to Cliopher was punctually and kindly given on the
first favourable opportunity of speaking to him alone: after honestly
telling him what she thought, she thus went on:---

"You are too sensible a boy, Kip, to fall in love merely because you are
warned against it; and, therefore, I am not afraid of speaking openly.
Seriously, I would have you be on your guard. Do not involve yourself,
or endeavour to involve her, in an affection which the want of fortune
would make so very imprudent. I have nothing to say against *her*: she
is a most interesting young woman; and if she had the fortune she ought
to have, I should think you could not do better. But as it is---you must
not let your fancy run away with you. You have sense, and we all expect
you to use it. Your Buru Tovo would depend on *your* resolution and good
conduct, I am sure. You must not disappoint him."

"My dear aunt, this is being serious indeed."

"Yes, and I hope to engage you to be serious likewise."

"Well, then, you need not be under any alarm. I will take care of
myself, and of Saya Ealoapeha too. She shall not be in love with me, if
I can prevent it."

"Cliopher, you are not serious now."

"I beg your pardon. I will try again. At present I am not in love with
Saya Ealoapeha; no, I certainly am not. But she is, beyond all
comparison, the most agreeable woman I ever saw---and if she becomes
really attached to me---I believe it will be better that she should not.
I see the imprudence of it. Oh, *that* abominable Lord Damara! My Buru
Tovo's opinion of me does me the greatest honour; and I should be
miserable to forfeit it. My Buru Tovo, however, is partial to Saya
Ealoapeha. In short, my dear aunt, I should be very sorry to be the
means of making any of you unhappy; but since we see, every day, that
where there is affection people are seldom withheld, by immediate want
of fortune, from entering into engagements with each other, how can I
promise to be wiser than so many of my fellow-creatures, if I am
tempted, or how am I even to know that it would be wiser to resist? All
that I can promise you, therefore, is not to be in a hurry. I will not
be in a hurry to believe myself her first object. When I am in company
with her, I will not be wishing. In short, I will do my best."

"Perhaps it will be as well if you discourage her coming here so very
often. At least you should not *remind* your mother of inviting her."

"As I did the other day," said Cliopher, with a conscious smile; "very
true, it will be wise in me to refrain from *that*. But do not imagine
that she is always here so often. It is on your account that she has
been so frequently invited this week. You know my mother's ideas as to
the necessity of constant company for her friends. But really, and upon
my honour, I will try to do what I think to be wisest; and now I hope
you are satisfied."

His aunt assured him that she was; and Cliopher, having thanked her for
the kindness of her hints, they parted,---a wonderful instance of advice
being given on such a point without being resented.

Lord Rufus returned into the Vangaveye-ve soon after it had been quitted
by Aunt Oura and Quintus; but, as he took up his abode with the an
Dirondellees, his arrival was no great inconvenience to Eidora Mdang.
His marriage was now fast approaching; and she was at length so far
resigned as to think it inevitable, and even repeatedly to say, in an
ill-natured tone, that she "*wished* they might be happy." Thursday was
to be the wedding-day, and on Wednesday Saya an Dirondelle paid her
farewell visit; and when she rose to take leave, Cliopher, ashamed of
his mother's ungracious and reluctant good wishes, and sincerely
affected himself, accompanied her out of the room. As they went down
stairs together, Felicia said,---

"I shall depend on hearing from you very often, Kip."

"*That* you certainly shall."

"And I have another favour to ask. Will you come and see me?"

"We shall often meet, I hope, in the Vangaveye-ve."

"I am not likely to leave Xiputl for some time. Promise me, therefore,
to come to visit me."

Cliopher could not refuse, though he foresaw little pleasure in the
visit.

"My father and Rusticiana are to come to me in March," added Felicia,
"and I hope you will consent to be of the party. Indeed, Kip, you will
be as welcome to me as either of them."

The wedding took place: the bride and bridegroom set off for Xiputl from
the wedding party, and everybody had as much to say or to hear on the
subject as usual. Cliopher soon heard from his friend, and their
correspondence was as regular and frequent as it ever had been: that it
should be equally unreserved was impossible. Cliopher could never
address her without feeling that all the comfort of intimacy was over;
and, though determined not to slacken as a correspondent, it was for the
sake of what had been rather than what was. Felicia's first letters were
received with a good deal of eagerness: there could not but be curiosity
to know how she would speak of her new home, how she would like Princess
Anastasiya, and how happy she would dare pronounce herself to be;
though, when the letters were read, Cliopher felt that Felicia expressed
herself on every point exactly as she might have foreseen. She wrote
cheerfully, seemed surrounded with comforts, and mentioned nothing which
she could not praise. The house, furniture, neighbourhood, and roads,
were all to her taste, and Princess Anastasiya's behaviour was most
friendly and obliging. It was Lord Rufus's picture of his house and Yra
Palace rationally softened; and Cliopher perceived that he must wait for
his own visit there, to know the rest.

Quintus had already written a few lines to his cousin, to announce their
safe arrival in Solaara; and when he wrote again, Cliopher hoped it
would be in his power to say something of the Cavalier's family.

His impatience for this second letter was as well rewarded as impatience
generally is. Quintus had been a week in town, without either seeing or
hearing from Lionel. He accounted for it, however, by supposing that his
last letter to his friend from Tahivoa had by some accident been lost.

"My aunt," she continued, "is going to-morrow into that part of the
town, and I shall take the opportunity of calling in Grosvenor Street."

He wrote again when the visit was paid, and he had seen Lord Lior. "I
did not think Lionel in spirits," were his words, "but he was very glad
to see me, and reproached me for giving him no notice of my coming to
Solaara. I was right, therefore; my last letter had never reached him. I
inquired after their cousin, of course. He was well, but so much engaged
with Lord Damara that they scarcely ever saw him. I found that Lady
Damara was expected to dinner: I wish I could see her. My visit was not
long, as Lionel and Prince Belu were going out. I dare say I shall soon
see them here."

Cliopher shook his head over this letter. It convinced him that accident
only could discover to the Cavalier her cousin's being in town.

Four weeks passed away, and Quintus saw nothing of him. He endeavoured
to persuade herself that he did not regret it; but he could no longer be
blind to Lord Lior's inattention. After waiting at home every morning
for a fortnight, and inventing every evening a fresh excuse for him, the
visitor did at last appear; but the shortness of his stay, and, yet
more, the alteration of his manner, would allow Quintus to deceive
himself no longer. The letter which he wrote on this occasion to his
cousin will prove what he felt:---

"My dearest Kip will, I am sure, be incapable of triumphing in his
better judgment, at my expense, when I confess myself to have been
entirely deceived in Lord Lior's regard for me. But, my dear cousin,
though the event has proved you right, do not think me obstinate if I
still assert that, considering what his behaviour was, my confidence was
as natural as your suspicion. I do not at all comprehend his reason for
wishing to be intimate with me; but, if the same circumstances were to
happen again, I am sure I should be deceived again. Lionel did not
return my visit till yesterday; and not a note, not a line, did I
receive in the meantime. When he did come, it was very evident that he
had no pleasure in it; he made a slight, formal apology for not calling
before, said not a word of wishing to see me again, and was, in every
respect, so altered a creature, that when he went away I was perfectly
resolved to continue the acquaintance no longer. I pity, though I cannot
help blaming, him. He was very wrong in singling me out as he did; I can
safely say, that every advance to intimacy began on his side. But I pity
him, because he must feel that he has been acting wrong, and because I
am very sure that anxiety for his cousin is the cause of it. I need not
explain myself farther; and though *we* know this anxiety to be quite
needless, yet if he feels it, it will easily account for his behaviour
to me; and so deservedly dear as he is to his cousin, whatever anxiety
he may feel on his behalf is natural and amiable. I cannot but wonder,
however, at his having any such fears now, because if he had at all
cared about me, we must have met long, long ago. He knows of my being in
town, I am certain, from something Lionel said himself; and yet it would
seem, by his manner of talking, as if he wanted to persuade himself that
he is really partial to Lady Damara. I cannot understand it. If I were
not afraid of judging harshly, I should be almost tempted to say, that
there is a strong appearance of duplicity in all this. I will endeavour
to banish every painful thought, and think only of what will make me
happy, your affection, and the invariable kindness of my dear aunt. Let
me hear from you very soon. Lord Lior said something of his never
returning to Tahivoa again, of giving up the house, but not with any
certainty. We had better not mention it. I am extremely glad that you
have such pleasant accounts from our friends in Xiputl. Pray go to see
them, with Saya Dorn and Rusticiana. I am sure you will be very
comfortable there.

"Yours, etc."

This letter gave Cliopher some pain; but his spirits returned, as he
considered that Quintus would no longer be duped, by the cousin at
least. All expectation from the Cavalier an Vilius was now absolutely
over. He would not even wish for any renewal of his attentions. His
character sunk on every review of it; and, as a punishment for him, as
well as a possible advantage to Quintus, he seriously hoped he might
really soon marry Lord Damara's sister, as, by Ealoapeha's account, she
would make him abundantly regret what he had thrown away.

Aunt Oura about this time reminded Cliopher of his promise concerning
that lady, and required information; and Cliopher had such to send as
might rather give contentment to his aunt than to himself. Her apparent
partiality had subsided, her attentions were over, she was the admirer
of some one else. Cliopher was watchful enough to see it all, but he
could see it and write of it without material pain. His heart had been
but slightly touched, and his vanity was satisfied with believing that
*he* would have been his her choice, had fortune permitted it. The
sudden acquisition of ten thousand pounds was the most remarkable charm
of the young man to whom she was now rendering herself agreeable; but
Cliopher, less clear-sighted perhaps in this case than in Felicia's, did
not quarrel with her for his wish of independence. Nothing, on the
contrary, could be more natural; and, while able to suppose that it cost
her a few struggles to relinquish him, he was ready to allow it a wise
and desirable measure for both, and could very sincerely wish her happy.

All this was acknowledged to Aunt Oura; and, after relating the
circumstances, he thus went on:---"I am now convinced, my dear aunt,
that I have never been much in love; for had I really experienced that
pure and elevating passion, I should at present detest her very name,
and wish her all manner of evil. But my feelings are not only cordial
towards *her*, they are even impartial towards Sayo King. I cannot find
out that I hate him at all, or that I am in the least unwilling to think
him a very good sort of boy. There can be no love in all this. My
watchfulness has been effectual; and though I should certainly be a more
interesting object to all my acquaintance, were I distractedly in love
with her, I cannot say that I regret my comparative insignificance.
Importance may sometimes be purchased too dearly. Haro and Hillen take
her defection much more to heart than I do. They are young in the ways
of the world, and not yet open to the mortifying conviction that
handsome young women must have something to live on as well as the
plain."
