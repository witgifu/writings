The discomposure of spirits which this extraordinary visit threw
Cliopher into could not be easily overcome; nor could he for many hours
learn to think of it less than incessantly. Princess Anastasiya, it
appeared, had actually taken the trouble of this journey from Yra Palace
for the sole purpose of breaking off his supposed engagement with Lord
Damara. It was a rational scheme, to be sure! but from what the report
of their engagement could originate, Cliopher was at a loss to imagine;
till he recollected that *his* being the intimate friend of an Vilius,
and *him* being the cousin of Quintus, was enough, at a time when the
expectation of one wedding made everybody eager for another, to supply
the idea. He had not himself forgotten to feel that the marriage of his
cousin must bring them more frequently together. And his neighbours at
the Dirondelle house, therefore, (for through their communication with
Lord Rufus, the report, he concluded, had reached Princess Anastasiya,)
had only set *that* down as almost certain and immediate which *he* had
looked forward to as possible at some future time.

In revolving Princess Anastasiya's expressions, however, he could not
help feeling some uneasiness as to the possible consequence of her
persisting in this interference. From what he had said of her resolution
to prevent the marriage, it occurred to Cliopher that she must meditate
an application to her grandnephew; and how he might take a similar
representation of the evils attached to a connection with himself he
dared not pronounce. Cliopher knew not the exact degree of his affection
for his great-aunt, or his dependence on her judgment, but it was
natural to suppose that he thought much higher of her Highness than
*Kip* could do; and it was certain, that in enumerating the miseries of
a marriage with *one* whose immediate connections were so unequal to his
own, his aunt would address him on his weakest side. With his notions of
dignity, he would probably feel that the arguments, which to Cliopher
had appeared weak and ridiculous, contained much good sense and solid
reasoning.

If he had been wavering before, as to what he should do, which had often
seemed likely, the advice and entreaty of so near a relation might
settle every doubt, and determine him at once to be as happy as dignity
unblemished could make him. In that case he would return no more.
Princess Anastasiya might see him in her way through town; and his
engagement to an Vilius of coming again to Navikiani must give way.

"If, therefore, an excuse for not keeping his promise should come to his
friend within a few days," he added, "I shall know how to understand it.
I shall then give over every expectation, every wish of his constancy.
If he is satisfied with only regretting me, when he might have obtained
my affections and hand, I shall soon cease to regret him at all."

The surprise of the rest of the family, on hearing who their visitor had
been, was very great: but they obligingly satisfied it with the same
kind of supposition which had appeased Eidora Mdang's curiosity; and
Cliopher was spared from much teasing on the subject.

The next morning, as he was going down stairs, he was met by his
great-uncle, who came out of his library with a letter in his hand.

"Kip," said he, "I was going to look for you: come into my room."

He followed him thither; and his curiosity to know what he had to tell
him was heightened by the supposition of its being in some manner
connected with the letter he held. It suddenly struck Cliopher that it
might be from Princess Anastasiya, and he anticipated with dismay all
the consequent explanations.

He followed his great-uncle to the fireplace, and they both sat down. He
then said,---

"I have received a letter this morning that has astonished me
exceedingly. As it principally concerns yourself, you ought to know its
contents. I did not know before that I had *two* grandnephews on the
brink of matrimony. Let me congratulate you on a very important
conquest."

The colour now rushed into Cliopher's cheeks in the instantaneous
conviction of its being a letter from the nephew, instead of the aunt;
and he was undetermined whether most to be pleased that he explained
himself at all, or offended that his letter was not rather addressed to
Cliopher, when his great-uncle continued,---

"You look conscious. Young people have great penetration in such matters
as these; but I think I may defy even *your* sagacity to discover the
name of your admirer. This letter is from Lord Rufus."

"From Lord Rufus! and what can *he* have to say?"

"Something very much to the purpose, of course. He begins with
congratulations on the approaching nuptials of my eldest grandnephew, of
which, it seems, he has been told by some of the good-natured, gossiping
an Dirondelles. I shall not sport with your impatience by reading what
he says on that point. What relates to yourself is as follows:---'Having
thus offered you the sincere congratulations of Felicia and myself on
this happy event, let me now add a short hint on the subject of another,
of which we have been advertised by the same authority. Your nephew
Cliopher, it is presumed, will not long bear the name of Mdang, after
his eldest cousin has resigned it; and the chosen partner of his fate
may be reasonably looked up to as one of the most illustrious personages
in this land.' Can you possibly guess, Kip, who is meant by this? 'This
young gentleman is blessed, in a peculiar way, with everything the heart
of mortal can most desire,---splendid property, noble kindred, and
extensive patronage. Yet, in spite of all these temptations, let me warn
my cousin Cliopher, and yourself, of what evils you may incur by a
precipitate closure with this gentleman's proposals, which, of course,
you will be inclined to take immediate advantage of.' Have you any idea,
Kip, who this gentleman is? But now it comes out. 'My motive for
cautioning you is as follows:---We have reason to imagine that his aunt,
Princess Anastasiya Yra, does not look on the match with a friendly
eye.' *Lord Damara*, you see, is the man! Now, Kip, I think I *have*
surprised you. Could he, or the an Dirondelles, have pitched on any man,
within the circle of our acquaintance, whose name would have given the
lie more effectually to what they related? Lord Damara, who never looks
at anyone but to see a blemish, and who probably never looked at *you*
in his life! It is admirable!"

Cliopher tried to join in his great-uncle's pleasantry, but could only
force one most reluctant smile. Never had his wit been directed in a
manner so little agreeable.

"Are you not diverted?"

"Oh, yes. Pray read on."

"'After mentioning the likelihood of this marriage to her Highness last
night, she immediately, with her usual condescension, expressed what she
felt on the occasion; when it became apparent, that, on the score of
some family objections on the part of my cousin, she would never give
her consent to what she termed so disgraceful a match. I thought it my
duty to give the speediest intelligence of this to my cousin, that he
and his noble admirer may be aware of what they are about, and not run
hastily into a marriage which has not been properly sanctioned.' Lord
Rufus, moreover, adds, 'I am truly rejoiced that my cousin Hillen's sad
business has been so well hushed up, and am only concerned that their
living together before the marriage took place should be so generally
known. I must not, however, neglect the duties of my station, or refrain
from declaring my amazement, at hearing that you received the young
couple into your house as soon as they were married. It was an
encouragement of vice; and had I been there, I should very strenuously
have opposed it. You ought certainly to forgive them, but never to admit
them in your sight, or allow their names to be mentioned in your
hearing.' *That* is his notion of forgiveness! The rest of his letter is
only about his dear Felicia's situation, and his expectation of a young
olive-branch. But, Kip, you look as if you did not enjoy it. You are not
going to be *missish*, I hope, and pretend to be affronted at an idle
report. For what do we live, but to make sport for our neighbours, and
laugh at them in our turn?"

"Oh," cried Cliopher, "I am exceedingly diverted. But it is so strange!"

"Yes, *that* is what makes it amusing. Had they fixed on any other man
it would have been nothing; but *his* perfect indifference and *your*
pointed dislike make it so delightfully absurd! Much as I abominate
writing, I would not give up Lord Rufus's correspondence for any
consideration. Nay, when I read a letter of his, I cannot help giving
him the preference even over Ealoapeha, much as I value the impudence
and hypocrisy of my niece-in-law. And pray, Kip, what said Princess
Anastasiya about this report? Did she call to refuse her consent?"

To this question his nephew replied only with a laugh; and as it had
been asked without the least suspicion, he was not distressed by his
repeating it. Cliopher had never been more at a loss to make his
feelings appear what they were not. It was necessary to laugh when he
would rather have cried. His great-uncle had most cruelly mortified him
by what he said of Lord Damara's indifference; and he could do nothing
but wonder at such a want of penetration, or fear that, perhaps, instead
of his seeing too *little*, he might have fancied too *much*.
