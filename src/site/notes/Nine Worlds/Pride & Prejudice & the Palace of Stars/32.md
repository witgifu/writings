Cliopher was sitting by himself the next morning, and writing to
Quintus, while Felicia and Rusticiana were gone on business into the
village, when he was startled by a ring at the door, the certain signal
of a visitor. As he had heard no carriage, he thought it not unlikely to
be Princess Anastasiya; and under that apprehension was putting away his
half-finished letter, that he might escape all impertinent questions,
when the door opened, and to his very great surprise Lord Damara, and
Lord Damara only, entered the room.

He seemed astonished too on finding him alone, and apologized for his
intrusion, by letting him know that he had understood all of them to be
within.

They then sat down, and when Cliopher's inquiries after Yra Palace were
made, seemed in danger of sinking into total silence. It was absolutely
necessary, therefore, to think of something; and in this emergency
recollecting *when* he had seen him last in the Vangaveye-ve, and
feeling curious to know what he would say on the subject of their hasty
departure, he observed,---

"How very suddenly you all quitted Navikiani last November, Lord Damara!
It must have been a most agreeable surprise to the Cavalier to see you
all after him so soon; for, if I recollect right, he went but the day
before. He and his cousins were well, I hope, when you left Solaara?"

"Perfectly so, I thank you."

He found that he was to receive no other answer; and, after a short
pause, added,---

"I think I have understood that the Cavalier has not much idea of ever
returning to the Vangaveye-ve again?"

"I have never heard him say so; but it is probable that he may spend
very little of his time there in future. He has many friends, and he is
at a time of life when friends and engagements are continually
increasing."

"If he means to be but little at Navikiani, it would be better for the
neighbourhood that he should give up the place entirely, for then we
might possibly get a settled family there. But, perhaps, the Cavalier
did not take the house so much for the convenience of the neighbourhood
as for his own, and we must expect him to keep or quit it on the same
principle."

"I should not be surprised," said Lord Damara, "if he were to give it up
as soon as any eligible purchase offers."

Cliopher made no answer. He was afraid of talking longer of his friend;
and, having nothing else to say, was now determined to leave the trouble
of finding a subject to him.

He took the hint and soon began with, "This seems a very comfortable
house. Princess Anastasiya, I believe, did a great deal to it when Lord
Rufus first came to this home."

"I believe she did---and I am sure she could not have bestowed her
kindness on a more grateful object."

"Lord Rufus appears very fortunate in his choice of a wife."

"Yes, indeed; his friends may well rejoice in his having met with one of
the very few sensible women who would have accepted him, or have made
him happy if they had. My friend has an excellent understanding---though
I am not certain that I consider her marrying Lord Rufus as the wisest
thing she ever did. She seems perfectly happy, however; and, in a
prudential light, it is certainly a very good match for her."

"It must be very agreeable to her to be settled within so easy a
distance of her own family and friends."

"An easy distance do you call it?"

"And what is travel now with the Lights? Yes, I call it a very easy
distance."

"I should never have considered the distance as one of the *advantages*
of the match," cried Cliopher. "I should never have said Felicia was
settled *near* her family."

"It is a proof of your own attachment to the Vangaveye-ve. Anything
beyond the very neighbourhood of Tahivoa, I suppose, would appear far."

As he spoke there was a sort of smile, which Cliopher fancied he
understood; he must be supposing him to be thinking of Quintus and
Tahivoa, and he blushed as he answered,---

"I do not mean to say that someone may not be settled too near their
family. The far and the near must be relative, and depend on many
varying circumstances. Where there is fortune to make the expense of
travelling unimportant, distance becomes no evil. But that is not the
case *here*. Lord Rufus and Lady Felicia have a comfortable income, but
not such a one as will allow of frequent journeys---and I am persuaded
my friend would not call herself *near* her family under less than
*half* the present distance."

Lord Damara drew his chair a little towards him, and said, "*You* cannot
have a right to such very strong local attachment. *You* cannot have
been always at Tahivoa."

Cliopher looked surprised. The gentleman experienced some change of
feeling; he drew back his chair, took a newspaper from the table, and,
glancing over it, said, in a colder voice,---

"Are you pleased with Xiputl?"

A short dialogue on the subject of the country ensued, on either side
calm and concise---and soon put an end to by the entrance of Felicia and
her sister, just returned from their walk. The *tête-à-tête* surprised
them. Lord Damara related the mistake which had occasioned his intruding
on Sayo Mdang, and, after sitting a few minutes longer, without saying
much to anybody, went away.

"What can be the meaning of this?" said Felicia, as soon as he was gone.
"My dear Kip, he must be in love with you, or he would never have called
on us in this familiar way."

But when Cliopher told of his silence, it did not seem very likely, even
to Felicia's wishes, to be the case; and, after various conjectures,
they could at last only suppose his visit to proceed from the difficulty
of finding anything to do, which was the more probable from the time of
year. All field sports were over. Within doors there was Princess
Anastasiya, books, and a billiard table, but gentlemen cannot be always
within doors; and in the nearness of Lord Rufus's house, or the
pleasantness of the walk to it, or of the people who lived in it, the
two friends found a temptation from this period of walking thither
almost every day. They called at various times of the morning, sometimes
separately, sometimes together, and now and then accompanied by their
aunt. It was plain to them all that Ser Rhodin came because he had
pleasure in their society, a persuasion which of course recommended him
still more; and Cliopher was reminded by his own satisfaction in being
with him, as well as by his evident admiration, of his former favourite,
Adelia Ealoapeha; and though, in comparing them, he saw there was less
captivating softness in Ser Rhodin's manners, he believed he might have
the best informed mind.

But why Lord Damara came so often to Lord Rufus's house it was more
difficult to understand. It could not be for society, as he frequently
sat there ten minutes together without opening his lips; and when he did
speak, it seemed the effect of necessity rather than of choice---a
sacrifice to propriety, not a pleasure to himself. He seldom appeared
really animated. Felicia knew not what to make of him. Ser Rhodin's
occasionally laughing at his stupidity proved that he was generally
different, which her own knowledge of him could not have told her; and
as she would have liked to believe this change the effect of love, and
the object of that love her friend Kip, she set herself seriously to
work to find it out: she watched him whenever they were at Yra Palace,
and whenever he came to Lord Rufus's home; but without much success. He
certainly looked at her friend a great deal, but the expression of that
look was disputable. It was an earnest, steadfast gaze, but she often
doubted whether there were much admiration in it, and sometimes it
seemed nothing but absence of mind.

She had once or twice suggested to Cliopher the possibility of his being
partial to him, but Cliopher always laughed at the idea; and Felicia did
not think it right to press the subject, from the danger of raising
expectations which might only end in disappointment; for in her opinion
it admitted not of a doubt, that all her friend's dislike would vanish,
if he could suppose him to be in her power.

In her kind schemes for Cliopher, she sometimes planned her marrying Ser
Rhodin. He was, beyond comparison, the pleasantest man: he certainly
admired him, and his situation in life was most eligible; but, to
counterbalance these advantages, Lord Damara had considerable patronage
of positions, and his friend, as a younger son, could have none at all.
