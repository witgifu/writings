Their cousin's wedding-day arrived; and Quintus and Cliopher felt for
him probably more than he felt for himself. The yacht was sent to meet
them, and they were to return in it by dinnertime. Their arrival was
dreaded by the elder Sayo Mdangs---and Quintus more especially, who gave
Hillen the feelings which would have attended himself, had *he* been the
culprit, and was wretched in the thought of what his cousin must endure.

They came. The family were assembled in the breakfast-room to receive
them. Smiles decked the face of Eidora Mdang, as the yacht sailed up to
the lagoon; her uncle looked impenetrably grave; her nephews, son, and
niece, alarmed, anxious, uneasy.

Hillen's voice was heard in the vestibule; the door was thrown open, and
he ran into the room. His aunt stepped forwards, embraced him, and
welcomed him with rapture; gave his hand with an affectionate smile to
Ealoapeha, who followed her man; and wished them both joy, with an
alacrity which showed no doubt of their happiness.

Their reception from Buru Tovo, to whom they then turned, was not quite
so cordial. His countenance rather gained in austerity; and he scarcely
opened his lips. The easy assurance of the young couple, indeed, was
enough to provoke him.

Cliopher was disgusted, and even Sayo Mdang was shocked. Hillen was
Hillen still; untamed, unabashed, wild, noisy, and fearless. He turned
from cousin to cousin, demanding their congratulations; and when at
length they all sat down, looked eagerly round the room, took notice of
some little alteration in it, and observed, with a laugh, that it was a
great while since he had been there.

Ealoapeha was not at all more distressed than himself; but her manners
were always so pleasing, that, had her character and her marriage been
exactly what they ought, her smiles and her easy address, while she
claimed their relationship, would have delighted them all. Cliopher had
not before believed her quite equal to such assurance; but he sat down,
resolving within himself to draw no limits in future to the impudence of
an impudent woman. *He* blushed, and Quintus blushed; but the cheeks of
the two who caused their confusion suffered no variation of colour.

There was no want of discourse. The groom and his aunt could neither of
them talk fast enough; and Ealoapeha, who happened to sit near Cliopher,
began inquiring after her acquaintance in that neighbourhood, with a
good-humoured ease, which he felt very unable to equal in his replies.
They seemed each of them to have the happiest memories in the world.
Nothing of the past was recollected with pain; and Hillen led
voluntarily to subjects which his cousins would not have alluded to for
the world.

"Only think of its being three months," he cried, "since I went away: it
seems but a fortnight, I declare; and yet there have been things enough
happened in the time. Good gracious! when I went away, I am sure I had
no more idea of being married till I came back again! though I thought
it would be very good fun if I was."

His great-uncle lifted up his eyes, Quintus was distressed, Cliopher
looked expressively at Hillen; but he, who never heard nor saw anything
of which he chose to be insensible, gaily continued,---

"Oh, aunty, do the people hereabouts know I am married? I was afraid
they might not; and we overtook William Goulding in his boat, so I was
determined he should know it, and so I leaned out next to him, and took
off my glove and let my hand just rest upon the edge, so that he might
see the ring, and then I bowed and smiled like anything."

Cliopher could bear it no longer. He got up and ran out of the room; and
returned no more, till he heard them passing through the hall to the
dining-parlour. He then joined them soon enough to see Hillen, with
anxious parade, walk up to his aunt's right hand, and hear him say to
his eldest cousin,---

"Ah, Quintus, I take your place now, and you must go lower, because I am
married."

It was not to be supposed that time would give Hillen that embarrassment
from which he had been so wholly free at first. His ease and good
spirits increased. He longed to see Aunt Onaya, the an Dirondelles, and
all their other neighbours, and to hear himself called "Sayo Ealoapeha"
by each of them; and in the meantime he went after dinner to show his
ring and boast of being married to Saya Hill and the two housemaids.

"Well, aunty," said he, when they were all returned to the
breakfast-room, "and what do you think of my wife? Is not she a charming
woman? I am sure my cousins must all envy me. I only hope they may have
half my good luck. They must all go to Nijan. That is the place to get
spouses. What a pity it is, aunty, we did not all go!"

"Very true; and if I had my will we should. But, my dear Hillen, I don't
at all like your going such a way off. Must it be so?"

"Oh, Lord! yes; there is nothing in that. I shall like it of all things.
You and great-uncle Tovo, and my cousins, must come and see us. We shall
be at Boloyo City all the winter, and I dare say there will be some
balls, and I will take care to get good partners for them all."

"I should like it beyond anything!" said his aunt.

"And then when you go away, you may leave one or two of my cousins or my
brother behind you; and I dare say I shall get spouses for them before
the winter is over."

"I thank you for my share of the favour," said Cliopher; "but I do not
particularly like your way of getting a spouse."

Their visitors were not to remain above ten days with them. Saya
Ealoapeha had received her commission before she left Solaara, and she
was to join her regiment at the end of a fortnight.

No one but Eidora Mdang regretted that their stay would be so short; and
she made the most of the time by visiting about with her nephew, and
having very frequent parties at home. These parties were acceptable to
all; to avoid a family circle was even more desirable to such as did
think than such as did not.

Ealoapeha's affection for Hillen was just what Cliopher had expected to
find it; not equal to Hillen's for her. He had scarcely needed his
present observation to be satisfied, from the reason of things, that
their elopement had been brought on by the strength of his love rather
than by hers; and he would have wondered why, without violently caring
for him, she chose to elope with him at all, had he not felt certain
that her flight was rendered necessary by distress of circumstances; and
if that were the case, she was not the young woman to resist an
opportunity of having a companion.

Hillen was exceedingly fond of her. She was his dear Ealoapeha on every
occasion; no one was to be put in competition with her. She did
everything best in the world; and he was sure she would catch more fish
on the first of September than anybody else in the province.

One morning, soon after their arrival, as he was sitting with her two
elder cousins, he said to Cliopher,---

"Kip, I never gave *you* an account of my wedding, I believe. You were
not by, when I told aunty, and the others, all about it. Are not you
curious to hear how it was managed?"

"No, really," replied Cliopher; "I think there cannot be too little said
on the subject."

"La! You are so strange! But I must tell you how it went off. We were
married, you know, at St. Clement's, because Ealoapeha's lodgings were
in that parish. And it was settled that we should all be there by eleven
o'clock. My aunt and I were to go together; and the others were to meet
us at the church.

"Well, Monday morning came, and I was in such a fuss! I was so afraid,
you know, that something would happen to put it off, and then I should
have gone quite distracted. And there was my aunt, all the time I was
dressing, preaching and talking away just as if she was reading a
sermon. However, I did not hear above one word in ten, for I was
thinking, you may suppose, of my dear Ealoapeha. I longed to know
whether she would be married in her blue coat.

"Well, and so we breakfasted at ten as usual: I thought it would never
be over; for, by the bye, you are to understand that my aunt was horrid
unpleasant all the time I was with them. If you'll believe me, I did not
once put my foot out of doors, though I was there a fortnight. Not one
party, or scheme, or anything! To be sure, Solaara was rather thin, but,
however, the Little Theatre was open.

"Well, and so, just as the carriage came to the door, my aunt was called
away upon business to that horrid man Sayo Stone. And then, you know,
when once they get together, there is no end of it. Well, I was so
frightened I did not know what to do, for my aunt was to give me away;
and if we were beyond the hour we could not be married all day. But,
luckily, she came back again in ten minutes' time, and then we all set
out. However, I recollected afterwards, that if she *had* been prevented
going, the wedding need not be put off, for Lord Damara might have done
as well."

"Lord Damara!" repeated Cliopher, in utter amazement.

"Oh, yes! he was to come there with Ealoapeha, you know. But, gracious
me! I quite forgot! I ought not to have said a word about it. I promised
them so faithfully! What will Ealoapeha say? It was to be such a
secret!"

"If it was to be a secret," said Quintus, "say not another word on the
subject. You may depend upon my seeking no further."

"Oh, certainly," said Cliopher, though burning with curiosity; "we will
ask you no questions."

"Thank you," said Hillen; "for if you did, I should certainly tell you
all, and then Ealoapeha would be so angry."

On such encouragement to ask, Cliopher was forced to put it out of his
power, by running away.

But to live in ignorance on such a point was impossible; or at least it
was impossible not to try for information. Lord Damara had been at his
cousin's wedding. It was exactly a scene, and exactly among people,
where he had apparently least to do, and least temptation to go.
Conjectures as to the meaning of it, rapid and wild, hurried into his
brain; but he was satisfied with none. Those that best pleased him, as
placing his conduct in the noblest light, seemed most improbable. He
could not bear such suspense; and hastily seizing a sheet of paper,
wrote a short letter to his aunt, to request an explanation of what
Hillen had dropped, if it were compatible with the secrecy which had
been intended.

"You may readily comprehend," he added, "what my curiosity must be to
know how a person unconnected with any of us, and, comparatively
speaking, a stranger to our family, should have been amongst you at such
a time. Pray write instantly, and let me understand it---unless it is,
for very cogent reasons, to remain in the secrecy which Hillen seems to
think necessary; and then I must endeavour to be satisfied with
ignorance."

"Not that I *shall*, though," he added to himself, and he finished the
letter; "and, my dear aunt, if you do not tell me in an honourable
manner, I shall certainly be reduced to tricks and stratagems to find it
out."

Quintus's delicate sense of honour would not allow him to speak to
Cliopher privately of what Hillen had let fall; Cliopher was glad of
it:---till it appeared whether his inquiries would receive any
satisfaction, he had rather be without a confidante.
