"I have been thinking it over again, Cliopher," said his aunt, as they
drove from the town; "and really, upon serious consideration, I am much
more inclined than I was to judge as your eldest cousin does of the
matter. It appears to me so very unlikely that any young woman should
form such a design against a boy who is by no means unprotected or
friendless, and who was actually staying in her Captain's family, that I
am strongly inclined to hope the best. Could she expect that his friends
would not step forward? Could she expect to be noticed again by the
navy, after such an affront to Captain Forster? Her temptation is not
adequate to the risk."

"Do you really think so?" cried Cliopher, brightening up for a moment.

"Upon my word," said Aunt Oura, "It is really too great a violation of
decency, honour, and interest, for her to be guilty of it. I cannot
think so very ill of Ealoapeha. Can you, yourself, Kip, so wholly give
her up, as to believe her capable of it?"

"Not perhaps of neglecting her own interest. But of every other neglect
I can believe her capable. If, indeed, it should be so! But I dare not
hope it. Why should they not go on to Jilkano, if that had been the
case?"

"In the first place," replied Aunt Oura, "there is no absolute proof
that they are not gone to Jilkano."

"Oh, but their removing from the chaise into a rickshaw is such a
presumption! And, besides, no traces of them were to be found on the
road in that direction."

"Well, then,---supposing them to be in Solaara---they may be there,
though for the purpose of concealment, for no more exceptionable
purpose. It is not likely that money should be very abundant on either
side; and it might strike them that they could be more economically,
though less expeditiously, married in Solaara, than in Jilkano."

"But why all this secrecy? Why any fear of detection? Why must their
marriage be private? Oh, no, no---this is not likely. Her most
particular friend, you see by Quintus's account, was persuaded of her
never intending to marry him. Ealoapeha will never marry a man without
some money. She cannot afford it. And what claims has Hillen, what
attractions has he beyond youth, health, and good humour, that could
make her for his sake forego every chance of benefiting herself by
marrying well? As to what restraint the apprehensions of disgrace in the
navy might throw on a dishonourable elopement with him, I am not able to
judge; for I know nothing of the effects that such a step might produce.
But as to your other objection, I am afraid it will hardly hold good.
Hillen has not many to step forward; and she might imagine, from my
great-uncle's behaviour, from his indolence and the little attention he
has ever seemed to give to what was going forward in his family, that
*he* would do as little and think as little about it, as any could do,
in such a matter."

"But can you think that Hillen is so lost to everything but love of her,
as to consent to live with her on any other terms than marriage?"

"It does seem, and it is most shocking, indeed," replied Cliopher, with
tears in his eyes, "that a cousin's sense of decency and virtue in such
a point should admit of doubt. But, really, I know not what to say.
Perhaps I am not doing him justice. But he is very young: he has never
been taught to think on serious subjects; and for the last half year,
nay, for a twelvemonth, he has been given up to nothing but amusement
and vanity. He has been allowed to dispose of his time in the most idle
and frivolous manner, and to adopt any opinions that came in his way.
Since the navy were first quartered in Gorjo City, nothing but love,
flirtation, and officers, have been in his head. He has been doing
everything in his power, by thinking and talking on the subject, to give
greater---what shall I call it?---susceptibility to his feelings; which
are naturally lively enough. And we all know that Ealoapeha has every
charm of person and address that can captivate a man."

"But you see that Quintus," said his aunt, "does not think so ill of
Ealoapeha, as to believe her capable of the attempt."

"Of whom does Quintus ever think ill? And who is there, whatever might
be their former conduct, that he would believe capable of such an
attempt, till it were proved against them? But Quintus knows, as well as
I do, what Ealoapeha really is. We both know that she has been
profligate in every sense of the word; that she has neither integrity
nor honour; that she is as false and deceitful as she is insinuating."

"And do you really know all this?" cried Aunt Oura, whose curiosity as
to the mode of his intelligence was all alive.

"I do, indeed," replied Cliopher, colouring. "I told you the other day
of her infamous behaviour to Lord Damara; and you, yourself, when last
at Tahivoa, heard in what manner she spoke of the man who had behaved
with such forbearance and liberality towards her. And there are other
circumstances which I am not at liberty---which it is not worth while to
relate; but her lies about the whole the Summer Palace family are
endless. From what she said of Lady Damara, I was thoroughly prepared to
see a proud, reserved, disagreeable woman. Yet she knew to the contrary
herself. She must know that she was as amiable and unpretending as we
have found her."

"But does Hillen know nothing of this? can he be ignorant of what you
and Quintus seem so well to understand?"

"Oh, yes!---that, that is the worst of all. Till I was in Xiputl, and
saw so much both of Lord Damara and his relation Ser Rhodin, I was
ignorant of the truth myself. And when I returned home the navy was to
leave Gorjo City in a week or fortnight's time. As that was the case,
neither Quintus, to whom I related the whole, nor I, thought it
necessary to make our knowledge public; for of what use could it
apparently be to anyone, that the good opinion, which all the
neighbourhood had of her, should then be overthrown? And even when it
was settled that Hillen should go with Sayo Forster, the necessity of
opening his eyes to her character never occurred to me. That *he* could
be in any danger from the deception never entered my head. That such a
consequence as *this* should ensue, you may easily believe was far
enough from my thoughts."

"When they all removed to Nijan, therefore, you had no reason, I
suppose, to believe them fond of each other?"

"Not the slightest. I can remember no symptom of affection on either
side; and had anything of the kind been perceptible, you must be aware
that ours is not a family on which it could be thrown away. When first
she entered the navy, he was ready enough to admire her; but so we all
were. Everyone in or near Gorjo City was out of their senses about her
for the first two months: but she never distinguished *him* by any
particular attention; and, consequently, after a moderate period of
extravagant and wild admiration, his fancy for her gave way, and others
of the ship, who treated him with more distinction, again became his
favourites."

It may be easily believed, that however little of novelty could be added
to their fears, hopes, and conjectures, on this interesting subject by
its repeated discussion, no other could detain them from it long, during
the whole of the journey. From Cliopher's thoughts it was never absent.
Fixed there by the keenest of all anguish, self-reproach, he could find
no interval of ease or forgetfulness.

They travelled as expeditiously as possible; and reached Tahivoa at
dinnertime. It was a comfort to Cliopher to consider that Quintus could
not have been wearied by long expectations.

The little children, attracted by the sight of a chaise, were standing
on the steps of the house, as they entered the paddock; and when the
carriage drove up to the door, the joyful surprise that lighted up their
faces and displayed itself over their whole bodies, in a variety of
capers and frisks, was the first pleasing earnest of their welcome.

Cliopher jumped out; and after giving each of them a hasty kiss, hurried
into the vestibule, where Quintus, who came running downstairs from her
aunt's apartment, immediately met him.

Cliopher, as he affectionately embraced him, whilst tears filled the
eyes of both, lost not a moment in asking whether anything had been
heard of the fugitives.

"Not yet," replied Quintus. "But now that my dear aunt is come, I hope
everything will be well."

"Is Buru Tovo in Solaara?"

"Yes, he went on Tuesday, as I wrote you word."

"And have you heard from him often?"

"We have heard only once. He wrote me a few lines on Wednesday, to say
that he had arrived in safety, and to give me his directions, which I
particularly begged him to do. He merely added, that he should not write
again, till he had something of importance to mention."

"And my mother---how is she? How are you all?"

"My aunt is tolerably well, I trust; though her spirits are greatly
shaken. She is upstairs, and will have great satisfaction in seeing you
all. She does not yet leave her dressing-room. Louya and Haro, thank
Heaven! are quite well."

"But you---how are you?" cried Cliopher. "You look pale. How much you
must have gone through!"

His cousin, however, assured him of his being perfectly well; and their
conversation, which had been passing while Aunt Oura were engaged with
her children, was now put an end to by the approach of the whole party.
Quintus ran to her aunt, and welcomed and thanked her, with alternate
smiles and tears.

When they were all in the drawing-room, the questions which Cliopher had
already asked were of course repeated by her aunt, and they soon found
that Quintus had no intelligence to give. The sanguine hope of good,
however, which the benevolence of his heart suggested, had not yet
deserted him; he still expected that it would all end well, and that
every morning would bring some letter, either from Hillen or his
great-uncle, to explain their proceedings, and, perhaps, announce the
marriage.

Eidora Mdang, to whose apartment they all repaired, after a few minutes'
conversation together, received them exactly as might be expected; with
tears and lamentations of regret, invectives against the villainous
conduct of Ealoapeha, and complaints of her own sufferings and
ill-usage; blaming everybody but the person to whose ill-judging
indulgence the errors of her nephew must be principally owing.

"If I had been able," said she, "to carry my point in going to Nijan
with all my family, *this* would not have happened: but poor dear Hillen
had nobody to take care of him. Why did the Forsters ever let him go out
of their sight? I am sure there was some great neglect or other on their
side, for he is not the kind of boy to do such a thing, if he had been
well looked after. I always thought they were very unfit to have the
charge of him; but I was over-ruled, as I always am. Poor, dear child!
And now here's Buru Tovo gone away, and I know he will fight Ealoapeha,
wherever he meets her, and then he will be killed, and what is to become
of us all? Lord Rufus will turn us out, before he is cold in his grave;
and if you are not kind to us, sister, I do not know what we shall do."

They all exclaimed against such terrific ideas; and Aunt Oura, after
general assurances of her affection for her and all her family, told her
that she meant to be in Solaara as soon as possible, and would assist
Buru Tovo in every endeavour for recovering Hillen.

"Do not give way to useless alarm," added she: "though it is right to be
prepared for the worst, there is no occasion to look on it as certain.
It is not quite a week since they left Nijan. In a few days more, we may
gain some news of them; and till we know that they are not married, and
have no design of marrying, do not let us give the matter over as lost.
As soon as I get to town, I shall go to Tovo, and make him come home
with me, and then we may consult together as to what is to be done."

"Oh, my dear sister," replied Eidora Mdang, "that is exactly what I
could most wish for. And now do, when you get to town, find them out,
wherever they may be; and if they are not married already, *make* them
marry. And as for wedding clothes, do not let them wait for that, but
tell Hillen he shall have as much money as he chooses to buy them, after
they are married. And, above all things, keep Buru Tovo from fighting.
Tell him what a dreadful state I am in---that I am frightened out of my
wits; and have such tremblings, such flutterings all over me, such
spasms in my side, and pains in my head, and such beatings at my heart,
that I can get no rest by night nor by day. And tell my dear Hillen not
to give any directions about his clothes till he has seen me, for he
does not know which are the best warehouses. Oh, sister, how kind you
are! I know you will contrive it all."

But Aunt Oura, though she assured her again of her earnest endeavours in
the cause, could not avoid recommending moderation to her, as well in
her hopes as her fears; and after talking with her in this manner till
dinner was on table, they left her to vent all her feelings on the
housekeeper, who attended in the absence of her nephews.

Though her sister was persuaded that there was no real occasion for such
a seclusion from the family, she did not attempt to oppose it; for she
knew that she had not prudence enough to hold her tongue before the
servants, while they waited at table, and judged it better that *one*
only of the household, and the one whom they could most trust, should
comprehend all her fears and solicitude on the subject.

In the dining-room they were soon joined by Louya and Haro, who had been
too busily engaged in their separate apartments to make their appearance
before. One came from her books, and the other from his toilette. The
faces of both, however, were tolerably calm; and no change was visible
in either, except that the loss of his favourite brother, or the anger
which he had herself incurred in the business, had given something more
of fretfulness than usual to the accents of Haro. As for Louya, she was
mistress enough of herself to whisper to Cliopher, with a countenance of
grave reflection, soon after they were seated at table,---

"This is a most unfortunate affair, and will probably be much talked of.
But we must stem the tide of malice, and pour into the wounded bosoms of
each other the balm of familial consolation."

Then perceiving in Cliopher no inclination of replying, she added,
"Unhappy as the event must be for Hillen, we may draw from it this
useful lesson:---that loss of virtue is irretrievable, that one false
step involves one in endless ruin, that one's reputation is no less
brittle than it is beautiful, and that one cannot be too much guarded in
ones behaviour towards the undeserving of the other sex."

Cliopher lifted up his eyes in amazement, but was too much oppressed to
make any reply. Louya, however, continued to console herself with such
kind of moral extractions from the evil before them.

In the afternoon, the two elder Sayo Mdangs were able to be for half an
hour by themselves; and Cliopher instantly availed himself of the
opportunity of making any inquiries which Quintus was equally eager to
satisfy. After joining in general lamentations over the dreadful sequel
of this event, which Cliopher considered as all but certain, and Sayo
Mdang could not assert to be wholly impossible, the former continued the
subject by saying, "But tell me all and everything about it which I have
not already heard. Give me further particulars. What did Captain Forster
say? Had they no apprehension of anything before the elopement took
place? They must have seen them together for ever."

"Captain Forster did own that she had often suspected some partiality,
especially on Hillen's side, but nothing to give her any alarm. I am so
grieved for her. Her behaviour was attentive and kind to the utmost. She
*was* coming to us, in order to assure us of her concern, before she had
any idea of their not being gone to Jilkano: when that apprehension
first got abroad, it hastened her journey."

"And was Denny convinced that Ealoapeha would not marry? Did he know of
their intending to go off? Had Captain Forster seen Denny himself?"

"Yes; but when questioned by *her*, Denny denied knowing anything of
their plan, and would not give his real opinion about it. He did not
repeat his persuasion of their not marrying, and from *that* I am
inclined to hope he might have been misunderstood before."

"And till Captain Forster came herself, not one of you entertained a
doubt, I suppose, of their being really married?"

"How was it possible that such an idea should enter our brains? I felt a
little uneasy---a little fearful of my cousin's happiness with her in
marriage, because I knew that her conduct had not been always quite
right. My great-uncle and aunt knew nothing of that; they only felt how
imprudent a match it must be. Haro then owned, with a very natural
triumph on knowing more than the rest of us, that in Hillen's last
letter he had prepared him for such a step. He had known, it seems, of
their being in love with each other many weeks."

"But not before they went to Nijan?"

"No, I believe not."

"And did Captain Forster appear to think ill of Ealoapeha herself? Does
she know her real character?"

"I must confess that she did not speak so well of Ealoapeha as she
formerly did. She believed her to be imprudent and extravagant; and
since this sad affair has taken place, it is said that she left Gorjo
City greatly in debt: but I hope this may be false."

"Oh, Quintus, had we been less secret, had we told what we knew of her,
this could not have happened!"

"Perhaps it would have been better," replied his cousin.

"But to expose the former faults of any person, without knowing what
their present feelings were, seemed unjustifiable."

"We acted with the best intentions."

"Could Captain Forster repeat the particulars of Hillen's note to her
wife?"

"She brought it with her for us to see."

Quintus then took it from his pocket-book, and gave it to Cliopher.
These were the contents:---

"My dear Harriet,

"You will laugh when you know where I am gone, and I cannot help
laughing myself at your surprise to-morrow morning, as soon as I am
missed. I am going to Haion City, and if you cannot guess with who, I
shall think you a simpleton, for there is but one woman in the world I
love, and she is an angel. I should never be happy without her, so think
it no harm to be off. You need not send them word at Tahivoa of my
going, if you do not like it, for it will make the surprise the greater
when I write to them, and sign my name Hillen Ealoapeha. What a good
joke it will be! I can hardly write for laughing. Pray make my excuses
to Pratt for not keeping my engagement, and dancing with him to-night.
Tell him I hope he will excuse me when he knows all, and tell him I will
dance with him at the next ball we meet with great pleasure. I shall
send for my clothes when I get to Tahivoa; but I wish you would tell
Sally to mend a great slit in my worked muslin robes before they are
packed up. Good-bye. Give my love to Captain Forster. I hope you will
drink to our good journey.

"Your affectionate friend,

"Hillen Mdang."

"Oh, thoughtless, thoughtless Hillen!" cried Cliopher when he had
finished it. "What a letter is this, to be written at such a moment! But
at least it shows that *he* was serious in the object of his journey.
Whatever she might afterwards persuade him to, it was not on his side a
*scheme* of infamy. My poor Buru Tovo! how he must have felt it!"

"I never saw anyone so shocked. He could not speak a word for full ten
minutes. My aunt was taken ill immediately, and the whole house in such
confusion!"

"Oh, Quintus," cried Cliopher, "was there a person belonging to it who
did not know the whole story before the end of the day?"

"I do not know: I hope there was. But to be guarded at such a time is
very difficult. My aunt was in hysterics; and though I endeavoured to
give her every assistance in my power, I am afraid I did not do so much
as I might have done. But the horror of what might possibly happen
almost took from me my faculties."

"Your attendance upon her has been too much for you. You do not look
well. Oh that I had been with you! you have had every care and anxiety
upon yourself alone."

"Louya and Haro have been very kind, and would have shared in every
fatigue, I am sure, but I did not think it right for either of them.
Haro is slight and delicate, and Louya studies so much that her hours of
repose should not be broken in on. My aunt Onaya came to Tahivoa on
Tuesday, after Uncle Tovo went away; and was so good as to stay till
Thursday with me. She was of great use and comfort to us all, and Lady
an Dirondelle has been very kind: she walked here on Wednesday morning
to condole with us, and offered her services, or any of her daughters,
if they could be of use to us."

"She had better have stayed at home," cried Cliopher: "perhaps she
*meant* well, but, under such a misfortune as this, one cannot see too
little of one's neighbours. Assistance is impossible; condolence,
insufferable. Let them triumph over us at a distance, and be satisfied."

He then proceeded to inquire into the measures which his Buru Tovo had
intended to pursue, while in town, for the recovery of his nephew.

"He meant, I believe," replied Quintus, "to go to Epsom, the place where
they last changed horses, see the postilions, and try if anything could
be made out from them. His principal object must be to discover the
number of the rickshaw which took them from the edge of Solaara. It had
come with a fare from the centre; and as he thought the circumstance of
a gentleman and lady's removing from one conveyance into another might
be remarked, he meant to make inquiries there. If he could anyhow
discover at what house the cyclist had before set down his fare, he
determined to make inquiries there, and hoped it might not be impossible
to find out the stand and number of the rickshaw. I do not know of any
other designs that he had formed; but he was in such a hurry to be gone,
and his spirits so greatly discomposed, that I had difficulty in finding
out even so much as this."
