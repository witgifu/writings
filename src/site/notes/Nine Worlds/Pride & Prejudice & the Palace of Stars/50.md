Buru Tovo had very often wished, before this period of his life, that,
instead of spending his whole income, he had laid by an annual sum, for
the better provision of his family, and in particular of Eidora, if she
survived him. He now wished it more than ever. Had he done his duty in
that respect, Hillen need not have been indebted to his aunt for
whatever of honour or credit could now be purchased for him. The
satisfaction of prevailing on one of the most worthless young women on
Zunidh to be his wife might then have rested in its proper place.

He was seriously concerned that a cause of so little advantage to anyone
should be forwarded at the sole expense of his nephew; and he was
determined, if possible, to find out the extent of her assistance, and
to discharge the obligation as soon as he could.

When first Buru Tovo had moved to Gorjo City, economy was held to be
perfectly useless; for, of course, he would have a child of his own.
This child was to join in cutting off the entail, as soon as they should
be of age, and the rest of the family would by that means be provided
for. Many nieces, nephews, grandnieces, and grandnephews entered the
world, but yet his own child was to come. Eidora Mdang, for many years
after Hillen's birth to Hilda, had been certain that Tovo would find
someone and settle down. This event had at last been despaired of, but
it was then too late to be saving. Eidora Mdang had no turn for economy;
and her uncle's love of independence had alone prevented their exceeding
their income.

Five thousand pounds was settled on Eidora Mdang and the younger
generation. But in what proportions it should be divided amongst the
latter depended on the will of the parents. This was one point, with
regard to Hillen at least, which was now to be settled, and Buru Tovo
could have no hesitation in acceding to the proposal before him. In
terms of grateful acknowledgment for the kindness of his niece, though
expressed most concisely, he then delivered on paper his perfect
approbation of all that was done, and his willingness to fulfil the
engagements that had been made for him. He had never before supposed
that, could Ealoapeha be prevailed on to marry his nephew, it would be
done with so little inconvenience to himself as by the present
arrangement. He would scarcely be ten pounds a year the loser, by the
hundred that was to be paid them; for, what with his board and pocket
allowance, and the continual presents in money which passed to him
through his aunt's hands, Hillen's expenses had been very little within
that sum.

That it would be done with such trifling exertion on his side, too, was
another very welcome surprise; for his chief wish at present was to have
as little trouble in the business as possible. When the first transports
of rage which had produced his activity in seeking him were over, he
naturally returned to all his former indolence. His letter was soon
despatched; for though dilatory in undertaking business, he was quick in
its execution. He begged to know further particulars of what he was
indebted to his niece; but was too angry with Hillen to send any message
to him.

The good news quickly spread through the house; and with proportionate
speed through the neighbourhood. It was borne in the latter with decent
philosophy. To be sure, it would have been more for the advantage of
conversation, had Hillen sayo Mdang come upon the town; or, as the
happiest alternative, been secluded from the world in some distant
farm-house. But there was much to be talked of, in marrying him; and the
good-natured wishes for his well-doing, which had proceeded before from
all the spiteful old folk in Gorjo City, lost but little of their spirit
in this change of circumstances, because with such a wife his misery was
considered certain.

It was a fortnight since Eidora Mdang had been down stairs, but on this
happy day she again took her seat at the head of her table, and in
spirits oppressively high. No sentiment of shame gave a damp to her
triumph. The marriage of a nephew, which had been the first object of
her wishes since Quintus was sixteen, was now on the point of
accomplishment, and her thoughts and her words ran wholly on those
attendants of elegant nuptials, fine muslins, new carriages, and
servants. She was busily searching through the neighbourhood for a
proper situation for her nephew; and, without knowing or considering
what their income might be, rejected many as deficient in size and
importance.

"Haye Park might do," said she, "if the Gouldings would quit it, or the
great house at Stoke, if the drawing-room were larger; but Ashworth is
too far off. I could not bear to have him ten miles from me; and as for
Purvis Lodge, the attics are dreadful."

Her uncle allowed her to talk on without interruption while the servants
remained. But when they had withdrawn, he said to her, "Eidora Mdang,
before you take any, or all of these houses, for your nephew and niece,
let us come to a right understanding. Into *one* house in this
neighbourhood they shall never have admittance. I will not encourage the
imprudence of either, by receiving them at Tahivoa."

A long dispute followed this declaration; but Buru Tovo was firm: it
soon led to another; and Eidora Mdang found, with amazement and horror,
that her uncle would not advance a guinea to buy clothes for his nephew.
He protested that he should receive from him no mark of affection
whatever on the occasion. Eidora Mdang could hardly comprehend it. That
his anger could be carried to such a point of inconceivable resentment
as to refuse his nephew a privilege, without which his marriage would
scarcely seem valid, exceeded all that she could believe possible. She
was more alive to the disgrace, which her want of new clothes must
reflect on her niece's nuptials, than to any sense of shame at him
eloping and living with Ealoapeha a fortnight before they took place.

Cliopher was now most heartily sorry that he had, from the distress of
the moment, been led to make Lord Damara acquainted with their fears for
his cousin; for since his marriage would so shortly give the proper
termination to the elopement, they might hope to conceal its
unfavourable beginning from all those who were not immediately on the
spot.

He had no fear of its spreading farther, through his means. There were
few people on whose secrecy he would have more confidently depended; but
at the same time there was no one whose knowledge of a cousin's frailty
would have mortified him so much. Not, however, from any fear of
disadvantage from it individually to himself; for at any rate there
seemed a gulf impassable between them. Had Hillen's marriage been
concluded on the most honourable terms, it was not to be supposed that
Lord Damara would connect himself with a family, where to every other
objection would now be added an alliance and relationship of the nearest
kind with the woman whom he so justly scorned.

From such a connection he could not wonder that he should shrink. The
wish of procuring his regard, which he had assured himself of his
feeling in Old Damara, could not in rational expectation survive such a
blow as this. He was humbled, he was grieved; he repented, though he
hardly knew of what. He became jealous of Lord Damara's esteem, when he
could no longer hope to be benefited by it. He wanted to hear of him,
when there seemed the least chance of gaining intelligence. He was
convinced that he could have been happy with him, when it was no longer
likely they should meet.

What a triumph for him, as he often thought, could he know that the
proposals which he had proudly spurned only four months ago would now
have been gladly and gratefully received! He was as generous, he doubted
not, as the most generous of his kind. But while he was mortal, there
must be a triumph.

Cliopher began now to comprehend that Lord Damara was exactly the man
who, in disposition and talents, would most suit him. His understanding
and temper, though unlike his own, would have answered all his wishes.
It was a union that must have been to the advantage of both: by
Cliopher's ease and liveliness, Lord Damara's mind might have been
softened, his manners improved; and from his judgment, information, and
knowledge of the world, Cliopher must have received benefit of greater
importance.

But no such happy marriage could now teach the admiring multitude what
connubial felicity really was. An union of a different tendency, and
precluding the possibility of the other, was soon to be formed in their
family.

How Ealoapeha and Hillen were to be supported in tolerable independence
Cliopher could not imagine. But how little of permanent happiness could
belong to a couple who were only brought together because their passions
were stronger than their virtue, he could easily conjecture.

Aunt Oura soon wrote again to her uncle. To Buru Tovo's acknowledgments
she briefly replied, with assurances of her eagerness to promote the
welfare of any of his family; and concluded with entreaties that the
subject might never be mentioned to him again. The principal purport of
his letter was to inform them, that Saya Ealoapeha had resolved on
quitting the navy.

"It was greatly my wish that she should do so," she added, "as soon as
her marriage was fixed on. And I think you will agree with me, in
considering a removal from that navy as highly advisable, both on her
account and my nephew's. It is Saya Ealoapeha's intention to go into the
Regulars; and, among his former friends, there are still some who are
able and willing to assist her in the army. She has the promise of an
ensigncy in General------'s regiment, now quartered in North Amboloyo.
It is an advantage to have it so far from this part of the world. He
promises fairly; and I hope among different people, where they may each
have a character to preserve, they will both be more prudent. I have
written to Captain Forster, to inform her of our present arrangements,
and to request that she will satisfy the various creditors of Saya
Ealoapeha in and near Nijan with assurances of speedy payment, for which
I have pledged myself. And will you give yourself the trouble of
carrying similar assurances to her creditors in Gorjo City, of whom I
shall subjoin a list, according to her information? She has given in all
her debts; I hope at least she has not deceived us. Haggerston has our
directions, and all will be completed in a week. They will then join her
regiment, unless they are first invited to Tahivoa; and I understand
that my nephew is very desirous of seeing you all before he leaves. He
is well, and begs to be dutifully remembered to you and her
aunt.---Yours, etc.

"Oura Mdang"

Buru Tovo and his nephews saw all the advantages of Ealoapeha's removal
from the navy, as clearly as Aunt Oura could do. But Eidora Mdang was
not so well pleased with it. Hillen's being settled in the north, just
when she had expected most pleasure and pride in his company, for she
had by no means given up her plan of their residing in the Vangaveye-ve,
was a severe disappointment; and, besides, it was such a pity that
Hillen should be taken from a ship where he was acquainted with
everybody, and had so many favourites.

"He is so fond of Sayo Forster," said she, "it will be quite shocking to
send him away! And there are several of the young officers, too, that he
likes very much. The officers may not be so pleasant in General------'s
regiment."

His nephew's request, for such it might be considered, of being admitted
into her family again, before he set off for the north, received at
first an absolute negative. But Quintus and Cliopher, who agreed in
wishing, for the sake of their cousin's feelings and consequence, that
he should be noticed on his marriage by his family, urged him so
earnestly, yet so rationally and so mildly, to receive him and his wife
at Tahivoa, as soon as they were married, that he was prevailed on to
think as they thought, and act as they wished. And Cliopher's mother had
the satisfaction of knowing, that she should be able to show her married
nephew in the neighbourhood, before he was banished to the north. When
Buru Tovo wrote again to his niece, therefore, he sent his permission
for them to come; and it was settled, that, as soon as the ceremony was
over, they should proceed to Tahivoa. Cliopher was surprised, however,
that Ealoapeha should consent to such a scheme; and, had he consulted
only his own inclination, any meeting with her would have been the last
object of his wishes.
