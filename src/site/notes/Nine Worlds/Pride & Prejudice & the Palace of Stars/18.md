Till Cliopher entered the drawing-room at Navikiani, and looked in vain
for Saya Ealoapeha among the cluster of red coats there assembled, a
doubt of her being present had never occurred to him. The certainty of
meeting her had not been checked by any of those recollections that
might not unreasonably have alarmed him. He had dressed with more than
usual care, and prepared in the highest spirits for the conquest of all
that remained unsubdued of her heart, trusting that it was not more than
might be won in the course of the evening. But in an instant arose the
dreadful suspicion of her being purposely omitted, for Lord Damara's
pleasure, in the Cavalier's invitation to the officers; and though this
was not exactly the case, the absolute fact of her absence was
pronounced by his friend Sayo Denny, to whom Hillen eagerly applied, and
who told them that Ealoapeha had been obliged to go to town on business
the day before, and was not yet returned; adding, with a significant
smile,---

"I do not imagine her business would have called her away just now, if
she had not wished to avoid a certain gentleman here."

This part of his intelligence, though unheard by Hillen, was caught by
Cliopher; and, as it assured him that Damara was not less answerable for
Ealoapeha's absence than if his first surmise had been just, every
feeling of displeasure against the former was so sharpened by immediate
disappointment, that he could hardly reply with tolerable civility to
the polite inquiries which he directly afterwards approached to make.
Attention, forbearance, patience with Damara, was injury to Ealoapeha.
He was resolved against any sort of conversation with him, and turned
away with a degree of ill-humour which he could not wholly surmount even
in speaking to the Cavalier, whose blind partiality provoked him.

But Cliopher was not formed for ill-humour; and though every prospect of
his own was destroyed for the evening, it could not dwell long on his
spirits; and, having told all his griefs to Felicia an Dirondelle, whom
he had not seen for a week, he was soon able to make a voluntary
transition to the oddities of his cousin, and to point him out to her
particular notice. The two first dances, however, brought a return of
distress: they were dances of mortification. Lord Rufus, awkward and
solemn, apologizing instead of attending, and often moving wrong without
being aware of it, gave him all the shame and misery which a
disagreeable partner for a couple of dances can give. The moment of his
release from him was ecstasy.

He danced next with an officer, and had the refreshment of talking of
Ealoapeha, and of hearing that he was universally liked. When those
dances were over, he returned to Felicia an Dirondelle, and was in
conversation with her, when he found himself suddenly addressed by Lord
Damara, who took him so much by surprise in his application for his next
dance, that, without knowing what he did, he accepted him. He walked
away again immediately, and Cliopher was left to fret over his own want
of presence of mind: Felicia tried to console her.

"I dare say you will find him very agreeable."

"Heaven forbid! *That* would be the greatest misfortune of all! To find
a man agreeable whom one is determined to hate! Do not wish me such an
evil."

When the dancing recommenced, however, and Damara approached to claim
him, Felicia could not help cautioning him, in a whisper, not to be a
simpleton, and allow his fancy for Ealoapeha to make him appear
unpleasant in the eyes of a man of ten times her consequence. Cliopher
made no answer, and took his place in the set, amazed at the dignity to
which he was arrived in being allowed to stand opposite to Lord Damara,
and reading in his neighbours' looks their equal amazement in beholding
it. They stood for some time without speaking a word; and he began to
imagine that their silence was to last through the two dances, and, at
first, was resolved not to break it; till suddenly fancying that it
would be the greater punishment to his partner to oblige him to talk, he
made some slight observation on the dance. Lord Damara replied, and was
again silent. After a pause of some minutes, he addressed him a second
time, with---

"It is *your* turn to say something now, my lord. *I* talked about the
dance, and *you* ought to make some kind of remark on the size of the
room, or the number of couples."

He smiled, and assured Cliopher that whatever he wished him to say
should be said.

"Very well; that reply will do for the present. Perhaps, by-and-by, I
may observe that private dances are much pleasanter than public ones;
but *now* we may be silent."

"Do you talk by rule, then, while you are dancing?"

"Sometimes. One must speak a little, you know. It would look odd to be
entirely silent for half an hour together; and yet, for the advantage of
*some*, conversation ought to be so arranged as that they may have the
trouble of saying as little as possible."

"Are you consulting your own feelings in the present case, or do you
imagine that you are gratifying mine?"

"Both," replied Cliopher archly; "for I have always seen a great
similarity in the turn of our minds. We are each of an unsocial,
taciturn disposition, unwilling to speak, unless we expect to say
something that will amaze the whole room, and be handed down to
posterity with all the *éclat* of a proverb."

"This is no very striking resemblance of your own character, I am sure,"
said he. "How near it may be to *mine*, I cannot pretend to say. *You*
think it a faithful portrait, undoubtedly."

"I must not decide on my own performance."

He made no answer; and they were again silent till they had gone down
the dance, when he asked Cliopher if he and his cousins did not very
often walk to Gorjo City. He answered in the affirmative; and, unable to
resist the temptation, added, "When you met us there the other day, we
had just been forming a new acquaintance."

The effect was immediate. A deeper shade of *hauteur* overspread his
features, but he said not a word; and Cliopher, though blaming himself
for his own weakness, could not go on. At length Damara spoke, and in a
constrained manner said,---

"Saya Ealoapeha is blessed with such happy manners as may insure her
*making* friends; whether she may be equally capable of *retaining*
them, is less certain."

"She has been so unlucky as to lose your friendship," replied Cliopher,
with emphasis, "and in a manner which she is likely to suffer from all
her life."

Damara made no answer, and seemed desirous of changing the subject. At
that moment Saya Dorn appeared close to them, meaning to pass through
the set to the other side of the room; but, on perceiving Lord Damara,
she stopped, with a bow of superior courtesy, to compliment him on his
dancing and his partner.

"I have been most highly gratified, indeed, my dear sir; such very
superior dancing is not often seen. It is evident that you belong to the
first circles. Allow me to say, however, that your fair partner does not
disgrace you: and that I must hope to have this pleasure often repeated,
especially when a certain desirable event, my dear Sayo Kip (glancing at
his cousin and the Cavalier), shall take place. What congratulations
will then flow in! I appeal to Lord Damara;---but let me not interrupt
you, sir. You will not thank me for detaining you from the bewitching
converse of that young man, whose bright eyes are also upbraiding me."

The latter part of this address was scarcely heard by Damara; but Saya
Dorn's allusion to his friend seemed to strike him forcibly, and his
eyes were directed, with a very serious expression, towards the Cavalier
and Quintus, who were dancing together. Recovering himself, however,
shortly, he turned to his partner, and said,---

"Saya Dorn's interruption has made me forget what we were talking of."

"I do not think we were speaking at all. Saya Dorn could not have
interrupted any two people in the room who had less to say for
themselves. We have tried two or three subjects already without success,
and what we are to talk of next I cannot imagine."

"What think you of books?" said he, smiling.

"Books---oh no!---I am sure we never read the same, or not with the same
feelings."

"I am sorry you think so; but if that be the case, there can at least be
no want of subject. We may compare our different opinions."

"No---I cannot talk of books while dancing; my head is always full of
something else."

"The *present* always occupies you in such scenes---does it?" said he,
with a look of doubt.

"Yes, always," he replied, without knowing what he said; for his
thoughts had wandered far from the subject, as soon afterwards appeared
by him suddenly exclaiming, "I remember hearing you once say, Lord
Damara, that you hardly ever forgave;---that your resentment, once
created, was unappeasable. You are very cautious, I suppose, as to its
*being created*?"

"I am," said he, with a firm voice.

"And never allow yourself to be blinded by prejudice?"

"I hope not."

"It is particularly incumbent on those who never change their opinion,
to be secure of judging properly at first."

"May I ask to what these questions tend?"

"Merely to the illustration of *your* character," said he, endeavouring
to shake off his gravity. "I am trying to make it out."

"And what is your success?"

He shook his head. "I do not get on at all. I hear such different
accounts of you as puzzle me exceedingly."

"I can readily believe," answered he, gravely, "that reports may vary
greatly with respect to me; and I could wish, Sayo Mdang, that you were
not to sketch my character at the present moment, as there is reason to
fear that the performance would reflect no credit on either."

"But if I do not take your likeness now, I may never have another
opportunity."

"I would by no means suspend any pleasure of yours," he coldly replied.
Cliopher said no more, and they went down the other dance and parted in
silence; on each side dissatisfied, though not to an equal degree; for
in Damara's breast there was a tolerably powerful feeling towards
Cliopher, which soon procured his pardon, and directed all his anger
against another.

They had not long separated when Lord Lior came towards her, and, with
an expression of civil disdain, thus accosted him,---

"So, Sayo Kip, I hear you are quite delighted with Adelia Ealoapeha?
Your cousin has been talking to me about her, and asking me a thousand
questions; and I find that the young woman forgot to tell you, among her
other communications, that she was the daughter of old Ealoapeha, the
late Grand Duke's steward. Let me recommend you, however, as a friend,
not to give implicit confidence to all her assertions; for, as to Lord
Damara's using her ill, it is perfectly false: for, on the contrary, he
has been always remarkably kind to her, though Adelia Ealoapeha has
treated Lord Damara in a most infamous manner. I do not know the
particulars, but I know very well that Lord Damara is not in the least
to blame; that he cannot bear to hear Adelia Ealoapeha mentioned; and
that though my cousin thought he could not well avoid including her in
his invitation to the officers, he was excessively glad to find that she
had taken herself out of the way. Her coming into the province at all is
a most insolent thing, indeed, and I wonder how she could presume to do
it. I pity you, Sayo Kip, for this discovery of your favourite's guilt;
but really, considering her descent, one could not expect much better."

"Her guilt and his descent appear, by your account, to be the same,"
said Cliopher, angrily; "for I have heard you accuse her of nothing
worse than of being the son of the Grand Duke's steward, and of *that*,
I can assure you, she informed me herself."

"I beg your pardon," replied Lord Lior, turning away with a sneer.
"Excuse my interference; it was kindly meant."

"Insolent man!" said Cliopher to himself. "You are much mistaken if you
expect to influence me by such a paltry attack as this. I see nothing in
it but your own wilful ignorance and the malice of Lord Damara." He then
sought his eldest cousin, who had undertaken to make inquiries on the
same subject of the Cavalier. Quintus met him with a smile of such sweet
complacency, a glow of such happy expression, as sufficiently marked how
well he was satisfied with the occurrences of the evening. Cliopher
instantly read his feelings; and, at that moment, solicitude for
Ealoapeha, resentment against her enemies, and everything else, gave way
before the hope of Quintus's being in the fairest way for happiness.

"I want to know," said he, with a countenance no less smiling than his
cousin's, "what you have learnt about Saya Ealoapeha. But perhaps you
have been too pleasantly engaged to think of any third person, in which
case you may be sure of my pardon."

"No," replied Quintus, "I have not forgotten her; but I have nothing
satisfactory to tell you. The Cavalier does not know the whole of her
history, and is quite ignorant of the circumstances which have
principally offended Lord Damara; but he will vouch for the good
conduct, the probity and honour, of his friend, and is perfectly
convinced that Saya Ealoapeha has deserved much less attention from Lord
Damara than she has received; and I am sorry to say that by his account,
as well as his cousin's, Saya Ealoapeha is by no means a respectable
young woman. I am afraid she has been very imprudent, and has deserved
to lose Lord Damara's regard."

"The Cavalier does not know Saya Ealoapeha himself."

"No; he never saw her till the other morning at Gorjo City."

"This account then is what he has received from Lord Damara. I am
perfectly satisfied. But what does he say of the position?"

"He does not exactly recollect the circumstances, though he has heard
them from Lord Damara more than once, but he believes that it was left
to her *conditionally* only."

"I have not a doubt of the Cavalier's sincerity," said Cliopher warmly,
"but you must excuse my not being convinced by assurances only. The
Cavalier's defence of his friend was a very able one, I dare say; but
since he is unacquainted with several parts of the story, and has learnt
the rest from that friend himself, I shall venture still to think of
both as I did before."

He then changed the discourse to one more gratifying to each, and on
which there could be no difference of sentiment. Cliopher listened with
delight to the happy though modest hopes which Quintus entertained of
the Cavalier's regard, and said all in his power to heighten his
confidence in it. On their being joined by the Cavalier himself,
Cliopher withdrew to Felicia; to whose inquiry after the pleasantness of
his last partner he had scarcely replied, before Lord Rufus came up to
them, and told him with great exultation, that he had just been so
fortunate as to make a most important discovery.

"I have found out," said he, "by a singular accident, that there is now
in the room a near relation to my patroness. I happened to overhear the
gentleman himself mentioning to the man who does the honours of this
house the name of Princess Anastasiya. How wonderfully these sort of
things occur! Who would have thought of my meeting with---perhaps---a
great-nephew of Princess Anastasiya in this assembly! I am most thankful
that the discovery is made in time for me to pay my respects to him,
which I am now going to do, and trust he will excuse my not having done
it before. My total ignorance of the connection must plead my apology."

"You are not going to introduce yourself to Lord Damara?"

"Indeed I am. I shall entreat his pardon for not having done it earlier.
I believe him to be Princess Anastasiya's *great-nephew*. It will be in
my power to assure him that her Highness was quite well yesterday
se'nnight."

Cliopher tried hard to dissuade him from such a scheme; assuring him
that Lord Damara would consider his addressing him without introduction
as an impertinent freedom, rather than a compliment to his great-aunt;
that it was not in the least necessary there should be any notice on
either side, and that if it were, it must belong to Lord Damara, the
superior in consequence, to begin the acquaintance. Lord Rufus listened
to him with the determined air of following his own inclination, and
when he ceased speaking, replied thus,---

"My dear Sayo Cliopher, I have the highest opinion in the world of your
excellent judgment in all matters within the scope of your
understanding, but permit me to say that there must be a wide difference
between the established forms of ceremony amongst the gentry and those
which regulate the nobility; for, give me leave to observe that I
consider my office as equal in point of dignity with the highest rank in
the kingdom---provided that a proper humility of behaviour is at the
same time maintained. You must, therefore, allow me to follow the
dictates of my conscience on this occasion, which lead me to perform
what I look on as a point of duty. Pardon me for neglecting to profit by
your advice, which on every other subject shall be my constant guide,
though in the case before us I consider myself more fitted by education
and habitual study to decide on what is right than a young man like
yourself;" and with a low bow he left him to attack Lord Damara, whose
reception of his advances he eagerly watched, and whose astonishment at
being so addressed was very evident. Cliopher's relation prefaced his
speech with a solemn bow, and though he could not hear a word of it, he
felt as if hearing it all, and saw in the motion of his lips the words
"apology," "My house," and "Princess Anastasiya." It vexed him to see
him expose himself to such a man. Lord Damara was eyeing him with
unrestrained wonder; and when at last Lord Rufus allowed him to speak,
replied with an air of distant civility. Lord Rufus, however, was not
discouraged from speaking again, and Lord Damara's contempt seemed
abundantly increasing with the length of his second speech; and at the
end of it he only made him a slight bow, and moved another way: Lord
Rufus then returned to Cliopher.

"I have no reason, I assure you," said he, "to be dissatisfied with my
reception. Lord Damara seemed much pleased with the attention. He
answered me with the utmost civility, and even paid me the compliment of
saying, that he was so well convinced of Princess Anastasiya's
discernment as to be certain she could never bestow a favour unworthily.
It was really a very handsome thought. Upon the whole, I am much pleased
with him."

As Cliopher had no longer any interest of his own to pursue, he turned
his attention almost entirely on his cousin and the Cavalier; and the
train of agreeable reflections which his observations gave birth to made
him perhaps almost as happy as Quintus. He saw him in idea settled in
that very house, in all the felicity which a marriage of true affection
could bestow; and he felt capable, under such circumstances, of
endeavouring even to like the Cavalier's two cousins. His mother's
thoughts he plainly saw were bent the same way, and he determined not to
venture near her, lest he might hear too much. When they sat down to
supper, therefore, he considered it a most unlucky perverseness which
placed them within one of each other; and deeply was he vexed to find
that his mother was talking to that one person (Lady an Dirondelle)
freely, openly, and of nothing else but of her expectation that Quintus
would be soon married to the Cavalier. It was an animating subject, and
Eidora seemed incapable of fatigue while enumerating the advantages of
the match. His being such a charming man, and so rich, and living but
three miles from them, were the first points of self-gratulation; and
then it was such a comfort to think how fond the two cousins were of
Quintus, and to be certain that they must desire the connection as much
as she could do. It was, moreover, such a promising thing for her
younger family members, as Quintus's marrying so greatly must throw them
in the way of other rich people; and, lastly, it was so pleasant at her
time of life to be able to consign her single family members to the care
of their cousin, that she might not be obliged to go into company more
than she liked. It was necessary to make this circumstance a matter of
pleasure, because on such occasions it is the etiquette; but no one was
less likely than Eidora to find comfort in staying at home at any period
of her life. She concluded with many good wishes that Lady an Dirondelle
might soon be equally fortunate, though evidently and triumphantly
believing there was no chance of it.

In vain did Cliopher endeavour to check the rapidity of his mother's
words, or persuade her to describe her felicity in a less audible
whisper; for to his inexpressible vexation she could perceive that the
chief of it was overheard by Lord Damara, who sat opposite to them. His
mother only scolded him for being nonsensical.

"What is Lord Damara to me, pray, that I should be afraid of him? I am
sure we owe him no such particular civility as to be obliged to say
nothing *he* may not like to hear."

"For heaven's sake, madam, speak lower. What advantage can it be to you
to offend Lord Damara? You will never recommend yourself to his friend
by so doing."

Nothing that he could say, however, had any influence. His mother would
talk of her views in the same intelligible tone. Cliopher blushed and
blushed again with shame and vexation. He could not help frequently
glancing his eye at Lord Damara, though every glance convinced him of
what he dreaded; for though he was not always looking at his mother, he
was convinced that his attention was invariably fixed by him. The
expression of his face changed gradually from indignant contempt to a
composed and steady gravity.

At length, however, Eidora had no more to say; and Lady an Dirondelle,
who had been long yawning at the repetition of delights which she saw no
likelihood of sharing, was left to the comforts of cold ham and chicken.
Cliopher now began to revive. But not long was the interval of
tranquillity; for when supper was over, singing was talked of, and he
had the mortification of seeing Louya, after very little entreaty,
preparing to oblige the company. By many significant looks and silent
entreaties did he endeavour to prevent such a proof of
complaisance,---but in vain; Louya would not understand them; such an
opportunity of exhibiting was delightful to her, and she began her song.
Cliopher's eyes were fixed on her, with most painful sensations; and he
watched her progress through the several stanzas with an impatience
which was very ill rewarded at their close; for Louya, on receiving
amongst the thanks of the table the hint of a hope that she might be
prevailed on to favour them again, after the pause of half a minute
began another. Louya's powers were by no means fitted for such a
display; her voice was weak, and her manner affected. Cliopher was in
agonies. He looked at Quintus to see how he bore it; but Quintus was
very composedly talking to the Cavalier. He looked at his two cousins,
and saw them making signs of derision at each other, and at Damara, who
continued, however, impenetrably grave. He looked at his great-uncle to
entreat his interference, lest Louya should be singing all night. He
took the hint, and, when Louya had finished her second song, said
aloud,---

"That will do extremely well, child. You have delighted us long enough.
Let the other young people have time to exhibit."

Louya, though pretending not to hear, was somewhat disconcerted; and
Cliopher, sorry for her, and sorry for his great-uncle's speech, was
afraid his anxiety had done no good. Others of the party were now
applied to.

"If I," said Lord Rufus, "were so fortunate as to be able to sing, I
should have great pleasure, I am sure, in obliging the company with an
air; for I consider music as a very innocent diversion, and perfectly
compatible with my position. I do not mean, however, to assert that we
can be justified in devoting too much of our time to music, for there
are certainly other things to be attended to, for one in my position. In
the first place, they must make such an agreement for tithes as may be
beneficial to themselves and not offensive to their patron. They must
write their own speeches; and the time that remains will not be too much
for their other duties, and the care and improvement of their dwelling,
which they cannot be excused from making as comfortable as possible. And
I do not think it of light importance that they should have attentive
and conciliatory manners towards everybody, especially towards those to
whom they owe their preferment. I cannot acquit them of that duty; nor
could I think well of the one who should omit an occasion of testifying
their respect towards anybody connected with the family." And with a bow
to Lord Damara, he concluded his speech, which had been spoken so loud
as to be heard by half the room. Many stared---many smiled; but no one
looked more amused than Buru Tovo himself, while his niece seriously
commended Lord Rufus for having spoken so sensibly, and observed, in a
half-whisper to Lady an Dirondelle, that he was a remarkably clever,
good kind of man.

To Cliopher it appeared, that had his family made an agreement to expose
themselves as much as they could during the evening, it would have been
impossible for them to play their parts with more spirit, or finer
success; and happy did he think it for the Cavalier and his cousin that
some of the exhibition had escaped his notice, and that his feelings
were not of a sort to be much distressed by the folly which he must have
witnessed. That his two cousins and Lord Damara, however, should have
such an opportunity of ridiculing his relations was bad enough; and he
could not determine whether the silent contempt of the gentleman, or the
insolent smiles of the cousins, were more intolerable.

The rest of the evening brought his little amusement. He was teased by
Lord Rufus, who continued most perseveringly by his side; and though he
could not prevail with Cliopher to dance with him again, put it out of
his power to dance with others. In vain did he entreat him to stand up
with somebody else, and offered to introduce him to any young person in
the room. He assured her that, as to dancing, he was perfectly
indifferent to it; that his chief object was, by delicate attentions, to
recommend himself to Cliopher; and that he should therefore make a point
of remaining close to him the whole evening. There was no arguing upon
such a project. He owed his greatest relief to her friend Felicia, who
often joined them, and good-naturedly engaged Lord Rufus's conversation
to herself.

He was at least free from the offence of Lord Damara's further notice:
though often standing within a very short distance of him, quite
disengaged, he never came near enough to speak. He felt it to be the
probable consequence of his allusions to Saya Ealoapeha, and rejoiced in
it.

The Tahivoa party were the last of all the company to depart; and by a
manœuvre of Eidora Mdang had to wait for their yacht a quarter of an
hour after everybody else was gone, which gave them time to see how
heartily they were wished away by some of the family. Prince Belu and
his cousin scarcely opened their mouths except to complain of fatigue,
and were evidently impatient to have the house to themselves. They
repulsed every attempt of Eidora Mdang at conversation, and, by so
doing, threw a languor over the whole party, which was very little
relieved by the long speeches of Lord Rufus, who was complimenting the
Cavalier and his cousins on the elegance of their entertainment, and the
hospitality and politeness which had marked their behaviour to their
guests. Damara said nothing at all. Buru Tovo, in equal silence, was
enjoying the scene. The Cavalier and Quintus were standing together a
little detached from the rest, and talked only to each other. Cliopher
preserved as steady a silence as either Prince Belu or Lord Lior; and
even Hillen was too much fatigued to utter more than the occasional
exclamation of "Lord, how tired I am!" accompanied by a violent yawn.

When at length they arose to take leave, Eidora Mdang was most
pressingly civil in her hope of seeing the whole family soon at Tahivoa;
and addressed herself particularly to the Cavalier, to assure him how
happy he would make them, by eating a family dinner with them at any
time, without the ceremony of a formal invitation. The Cavalier was all
grateful pleasure; and he readily engaged for taking the earliest
opportunity of waiting on her after his return from Solaara, whither he
was obliged to go the next day for a short time.

Eidora Mdang was perfectly satisfied; and quitted the house under the
delightful persuasion that, allowing for the necessary preparations of
settlements, new yachts, and wedding clothes, she should undoubtedly see
her nephew settled at Navikiani in the course of three or four months.
Of having another family member married to Lord Rufus she thought with
equal certainty, and with considerable, though not equal, pleasure.
Cliopher was the least dear to her of all her family; and though the man
and the match were quite good enough for *her*, the worth of each was
eclipsed by the Cavalier and Navikiani.
