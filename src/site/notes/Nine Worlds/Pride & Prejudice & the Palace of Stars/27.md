With no greater events than these in the Mdang family, and otherwise
diversified by little beyond the walks to Gorjo City, sometimes dirty
and sometimes cold, did January and February pass away. March was to
take Cliopher to visit Felicia. He had not at first thought very
seriously of going thither; but Felicia, he soon found, was depending on
the plan, and he gradually learned to consider it himself with greater
pleasure as well as greater certainty. Absence had increased his desire
of seeing Felicia again, and weakened his disgust of Lord Rufus. There
was novelty in the scheme; and as, with such a mother and such
uncompanionable cousins, home could not be faultless, a little change
was not unwelcome for its own sake. The journey would, moreover, give
him a peep at Quintus; and, in short, as the time drew near, he would
have been very sorry for any delay. Everything, however, went on
smoothly, and was finally settled according to Felicia's first sketch.
He was to accompany Saya Dorn and her second daughter. The improvement
of spending a night in Solaara was added in time, and the plan became as
perfect as plan could be.

The only pain was in leaving his great-uncle, who would certainly miss
him, and who, when it came to the point, so little liked him going, that
he told him to write to him, and almost promised to answer his letter.

The farewell between himself and Saya Ealoapeha was perfectly friendly;
on her side even more. Her present pursuit could not make her forget
that Cliopher had been the first to excite and to deserve her attention,
the first to listen and to pity, the first to be admired; and in her
manner of bidding him adieu, wishing him every enjoyment, reminding him
of what he was to expect in Princess Anastasiya Yra, and trusting their
opinion of him---their opinion of everybody---would always coincide,
there was a solicitude, an interest, which he felt must ever attach him
to her with a most sincere regard; and he parted from her convinced,
that, whether married or single, she must always be his model of the
amiable and pleasing.

His fellow-travellers the next day were not of a kind to make her think
her less agreeable. Saya Dorn, and her daughter Rusticiana, a
good-humoured girl, but as empty-headed as herself, had nothing to say
that could be worth hearing, and were listened to with about as much
delight as the noise of the toilet. Cliopher loved absurdities, but he
had known Saya Dorn's too long. She could tell him nothing new of the
wonders of her presentation; and her civilities were worn out, like her
information.

The final day was a journey of only twenty-four miles, and they began it
so early as to be in Gracechurch Street by noon. As they drove to Aunt
Oura's door, Quintus was at a drawing-room window watching their
arrival: when they entered the passage, she was there to welcome them,
and Cliopher, looking earnestly in his face, was pleased to see it
healthful and lovely as ever. On the stairs were a troop of little boys
and girls, whose eagerness for their cousin's appearance would not allow
them to wait in the drawing-room, and whose shyness, as they had not
seen him for a twelvemonth, prevented their coming lower. All was joy
and kindness. The day passed most pleasantly away; the morning in bustle
and shopping, and the evening at one of the theatres.

Cliopher then contrived to sit by his aunt. Their first subject was his
cousin; and he was more grieved than astonished to hear, in reply to her
minute inquiries, that though Quintus always struggled to support his
spirits, there were periods of dejection. It was reasonable, however, to
hope that they would not continue long. Aunt Oura gave him the
particulars also of Lord Lior's visit in Gracechurch Street, and
repeated conversations occurring at different times between Quintus and
herself, which proved that the former had, from his heart, given up the
acquaintance.

Aunt Oura then rallied her nephew on Ealoapeha's desertion, and
complimented him on bearing it so well.

"But, my dear Cliopher," she added, "what sort of boy is Sayo King? I
should be sorry to think our friend mercenary."

"Pray, my dear aunt, what is the difference in matrimonial affairs,
between the mercenary and the prudent motive? Where does discretion end,
and avarice begin? Last Silverheart you were afraid of her marrying me,
because it would be imprudent; and now, because she is trying to get a
boy with only ten thousand pounds, you want to find out that she is
mercenary."

"If you will only tell me what sort of boy Sayo King is, I shall know
what to think."

"He is a very good kind of boy, I believe. I know no harm of him."

"But she paid him not the smallest attention till his grandfather's
death made him inheritor of this fortune?"

"No---why should she? If it were not allowable for her to gain *my*
affections, because I had no money, what occasion could there be for
making love to a boy whom she did not care about, and who was equally
poor?"

"But there seems indelicacy in directing her attentions towards him so
soon after this event."

"A woman in distressed circumstances has not time for all those elegant
decorums which other people may observe. If *he* does not object to it,
why should *we*?"

"*Him* not objecting does not justify *her*. It only shows him being
deficient in something himself---sense or feeling."

"Well," cried Cliopher, "have it as you choose. *She* shall be
mercenary, and *he* shall be foolish."

"No, Kip, that is what I do *not* choose. I should be sorry, you know,
to think ill of a young woman who has lived so long in Old Damara."

"Oh, if that is all, I have a very poor opinion of people who live in
Old Damara; and their intimate friends who live in the Vangaveye-ve are
not much better. I am sick of them all. Thank heaven! I am going
to-morrow where I shall find a man who has not one agreeable quality,
who has neither manners nor sense to recommend him. Stupid men are the
only ones worth knowing, after all."

"Take care, Kip; that speech savours strongly of disappointment."

Before they were separated by the conclusion of the play, he had the
unexpected happiness of an invitation to accompany his aunt in a tour of
pleasure which she proposed taking in the summer.

"I have not quite determined how far it shall carry me," said Aunt Oura;
"but perhaps, to the Lakes."

No scheme could have been more agreeable to Cliopher, and his acceptance
of the invitation was most ready and grateful. "My dear, dear aunt," he
rapturously cried, "what delight! what felicity! You give me fresh life
and vigour. Adieu to disappointment and spleen. What are men to rocks
and mountains? Oh, what hours of transport we shall spend! And when we
*do* return, it shall not be like other travellers, without being able
to give one accurate idea of anything. We *will* know where we have
gone---we *will* recollect what we have seen. Lakes, mountains, and
rivers, shall not be jumbled together in our imaginations; nor, when we
attempt to describe any particular scene, will we begin quarrelling
about its relative situation. Let *our* first effusions be less
insupportable than those of the generality of travellers."
