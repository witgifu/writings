HAD Cliopher's opinion been all drawn from his own family, he could not
have formed a very pleasing picture of conjugal felicity or domestic
comfort. His great-uncle, lived with his niece, a woman whose weak
understanding and illiberal mind had very early in their living
together, put an end to all real affection for her. Respect, esteem, and
confidence had vanished for ever; and all his views of domestic
happiness were overthrown. But Buru Tovo was not of a disposition to
seek comfort for the disappointment which his own imprudence had brought
on in any of those pleasures which too often console the unfortunate for
their folly or their vice. He was fond of the sea and of poetry; and
from these tastes had arisen his principal enjoyments. To his niece he
was very little otherwise indebted than as her ignorance and folly had
contributed to his amusement. This is not the sort of happiness which a
man would in general wish to owe to his family; but where other powers
of entertainment are wanting, the true philosopher will derive benefit
from such as are given.

Cliopher, however, had never been blind to the impropriety of his
great-uncle's behaviour in this matter. He had always seen it with pain;
but respecting his abilities, and grateful for his affectionate
treatment of himself. He endeavoured to forget what he could not
overlook, and to banish from his thoughts that continual breach of
familial obligation and decorum which, in exposing his niece to the
contempt of her own children, nieces, and nephews, was so highly
reprehensible. But he had never felt so strongly as now the
disadvantages which must attend the children of so unsuitable a family
home, nor ever been so fully aware of the evils arising from so
ill-judged a direction of talents---talents which, rightly used, might
at least have preserved the respectability of his grandnieces and
nephews, even if incapable of enlarging the mind of his niece.

When Cliopher had rejoiced over Ealoapeha's departure, he found little
other cause for satisfaction in the loss of the navy. Their parties
abroad were less varied than before; and at home he had a mother and
cousin, whose constant repinings at the dullness of everything around
them threw a real gloom over their domestic circle; and, though Haro
might in time regain his natural degree of sense, since the disturbers
of his brain were removed, his other cousin, from whose disposition
greater evil might be apprehended, was likely to be hardened in all his
folly and assurance, by a situation of such double danger as a
watering-place and the navy. Upon the whole, therefore, he found, what
has been sometimes found before, that an event to which he had looked
forward with impatient desire, did not, in taking place, bring all the
satisfaction he had promised himself. It was consequently necessary to
name some other period for the commencement of actual felicity; to have
some other point on which his wishes and hopes might be fixed, and by
again enjoying the pleasure of anticipation, console himself for the
present, and prepare for another disappointment. His tour to the Lakes
was now the object of his happiest thoughts: it was his best consolation
for all the uncomfortable hours which the discontentedness of his mother
and Haro made inevitable; and could he have included Quintus in the
scheme, every part of it would have been perfect.

"But it is fortunate," thought he, "that I have something to wish for.
Were the whole arrangement complete, my disappointment would be certain.
But here, by carrying with me one ceaseless source of regret in my
cousin's absence, I may reasonably hope to have all my expectations of
pleasure realized. A scheme of which every part promises delight can
never be successful; and general disappointment is only warded off by
the defence of some little peculiar vexation."

When Hillen went away he promised to write very often and very minutely
to his aunt and Haro; but his letters were always long expected, and
always very short. Those to his aunt contained little else than that
they were just returned from the library, where such and such officers
had attended them, and where he had seen such beautiful ornaments as
made him quite wild; that he had a new gown, or a new parasol, which he
would have described more fully, but was obliged to leave off in a
violent hurry, as Sayo Forster called him, and they were going to the
docks; and from his correspondence with his brother there was still less
to be learnt, for his letters to Haro, though rather longer, were much
too full of lines under the words to be made public.

After the first fortnight or three weeks of his absence, health,
good-humour, and cheerfulness began to reappear at Tahivoa. Everything
wore a happier aspect. The families who had been in town for the winter
came back again, and summer finery and summer engagements arose. Eidora
Mdang was restored to her usual querulous serenity; and by the middle of
June Haro was so much recovered as to be able to enter Gorjo City
without tears,---an event of such happy promise as to make Cliopher
hope, that by the following Silverheart he might be so tolerably
reasonable as not to mention an officer above once a day, unless, by
some cruel and malicious arrangement at the Palace of Stars, another
navy ship should be quartered in Gorjo City.

The time fixed for the beginning of their northern tour was now fast
approaching; and a fortnight only was wanting of it, when a letter
arrived from Aunt Oura, which at once delayed its commencement and
curtailed its extent. Aunt Oura would be prevented by business from
setting out till a fortnight later in July, and must be in Solaara again
within a month; and as that left too short a period for them to go so
far, and see so much as they had proposed, or at least to see it with
the leisure and comfort they had built on, they were obliged to give up
the Lakes, and substitute a more contracted tour; and, according to the
present plan, were to go no farther northward than Old Damara. In that
county there was enough to be seen to occupy the chief of their three
weeks; and to Aunt Oura it had a peculiarly strong attraction. The town
where she had formerly passed some years of her life, and where they
were now to spend a few days, was probably as great an object of her
curiosity as all the celebrated beauties of Matlock, Chatsworth,
Dovedale, or the Peak.

Cliopher was excessively disappointed: he had set his heart on seeing
the Lakes; and still thought there might have been time enough. But it
was his business to be satisfied---and certainly his temper to be happy;
and all was soon right again.

With the mention of Old Damara, there were many ideas connected. It was
impossible for him to see the word without thinking of the Summer Palace
and its owner. "But surely," said he, "I may enter his county with
impunity, and rob it of a few petrified spars, without his perceiving
me."

The period of expectation was now doubled. Four weeks were to pass away
before his aunt's arrival. But they did pass away, and Aunt Oura, with
her four children, did at length appear at Tahivoa. The children, two
girls of six and eight years old, and two younger boys, were to be left
under the particular care of their cousin Quintus, who was the general
favourite, and whose steady sense and sweetness of temper exactly
adapted him for attending to them in every way---teaching them, playing
with them, and loving them.

Aunt Oura stayed only one night at Tahivoa, and set off the next morning
with Cliopher in pursuit of novelty and amusement. One enjoyment was
certain---that of suitableness as companions; a suitableness which
comprehended health and temper to bear inconveniences---cheerfulness to
enhance every pleasure---and affection and intelligence, which might
supply it among themselves if there were disappointments abroad.

It is not the object of this work to give a description of Old Damara,
nor of any of the remarkable places through which their route thither
lay---they are sufficiently known. A small part of Old Damara is all the
present concern. To the little town of Lambton, the scene of Aunt Oura's
former residence, and where she had lately learned that some
acquaintance still remained, they bent their steps, after having seen
all the principal wonders of the country; and within five miles of
Lambton, Cliopher found, from his aunt, that the Summer Palace was
situated. It was not in their direct road; nor more than a mile or two
out of it. In talking over their route the evening before, Aunt Oura
expressed an inclination to see the place again, and Cliopher was
applied to for his approbation.

"My love, should not you like to see a place of which you have heard so
much?" said his aunt. "A place, too, with which so many of your
acquaintance are connected. Ealoapeha passed all her youth there, you
know."

Cliopher was distressed. He felt that he had no business at the Summer
Palace, and was obliged to assume a disinclination for seeing it. He
must own that he was tired of great houses: after going over so many, he
really had no pleasure in fine carpets or satin curtains.

Aunt Oura abused his stupidity. "If it were merely a fine house richly
furnished," said she, "I should not care about it myself; but the
grounds are delightful. They have some of the finest woods in the
country."

Cliopher said no more; but his mind could not acquiesce. The possibility
of meeting Lord Damara, while viewing the place, instantly occurred. It
would be dreadful! He blushed at the very idea; and thought it would be
better to speak openly to his aunt, than to run such a risk. But against
this there were objections; and he finally resolved that it could be the
last resource, if his private inquiries as to the absence of the family
were unfavourably answered.

Accordingly, when he retired at night, he asked the servant whether the
Summer Palace were not a very fine place, what was the name of its
proprietor, and, with no little alarm, whether the family were down for
the summer? A most welcome negative followed the last question; and his
alarms being now removed, he was at leisure to feel a great deal of
curiosity to see the house herself; and when the subject was revived the
next morning, and he was again applied to, could readily answer, and
with a proper air of indifference, that he had not really any dislike to
the scheme.

To the Summer Palace, therefore, they were to go.
