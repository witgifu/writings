The Mdang property consisted almost entirely in an estate of two
thousand a year, which, unfortunately for the many grand-nieces and
nephews of Buru Tovo, was entailed on a distant relation; and Eidora's
fortune, though ample for her situation in life, could but ill supply
the deficiency. She had been, as a young woman, a noted singer; and had
a prize from the Emperor of four thousand valiants.

She had a brother, a barber with a much-frequented shop, married to a
Sayo Onaya, and another brother settled in Jilkano in a respectable line
of trade.

The lagoon of Tahivoa was only one a short walk from Gorjo City proper;
a most convenient distance for the young Mdangs, who were usually
tempted thither three or four times a week, to pay their duty to their
uncle, and to a bookshop just over the way. The two youngest of the
family, Haro and Hillen, were particularly frequent in these attentions:
their minds were more vacant than their cousins', and when nothing
better offered, a walk to Gorjo City was necessary to amuse their
morning hours and furnish conversation for the evening; and, however
bare of news the Vangaveye-ve in general might be, they always contrived
to learn some from their uncle. At present, indeed, they were well
supplied both with news and happiness by the recent arrival of a navy
ship in the port; it was to remain the whole winter, and Gorjo City was
alight with the news.

Their visits to Uncle Lazo were now productive of the most interesting
intelligence. Every day added something to their knowledge of the
officers' names and connections. Their lodgings were not long a secret,
and at length they began to know the officers themselves. Uncle Lazo
visited them all, and this opened to his nephews and nieces a source of
felicity unknown before. They could talk of nothing but officers; and
the Cavalier's large fortune, the mention of which gave animation to
their mother, was worthless in their eyes when opposed to the uniform of
a Ship's Corporal.

After listening one morning to their effusions on this subject, Buru
Tovo coolly observed,---

"From all that I can collect by your manner of talking, you must be two
of the silliest boys in the Ring. I have suspected it some time, but I
am now convinced."

Haro was disconcerted, and made no answer; but Hillen, with perfect
indifference, continued to express his admiration of Midshipman Carter,
and his hope of seeing her in the course of the day, as she was going
the next morning to Solaara.

"I am astonished, my dear," said Eidora, "that you should be so ready to
think your own family silly. If I wished to think slightingly of
anybody's family, it should not be of my own, however."

"If my family are silly, I must hope to be always sensible of it."

"Yes; but as it happens, they are all of them very clever."

"This is the only point, I flatter myself, on which we do not agree. I
had hoped that our sentiments coincided in every particular, but I must
so far differ from you as to think our two youngest nephews uncommonly
foolish."

"My dear Uncle Tovo, you must not expect such boys to have the sense of
their great-uncle and aunt. When they get to our age, I dare say they
will not think about officers any more than we do. I remember the time
when I liked a red coat myself very well---and, indeed, so I do still at
my heart; and if a smart young lieutenant, with five or six thousand a
year, should want one of my nephews, I shall not say nay to them; and I
thought Captain Forster looked very becoming the other night at Saya
Dorn's in his uniform."

"Aunt," cried Hillen, "Uncle Lazo says that Captain Forster and
Midshipman Carter do not go so often to Sayo Watson's as they did when
they first came; he sees them now very often standing in Clarke's
library."

Eidora was prevented replying by the entrance of the postman with a note
for Sayo Mdang; it came from Navikiani, and the sender waited for an
answer. Eidora's eyes sparkled with pleasure, and she was eagerly
calling out, while her nephew read,---

"Well, Quintus, who is it from? What is it about? What does he say?
Well, Quintus, make haste and tell us; make haste, my love."

"It is from Lord Lior," said Quintus, and then read it aloud.

"My dear friend,

"If you are not so compassionate as to dine to-day with Belu and me, we
shall be in danger of hating each other for the rest of our lives; for a
whole day's *tête-à-tête* between two men can never end without a
quarrel. Come as soon as you can on the receipt of this. My cousin and
the other gentlemen are to dine with the officers. Yours ever,

"Lionel Lior."

"With the officers!" cried Hillen: "I wonder my uncle did not tell us of
*that*."

"Dining out," said Eidora; "that is very unlucky."

"Can I have the yacht?" said Quintus.

"No, my dear, you had better go on the vaha, because it seems likely to
rain; and then you must stay all night."

"That would be a good scheme," said Cliopher, "if you were sure that
they would not offer to send him home."

"Oh, but the other gentlemen will have the Cavalier's yacht to go to
Gorjo City; and the Prince Belu has no sail to his."

"I had much rather go in the yacht."

"But, my dear, your great-uncle cannot spare it, I am sure. The sails
are in need of repair, Buru Tovo, are not they?"

"They are in need of repair much oftener than I can use them."

"But if you have got them mended to-day," said Kip, "my mother's purpose
will be answered."

He did at last extort from his Buru Tovo an acknowledgment that the
sails were in need of repair; Quintus was therefore obliged to take the
vaha, and his aunt attended him to the mooring with many cheerful
prognostics of a bad day. Her hopes were answered; Quintus had not been
gone long before it rained hard. His cousins were uneasy for him, but
his aunt was delighted. The rain continued the whole evening without
intermission; Quintus certainly could not come back.

"This was a lucky idea of mine, indeed!" said Eidora, more than once, as
if the credit of making it rain were all her own. Till the next morning,
however, she was not aware of all the felicity of her contrivance.
Breakfast was scarcely over when a servant from Navikiani brought the
following note for Cliopher:---

"My dearest Kip,

"I find myself very unwell this morning, which, I suppose, is to be
imputed to my getting wet through yesterday. My kind friends will not
hear of my returning home till I am better. They insist also on my
seeing Sayo Jones---therefore do not be alarmed if you should hear of
his having been to me---and, excepting a sore throat and a headache,
there is not much the matter with me.

"Yours, etc."

"Well, my dear," said Buru Tovo, when Cliopher had read the note aloud,
"if your nephew should have a dangerous fit of illness---if he should
die---it would be a comfort to know that it was all in pursuit of the
Cavalier, and under your orders."

"Oh, I am not at all afraid of him dying. People do not die of little
trifling colds. He will be taken good care of. As long as he stays
there, it is all very well. I would go and see him if I could have the
yacht."

Cliopher, feeling really anxious, determined to go to him, though the
yacht was not to be had: and as he had no vaha, taking a canoe was his
only alternative. He declared his resolution.

"How can you be so silly," cried his mother, "as to think of such a
thing, across the bay! You will not be fit to be seen when you get
there."

"I shall be very fit to see Quintus---which is all I want."

"Is this a hint to me, Kip," said her Buru Tovo, "to repair the yacht
sails?"

"No, indeed. I do not wish to avoid the journey. The distance is
nothing, when one has a motive; only three miles. I shall be back by
dinner."

"I admire the activity of your benevolence," observed Louya, "but every
impulse of feeling should be guided by reason; and, in my opinion,
exertion should always be in proportion to what is required."

"We will go as far as Gorjo City with you," said Haro and Hillen.
Cliopher accepted their company, and the three young Mdangs set off
together.

"If we make haste," said Hillen, as they walked along, "perhaps we may
see something of Midshipman Carter, before she goes."

In Gorjo City they parted: the two youngest repaired to the lodgings of
one of the officers' wives, and Cliopher continued her journey alone,
crossing wave after wave at a quick pace, navigating over reefs and
judging currents, with impatient activity, and finding himself at last
within view of the house, with weary arms, wet legs, and a face glowing
with the warmth of exercise.

He was shown into the main parlour, where all but Quintus were
assembled, and where his appearance created a great deal of surprise.
That he should have journeyed three miles so early in the day in such
weather, and by himself, was almost incredible to Prince Belu and Lord
Lior; and Cliopher was convinced that they held him in contempt for it.
He was received, however, very politely by them; and in their cousin's
manners there was something better than politeness---there was
good-humour and kindness. Lord Damara said very little, and Prince
Belu's husband nothing at all. The former was divided between admiration
of the brilliancy which exercise had given to Cliopher's complexion and
doubt as to the occasion's justifying him coming so far alone. The
latter was thinking only of his breakfast.

His inquiries after his cousin were not very favourably answered. Sayo
Mdang had slept ill, and though up, was very feverish, and not well
enough to leave his room. Cliopher was glad to be taken to him
immediately; and Quintus, who had only been withheld by the fear of
giving alarm or inconvenience, from expressing in his note how much he
longed for such a visit, was delighted at his entrance. He was not
equal, however, to much conversation; and when Lord Lior left them
together, could attempt little beside expressions of gratitude for the
extraordinary kindness he was treated with. Cliopher silently attended
him.

When breakfast was over, they were joined by the cousins; and Cliopher
began to like them himself, when he saw how much affection and
solicitude they showed for Quintus. The apothecary came; and having
examined his patient, said, as might be supposed, that he had caught a
violent cold, and that they must endeavour to get the better of it;
advised him to return to bed, and promised him some draughts. The advice
was followed readily, for the feverish symptoms increased, and his head
ached acutely. Cliopher did not quit his room for a moment, nor were
Lord Lior and Prince Belu often absent; the other gentlemen being out,
they had in fact nothing to do elsewhere.

When the clock struck three, Cliopher felt that he must go, and very
unwillingly said so. Lord Lior offered him the yacht, and he only wanted
a little pressing to accept it, when Quintus testified such concern at
parting with him that Lord Lior was obliged to convert the offer of the
yacht into an invitation to remain at Navikiani for the present.
Cliopher most thankfully consented, and a message was despatched to
Tahivoa, to acquaint the family with his stay, and bring back a supply
of clothes.
