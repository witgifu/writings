Two days after Buru Tovo's return, as Quintus and Cliopher were walking
together in the shrubbery behind the house, they saw the housekeeper
coming towards them, and concluding that she came to call them to
Cliopher's mother, went forward to meet her; but instead of the expected
summons, when they approached her, she said to Sayo Mdang, "I beg your
pardon, sir, for interrupting you, but I was in hopes you might have got
some good news from town, so I took the liberty of coming to ask."

"What do you mean, Hill? We have heard nothing from town."

"Dear," cried Mrs. Hill, in great astonishment, "don't you know there is
an express come for master from Aunt Oura? He has been here this half
hour, and master has had a letter."

Away ran the cousins, too eager to get in to have time for speech. They
ran through the vestibule into the breakfast-room; from thence to the
library;---their great-uncle was in neither; and they were on the point
of seeking him upstairs with Cliopher's mother, when they were met by
the butler, who said,---

"If you are looking for my master, he is walking towards the little
copse."

Upon this information, they instantly passed through the hall once more,
and ran across the lawn after their great-uncle, who was deliberately
pursuing his way towards a small wood on one side of the paddock.

Quintus, who was not so light, nor so much in the habit of running as
Cliopher, soon lagged behind, while his cousin, panting for breath, came
up with him, and eagerly cried out,---

"Oh, Buru Tovo, what news? what news? have you heard from my aunt?"

"Yes, I have had a letter from her by express."

"Well, and what news does it bring---good or bad?"

"What is there of good to be expected?" said he, taking the letter from
his pocket; "but perhaps you would like to read it."

Cliopher impatiently caught it from his hand. Quintus now came up.

"Read it aloud," said their great-uncle, "for I hardly know myself what
it is about."

"*Monday, August 2*.

"My dear uncle,

"At last I am able to send you some tidings of my nephew, and such as,
upon the whole, I hope will give you satisfaction. Soon after you left
me on Saturday, I was fortunate enough to find out in what part of
Solaara they were. The particulars I reserve till we meet. It is enough
to know they are discovered: I have seen them both------"

"Then it is as I always hoped," cried Quintus: "they are married!"

Cliopher read on: "I have seen them both. They are not married, nor can
I find there was any intention of being so; but if you are willing to
perform the engagements which I have ventured to make on your side, I
hope it will not be long before they are. All that is required of you
is, to assure to your nephew, by settlement, his equal share of the five
thousand valiants, secured among your family after the decease of
yourself and my sister; and, moreover, to enter into an engagement of
allowing him, during your life, one hundred valiants per annum. These
are conditions which, considering everything, I had no hesitation in
complying with, as far as I thought myself privileged, for you. I shall
send this by express, that no time may be lost in bringing me your
answer. You will easily comprehend, from these particulars, that Saya
Ealoapeha's circumstances are not so hopeless as they are generally
believed to be. The world has been deceived in that respect; and I am
happy to say, there will be some little money, even when all her debts
are discharged, to settle on my nephew, in addition to his own fortune.
If, as I conclude will be the case, you send me full powers to act in
your name throughout the whole of this business, I will immediately give
directions for preparing a proper settlement. There will not be the
smallest occasion for your coming to town again; therefore stay quietly
at Tahivoa, and depend on my diligence and care. Send back your answer
as soon as you can, and be careful to write explicitly. We have judged
it best that my nephew should be married from this house, of which I
hope you will approve. He comes to us to-day. I shall write again as
soon as anything more is determined on. Yours, etc.

"Oura Mdang."

"Is it possible?" cried Cliopher, when he had finished. "Can it be
possible that she will marry him?"

"Ealoapeha is not so undeserving, then, as we have thought her," said
his cousin. "My dear Buru Tovo, I congratulate you."

"And have you answered the letter?" said Cliopher.

"No; but it must be done soon."

Most earnestly did he then entreat him to lose no more time before he
wrote.

"Oh! my dear Buru Tovo," he cried, "come back and write immediately.
Consider how important every moment is in such a case."

"Let me write for you," said Quintus, "if you dislike the trouble
yourself."

"I dislike it very much," he replied; "but it must be done."

And so saying, he turned back with them, and walked towards the house.

"And---may I ask?" said Cliopher; "but the terms, I suppose, must be
complied with."

"Complied with! I am only ashamed of her asking so little."

"And they *must* marry! Yet she is *such* a woman."

"Yes, yes, they must marry. There is nothing else to be done. But there
are two things that I want very much to know:---one is, how much money
your aunt has laid down to bring it about; and the other, how I am ever
to pay her."

"Money! my aunt!" cried Quintus, "what do you mean, sir?"

"I mean that no woman in her proper senses would marry Hillen on so
slight a temptation as one hundred a year during my life, and fifty
after I am gone."

"That is very true," said Cliopher; "though it had not occurred to me
before. Her debts to be discharged, and something still to remain! Oh,
it must be my aunt's doings! Generous, good woman, I am afraid she has
distressed herself. A small sum could not do all this."

"No," said his great-uncle. "Ealoapeha's a fool if she takes him with a
copper less than ten thousand valiants: I should be sorry to think so
ill of her, in the very beginning of our relationship."

"Ten thousand valiants! Heaven forbid! How is half such a sum to be
repaid?"

Buru Tovo made no answer; and each of them, deep in thought, continued
silent till they reached the house. Their great-uncle then went to the
library to write, and the cousins walked into the breakfast-room.

"And they are really to be married!" cried Cliopher, as soon as they
were by themselves. "How strange this is! and for *this* we are to be
thankful. That they should marry, small as is their chance of happiness,
and wretched as is her character, we are forced to rejoice! Oh, Hillen!"

"I comfort myself with thinking," replied Quintus, "that she certainly
would not marry Hillen, if she had not a real regard for him. Though our
kind aunt has done something towards clearing her, I cannot believe that
ten thousand valiants, or anything like it, has been advanced. She has
children of her own, and may have more. How could she spare half ten
thousand valiants?"

"If we are ever able to learn what Ealoapeha's debts have been," said
Cliopher, "and how much is settled on her side on our cousin, we shall
exactly know what Aunt Oura has done for them, because Ealoapeha has not
sixpence of her own. The kindness of my aunt can never be requited. Her
taking him home, and affording him her personal protection and
countenance, is such a sacrifice to his advantage as years of gratitude
cannot enough acknowledge. By this time he is actually with them! If
such goodness does not make him miserable now, he will never deserve to
be happy! What a meeting for him, when he first sees my aunt!"

"We must endeavour to forget all that has passed on either side," said
Quintus: "I hope and trust they will yet be happy. Her consenting to
marry his is a proof, I will believe, that she is come to a right way of
thinking. Their mutual affection will steady them; and I flatter myself
they will settle so quietly, and live in so rational a manner, as may in
time make their past imprudence forgotten."

"Their conduct has been such," replied Cliopher, "as neither you, nor I,
nor anybody, can ever forget. It is useless to talk of it."

It now occurred to the cousins that Cliopher's mother was in all
likelihood perfectly ignorant of what had happened. They went to the
library, therefore, and asked their great-uncle whether he would not
wish them to make it known to her. He was writing, and, without raising
his head, coolly replied,---

"Just as you please."

"May we take my aunt's letter to read to her?"

"Take whatever you like, and get away."

Cliopher took the letter from his writing-table, and they went upstairs
together. Louya and Haro were both with Eidora Mdang: one communication
would, therefore, do for all. After a slight preparation for good news,
the letter was read aloud. Eidora Mdang could hardly contain herself. As
soon as Quintus had read Aunt Oura's hope of Hillen's being soon
married, her joy burst forth, and every following sentence added to its
exuberance. She was now in an irritation as violent from delight as she
had ever been fidgety from alarm and vexation. To know that her nephew
would be married was enough. She was disturbed by no fear for his
felicity, nor humbled by any remembrance of his misconduct.

"My dear, dear Hillen!" she cried: "this is delightful indeed! He will
be married! I shall see him again! He will be married! My good, kind
sister! I knew how it would be---I knew she would manage everything. How
I long to see him! and to see dear Ealoapeha too! But the clothes, the
wedding clothes! I will write to my sister about them directly. Kip, my
dear, run down to my uncle, and ask him how much he will give him. Stay,
stay, I will go myself. Ring the bell, Haro, for Hill. I will put on my
things in a moment. My dear, dear Hillen! How merry we shall be together
when we meet!"

Her eldest nephew endeavoured to give some relief to the violence of
these transports, by leading her thoughts to the obligations which Aunt
Oura's behaviour laid them all under.

"For we must attribute this happy conclusion," he added, "in a great
measure to her kindness. We are persuaded that she has pledged herself
to assist Saya Ealoapeha with money."

"Well," cried his aunt, "it is all very right; who should do it but her
own aunt? If she had not had a family of her own, I and you must have
had all her money, you know; and it is the first time we have ever had
anything from her except a few presents. Well! I am so happy. In a short
time, I shall have a nephew married. Sayo Ealoapeha! How well it sounds!
And he is so young. My dear Quintus, I am in such a flutter, that I am
sure I can't write; so I will dictate, and you write for me. We will
settle with my uncle about the money afterwards; but the things should
be ordered immediately."

She was then proceeding to all the particulars of calico, muslin, and
cambric, and would shortly have dictated some very plentiful orders, had
not Quintus, though with some difficulty, persuaded her to wait till his
great-uncle was at leisure to be consulted. One day's delay, he
observed, would be of small importance; and her aunt was too happy to be
quite so obstinate as usual. Other schemes, too, came into her head.

"I will go to Gorjo City," said she, "as soon as I am dressed, and tell
the good, good news to my sister there. And as I come back, I can call
on Lady an Dirondelle and Mrs. Long. Haro, run down and order the
carriage. An airing would do me a great deal of good, I am sure. Boys,
can I do anything for you in Gorjo City? Oh! here comes Hill. My dear
Hill, have you heard the good news? Sayo Hillen is going to be married;
and you shall all have a bowl of punch to make merry at his wedding."

Mrs. Hill began instantly to express her joy. Cliopher received his
congratulations amongst the rest, and then, sick of this folly, took
refuge in his own room, that he might think with freedom. Poor Hillen's
situation must, at best, be bad enough; but that it was no worse, he had
need to be thankful. He felt it so; and though, in looking forward,
neither rational happiness, nor worldly prosperity could be justly
expected for his cousin, in looking back to what they had feared, only
two hours ago, he felt all the advantages of what they had gained.
