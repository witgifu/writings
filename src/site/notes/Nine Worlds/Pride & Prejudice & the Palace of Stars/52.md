Cliopher had the satisfaction of receiving an answer to his letter as
soon as he possibly could. He was no sooner in possession of it, than
hurrying into the little copse, where he was least likely to be
interrupted, he sat down on one of the benches, and prepared to be
happy; for the length of the letter convinced him that it did not
contain a denial.

"Gracechurch Street, *Sept. 6*.

"My dear Nephew,

"I have just received your letter, and shall devote this whole morning
to answering it, as I foresee that a *little* writing will not comprise
what I have to tell you. I must confess myself surprised by your
application; I did not expect it from *you*. Don't think me angry,
however, for I only mean to let you know, that I had not imagined such
inquiries to be necessary on *your* side. If you do not choose to
understand me, forgive my impertinence. Nothing but the belief of your
being a party concerned would have allowed me to act as I have done. But
if you are really innocent and ignorant, I must be more explicit. On the
very day of my coming home from Tahivoa, I had a most unexpected
visitor. Lord Damara called, he came to tell me that he had found out
where your cousin and Saya Ealoapeha were, and that he had seen and
talked with them both---Ealoapeha repeatedly, Hillen once. From what I
can collect, he left Old Damara only one day after ourselves, and came
to town with the resolution of hunting for them. The motive professed
was his conviction of its being owing to himself that Ealoapeha's
worthlessness had not been so well known as to make it impossible for
any young person of character to love or confide in her. He generously
imputed the whole to his mistaken pride, and confessed that he had
before thought it beneath him to lay his private actions open to the
world. His character was to speak for itself. He called it, therefore,
his duty to step forward, and endeavour to remedy an evil which had been
brought on by himself. If he *had another* motive, I am sure it would
never disgrace him. He had been some days in town before he was able to
discover them; but he had something to direct his search, which was more
than *I* had; and the consciousness of this was another reason for his
resolving to follow us. There is a lady, it seems, a Saya Younge, who
was some time ago governess to Lady Damara, and was dismissed from her
charge on some cause of disapprobation, though he did not say what. She
then took a large house in Edward Street, and has since maintained
herself by letting lodgings. This Saya Younge was, he knew, intimately
acquainted with Ealoapeha; and he went to her for intelligence, as soon
as he got to town. But it was two or three days before he could get from
her what he wanted. She would not betray her trust, I suppose, without
bribery and corruption, for she really did know where her friend was to
be found. Ealoapeha, indeed, had gone to her on their first arrival in
Solaara; and had she been able to receive them into her house, they
would have taken up their abode with her. At length, however, our kind
friend procured the wished-for direction. They were in ------ Street. He
saw Ealoapeha, and afterwards insisted on seeing Hillen. His first
object with him, he acknowledged, had been to persuade him to quit his
present disgraceful situation, and return to his friends as soon as they
could be prevailed on to receive him, offering his assistance as far as
it would go. But he found Hillen absolutely resolved on remaining where
he was. He cared for none of his friends; he wanted no help from Lord
Damara; he would not hear of leaving Ealoapeha. He was sure they should
be married some time or other, and it did not much signify when. Since
such were his feelings, it only remained, he thought, to secure and
expedite a marriage, which, in his very first conversation with
Ealoapeha, he easily learnt had never been *her* design. She confessed
herself obliged to leave the navy on account of some debts of honour
which were very pressing; and scrupled not to lay all the ill
consequences of Hillen's flight on his own folly alone. She meant to
resign her commission immediately; and as to her future situation, she
could conjecture very little about it. She must go somewhere, but she
did not know where, and she knew she should have nothing to live on.
Lord Damara asked why she did not marry your cousin at once. Though Buru
Tovo was not imagined to be very rich, he would have been able to do
something for her, and her situation must have been benefited by
marriage. But he found, in reply to this question, that Ealoapeha still
cherished the hope of more effectually making her fortune by marriage,
in some other place. Under such circumstances, however, she was not
likely to be proof against the temptation of immediate relief. They met
several times, for there was much to be discussed. Ealoapeha, of course,
wanted more than she could get; but at length was reduced to be
reasonable. Everything being settled between *them*, Lord Damara's next
step was to make me acquainted with it, and he first called in
Gracechurch Street. But I could not be seen; and Lord Damara found, on
further inquiry, that your great-uncle was still with me, but would quit
town the next morning. He did not judge your great-uncle to be a person
whom he could so properly consult as your aunt, and therefore readily
postponed seeing her till after the departure of the former. He did not
leave his name, and till the next day it was only known that a gentleman
had called on business. On Saturday he came again. Your great-uncle was
gone, I at home, and, as I said before, we had a great deal of talk
together. We met again on Sunday. It was not all settled before Monday:
as soon as it was, the express was sent off to Tahivoa. But our visitor
was very obstinate. I fancy, Kip, that obstinacy is the real defect of
his character, after all. He has been accused of many faults at
different times; but *this* is the true one. Nothing was to be done that
he did not do himself; though (and I do not speak it to be thanked,
therefore say nothing about it) I would most readily have settled the
whole. We battled it together for a long time, which was more than
either the gentleman or lady concerned in it deserved. But at last I was
forced to yield, and instead of being allowed to be of use to my nephew,
was forced to put up with only having the probable credit of it, which
went sorely against the grain; and my letter this morning gave me great
pleasure, because it required an explanation that would rob me of my
borrowed feathers, and give the praise where it is due. But, Kip, this
must go no further than yourself, or Quintus at most. You know pretty
well, I suppose, what has been done for the young people. Her debts are
to be paid, amounting, I believe, to considerably more than a thousand
valiants, another thousand in addition to his own settled upon *him*,
and her commission purchased. The reason why all this was to be done by
Lord Damara alone, was such as I have given above. It was owing to him,
to his reserve and want of proper consideration, that Ealoapeha's
character had been so misunderstood, and consequently that she had been
received and noticed as she was. Perhaps there was some truth in *this*;
though I doubt whether *his* reserve, or *anybody's* reserve can be
answerable for the event. But in spite of all this fine talking, my dear
Kip, you may rest perfectly assured that I would never have yielded, if
I had not given him credit for *another interest* in the affair. When
all this was resolved on, he returned again to his friends, who were
still staying at the Summer Palace; but it was agreed that he should be
in Solaara once more when the wedding took place, and all money matters
were then to receive the last finish. I believe I have now told you
everything. It is a relation which you tell me is to give you great
surprise; I hope at least it will not afford you any displeasure. Hillen
came to us, and Ealoapeha had constant admission to the house. *She* was
exactly what she had been when I knew her in the Vangaveye-ve; but I
would not tell you how little I was satisfied with *his* behaviour while
he stayed with us, if I had not perceived, by Quintus's letter last
Wednesday, that his conduct on coming home was exactly of a piece with
it, and therefore what I now tell you can give you no fresh pain. I
talked to him repeatedly in the most serious manner, representing to him
the wickedness of what he had done, and all the unhappiness he had
brought on her family. If he heard me, it was by good luck, for I am
sure he did not listen. I was sometimes quite provoked; but then I
recollected my dear Cliopher and Quintus, and for their sakes had
patience with him. Lord Damara was punctual in his return, and, as
Hillen informed you, attended the wedding. He dined with me the next
day, and was to leave town again on Wednesday or Thursday. Will you be
very angry with me, my dear Kip, if I take this opportunity of saying
(what I was never bold enough to say before) how much I like him? His
behaviour to us has, in every respect, been as pleasing as when we were
in Old Damara. His understanding and opinions all please me; he wants
nothing but a little more liveliness, and *that*, if he marry
*prudently*, his spouse may teach him. I thought him very sly; he hardly
ever mentioned your name. But slyness seems the fashion. Pray forgive
me, if I have been very presuming, or at least do not punish me so far
as to exclude me. I shall never be quite happy till I have been all
round the park. A low phaeton with a nice little pair of ponies would be
the very thing. But I must write no more. The children have been wanting
me this half hour.

"Yours, very sincerely,\
"Aunt Oura"

The contents of this letter threw Cliopher into a flutter of spirits, in
which it was difficult to determine whether pleasure or pain bore the
greatest share. The vague and unsettled suspicions which uncertainty had
produced, of what Lord Damara might have been doing to forward his
cousin's match---which he had feared to encourage, as an exertion of
goodness too great to be probable, and at the same time dreaded to be
just, from the pain of obligation---were proved beyond their greatest
extent to be true! He had followed them purposely to town, he had taken
on himself all the trouble and mortification attendant on such a
research; in which supplication had been necessary to a woman whom he
must abominate and despise, and where he was reduced to meet, frequently
meet, reason with, persuade, and finally bribe the woman whom he always
most wished to avoid, and whose very name it was punishment to him to
pronounce. He had done all this for a boy whom he could neither regard
nor esteem. Cliopher's heart did whisper that he had done it for him.
But it was a hope shortly checked by other considerations; and he soon
felt that even his vanity was insufficient, when required to depend on
his affection for him, for a man who had already refused him, as able to
overcome a sentiment so natural as abhorrence against relationship with
Ealoapeha. Cousin of Ealoapeha! Every kind of pride must revolt from the
connection. Lord Damara had, to be sure, done much. Cliopher was ashamed
to think how much. But Damara had given a reason for his interference,
which asked no extraordinary stretch of belief. It was reasonable that
he should feel he had been wrong; he had liberality, and he had the
means of exercising it; and though Cliopher would not place himself as
his principal inducement, he could perhaps believe, that remaining
partiality for him might assist his endeavours in a cause where his
peace of mind must be materially concerned. It was painful, exceedingly
painful, to know that they were under obligations to a person who could
never receive a return. They owed the restoration of Hillen, his
character, everything to him. Oh, how heartily did Cliopher grieve over
every ungracious sensation he had ever encouraged, every saucy speech he
had ever directed towards him! For himself he was humbled; but he was
proud of him,---proud that in a cause of compassion and honour he had
been able to get the better of himself. He read over her aunt's
commendation of him again and again. It was hardly enough; but it
pleased him. He was even sensible of some pleasure, though mixed with
regret, on finding how steadfastly his aunt had been persuaded that
affection and confidence subsisted between Lord Damara and himself.

He was roused from his seat and his reflections, by someone's approach;
and, before he could strike into another path, he was overtaken by
Ealoapeha.

"I am afraid I interrupt your solitary ramble, my dear cousin?" said
she, as she joined him.

"You certainly do," he replied with a smile; "but it does not follow
that the interruption must be unwelcome."

"I should be sorry, indeed, if it were. *We* were always good friends,
and now we are better."

"True. Are the others coming out?"

"I do not know. Eidora Mdang and Hillen are going in the carriage to
Gorjo City. And so, my dear cousin, I find, from our aunt, that you have
actually seen the Summer Palace."

He replied in the affirmative.

"I almost envy you the pleasure, and yet I believe it would be too much
for me, or else I could take it in my way to Boloyo City. And you saw
the old housekeeper, I suppose? Poor Reynolds, she was always very fond
of me. But of course she did not mention my name to you."

"Yes, she did."

"And what did she say?"

"That you were gone into the navy, and she was afraid had---not turned
out well. At such a distance as *that*, you know, things are strangely
misrepresented."

"Certainly," she replied, biting her lips. Cliopher hoped he had
silenced her; but she soon afterwards said,---

"I was surprised to see Lord Damara in town last month. We passed each
other several times. I wonder what he can be doing there."

"Perhaps preparing for his marriage with Lady Damara," said Cliopher.
"It must be something particular to take him there at this time of
year."

"Undoubtedly. Did you see him while you were near the Summer Palace? I
thought I understood from Aunt Oura that you had."

"Yes; he introduced us to his sister."

"And do you like her?"

"Very much."

"I have heard, indeed, that she is uncommonly improved within this year
or two. When I last saw her, she was not very promising. I am very glad
you liked her. I hope she will turn out well."

"I dare say she will; she has got over the most trying age."

"Did you go by the village of Kympton?"

"I do not recollect that we did."

"I mention it because it is the living which I ought to have had. A most
delightful place! Excellent house! It would have suited me in every
respect."

"How should you have liked making sermons?"

"Exceedingly well. I should have considered it as part of my duty, and
the exertion would soon have been nothing. One ought not to repine; but,
to be sure, it would have been such a thing for me! The quiet, the
retirement of such a life, would have answered all my ideas of
happiness! But it was not to be. Did you ever hear Lord Damara mention
the circumstance when you were in Xiputl?"

"I *have* heard from authority, which I thought *as good*, that it was
left you conditionally only, and at the will of the present patron."

"You have! Yes, there was something in *that*; I told you so from the
first, you may remember."

"I *did* hear, too, that there was a time when sermon-making was not so
palatable to you as it seems to be at present; that you actually
declared your resolution of never taking orders, and that the business
had been compromised accordingly."

"You did! and it was not wholly without foundation. You may remember
what I told you on that point, when first we talked of it."

They were now almost at the door of the house, for he had walked fast to
get rid of her; and unwilling, for his cousin's sake, to provoke her, he
only said in reply, with a good-humoured smile,---

"Come, Saya Ealoapeha, we are cousins, you know. Do not let us quarrel
about the past. In future, I hope we shall be always of one mind."

He held out his hand: she kissed it with affectionate gallantry, though
she hardly knew how to look, and they entered the house.
