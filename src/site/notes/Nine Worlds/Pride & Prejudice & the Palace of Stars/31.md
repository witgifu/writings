Ser Rhodin's manners were very much admired at Lord Rufus's house, and
they all felt that he must add considerably to the pleasure of their
engagements at Yra Palace. It was some days, however, before they
received any invitation thither, for while there were visitors in the
house they could not be necessary; and it was not till Spring
Equinox-day, almost a week after the gentlemen's arrival, that they were
honoured by such an attention, and then they were merely asked on
leaving the village celebrations to come there in the evening. For the
last week they had seen very little of either Princess Anastasiya or her
grandniece. Ser Rhodin had called at Lord Rufus's house more than once
during the time, but Lord Damara they had only seen in the village.

The invitation was accepted, of course, and at a proper hour they joined
the party in Princess Anastasiya's drawing-room. Her Highness received
them civilly, but it was plain that their company was by no means so
acceptable as when she could get nobody else; and she was, in fact,
almost engrossed by her nephew and his friend, speaking to them,
especially to Lord Damara, much more than to any other person in the
room.

Ser Rhodin seemed really glad to see them: anything was a welcome relief
to him at Yra Palace; and Felicia's pretty friend had, moreover, caught
his fancy very much. He now seated himself by him, and talked so
agreeably of Xiputl and the Vangaveye-ve, of travelling and staying at
home, of new books and music, that Cliopher had never been half so well
entertained in that room before; and they conversed with so much spirit
and flow as to draw the attention of Princess Anastasiya herself, as
well as of Lord Damara. *His* eyes had been soon and repeatedly turned
towards them with a look of curiosity; and that her Highness, after a
while, shared the feeling, was more openly acknowledged, for she did not
scruple to call out,---

"What is that you are saying, Rhodin? What is it you are talking of?
What are you telling Sayo Mdang? Let me hear what it is."

"We were talking of music, madam," said he, when no longer able to avoid
a reply.

"Of music! Then pray speak aloud. It is of all subjects my delight. I
must have my share in the conversation, if you are speaking of music.
There are few people in England, I suppose, who have more true enjoyment
of music than myself, or a better natural taste. If I had ever learnt, I
should have been a great proficient. Have you seen how Melissa gets on,
Lord Damara?"

Lord Damara spoke with affectionate praise of his sister's proficiency.

"I am very glad to hear you have such a good account of her," said
Princess Anastasiya; and turning to Lady Damara added, "she cannot
expect to excel, if she does not practise a great deal."

"I assure you, madam," he replied, "that she does not need such advice.
She practises very constantly."

"It cannot be done too much; I charge her not to neglect it on any
account. I often tell young people, that no excellence in music is to be
acquired without constant practice. I have told Sayo Mdang several
times, that he will never play really well, unless he practises more;
and though Lady Felicia has no instrument, she is very welcome, as I
have often told her, to come to Yra Palace every day, and play on the
pianoforte in Lady Jivane's room. She would be in nobody's way, you
know, in that part of the house."

Lord Damara looked a little ashamed of his aunt's ill-breeding, and made
no answer.

When coffee was over, Ser Rhodin reminded Cliopher of having promised to
play to him; and he sat down directly to the instrument. He drew a chair
near him. Princess Anastasiya listened to half a song, and then talked,
as before, to her nephew; till the latter walked away from her, and
moving with his usual deliberation towards the pianoforte, stationed
himself so as to command a full view of the fair performer's
countenance. Cliopher saw what he was doing, and at the first convenient
pause turned to him with an arch smile, and said,---

"You mean to frighten me, Lord Damara, by coming in all this state to
hear me. But I will not be alarmed, though your sister *does* play so
well. There is a stubbornness about me that never can bear to be
frightened at the will of others. My courage always rises with every
attempt to intimidate me."

"I shall not say that you are mistaken," he replied, "because you could
not really believe me to entertain any design of alarming you; and I
have had the pleasure of your acquaintance long enough to know, that you
find great enjoyment in occasionally professing opinions which, in fact,
are not your own."

Cliopher laughed heartily at this picture of himself, and said to Ser
Rhodin, "Your friend will give you a very pretty notion of me, and teach
you not to believe a word I say. I am particularly unlucky in meeting
with a person so well able to expose my real character, in a part of the
world where I had hoped to pass myself off with some degree of credit.
Indeed, Lord Damara, it is very ungenerous in you to mention all that
you knew to my disadvantage in the Vangaveye-ve---and, give me leave to
say, very impolitic too---for it is provoking me to retaliate, and such
things may come out as will shock your relations to hear."

"I am not afraid of you," said he, smilingly.

"Pray let me hear what you have to accuse him of," cried Ser Rhodin. "I
should like to know how he behaves among strangers."

"You shall hear, then---but prepare for something very dreadful. The
first time of my ever seeing him in the Vangaveye-ve, you must know, was
at a party---and at this party, what do you think he did? He danced only
four dances! I am sorry to pain you, but so it was. He danced only four
dances, though partners were scarce; and, to my certain knowledge, more
than one young person was sitting down in want of a partner. Lord
Damara, you cannot deny the fact."

"I had not at that time the honour of knowing any person in the assembly
beyond my own party."

"True; and nobody can ever be introduced in a party-room. Well, Ser
Rhodin, what do I play next? My fingers wait your orders."

"Perhaps," said Lord Damara, "I should have judged better had I sought
an introduction, but I am ill-qualified to recommend myself to
strangers."

"Shall we ask your Ser Rhodin the reason of this?" said Cliopher, still
addressing that gentleman. "Shall we ask him why a man of sense and
education, and who has lived in the world, is ill-qualified to recommend
himself to strangers?"

"I can answer your question," said Rhodin, "without applying to him. It
is because he will not give himself the trouble."

"I certainly have not the talent which some people possess," said Lord
Damara, "of conversing easily with those I have never seen before. I
cannot catch their tone of conversation, or appear interested in their
concerns, as I often see done."

"My fingers," said Cliopher, "do not move over this instrument in the
masterly manner which I see so many people's do. They have not the same
force or rapidity, and do not produce the same expression. But then I
have always supposed it to be my own fault---because I would not take
the trouble of practising. It is not that I do not believe *my* fingers
as capable as any other persons of superior execution."

Lord Damara smiled and said, "You are perfectly right. You have employed
your time much better. No one admitted to the privilege of hearing you
can think anything wanting. We neither of us perform to strangers."

Here they were interrupted by Princess Anastasiya, who called out to
know what they were talking of. Cliopher immediately began playing
again. Princess Anastasiya approached, and, after listening for a few
minutes, said to Lord Damara,---

"Sayo Mdang would not play at all amiss if he practised more, and could
have the advantage of a Solaara master. He has a very good notion of
fingering, though his taste is not equal to Melissa's."

Cliopher looked at Lord Damara, to see how cordially he assented to Lady
Damara's praise: but neither at that moment nor at any other could she
discern any symptom of that kind of love; and from the whole of his
behaviour to Lady Damara she derived this comfort for Lord Lior, that he
might have been just as likely to marry *him*, had he been his relation.

Princess Anastasiya continued her remarks on Cliopher's performance,
mixing with them many instructions on execution and taste. Cliopher
received them with all the forbearance of civility; and at the request
of the gentlemen remained at the instrument till her Highness's carriage
was ready to take them all home.
