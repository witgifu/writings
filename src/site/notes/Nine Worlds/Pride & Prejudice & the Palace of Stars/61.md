Happy for all her maternal feelings was the day on which Eidora Mdang
got rid of her two most deserving young family members. With what
delighted pride she afterwards visited Quintus an Vilius, and talked of
both Lords Damara, may be guessed. I wish I could say, for the sake of
her family, that the accomplishment of her earnest desire in the
establishment of so many of her family produced so happy an effect as to
make her a sensible, amiable, well-informed woman for the rest of her
life; though, perhaps, it was lucky for her uncle, who might not have
relished domestic felicity in so unusual a form, that she still was
occasionally nervous and invariably silly.

Buru Tovo missed his second grandnephew exceedingly; his affection drew
him oftener from home than anything else could do. He delighted in going
to the Summer Palace, especially when he was least expected.

The Cavalier and Quintus remained at Navikiani only a twelvemonth. So
near a vicinity to his mother and Gorjo City relations was not desirable
even to *his* easy temper, or *his* affectionate heart. The darling wish
of his cousins was then gratified: he bought an estate in a neighbouring
area to Old Damara; and Quintus and Cliopher, in addition to every other
source of happiness, were within thirty miles of each other.

Haro, to his very material advantage, spent the chief of his time with
his two elder cousins. In society so superior to what he had generally
known, his improvement was great. He was not of so ungovernable a temper
as Hillen; and, removed from the influence of Hillen's example, he
became, by proper attention and management, less irritable, less
ignorant, and less insipid. From the further disadvantage of Hillen's
society he was of course carefully kept; and though Hillen Ealoapeha
frequently invited him to come and stay with him, with the promise of
balls and young officers, his great-uncle would never consent to his
going.

Louya was the only one who remained at home; and she was necessarily
drawn from the pursuit of accomplishments by Eidora Mdang's being quite
unable to sit alone. Louya was obliged to mix more with the world, but
she could still moralize over every morning visit; and as she was no
longer mortified by comparisons between her cousins' beauty and her own,
it was suspected by her great-uncle that she submitted to the change
without much reluctance.

As for Ealoapeha and Hillen, their characters suffered no revolution
from the marriage of his cousins. She bore with philosophy the
conviction that Cliopher must now become acquainted with whatever of her
ingratitude and falsehood had before been unknown to him; and, in spite
of everything, was not wholly without hope that Lord Damara might yet be
prevailed on to make her fortune. The congratulatory letter which
Cliopher received from Hillen on his marriage explained to him that, by
her husband at least, if not by herself, such a hope was cherished. The
letter was to this effect:---

"My dear Kip,

"I wish you joy. If you love Lord Damara half so well as I do my dear
Ealoapeha, you must be very happy. It is a great comfort to have you so
rich; and when you have nothing else to do, I hope you will think of us.
I am sure Ealoapeha would like a place at court very much; and I do not
think we shall have quite money enough to live upon without some help.
Any place would do of about three or four hundred a year; but, however,
do not speak to Lord Damara about it, if you had rather not.

"Yours," etc.

As it happened that Cliopher had much rather not, he endeavoured in his
answer to put an end to every entreaty and expectation of the kind. Such
relief, however, as it was in his power to afford, by the practice of
what might be called economy in his own private expenses, he frequently
sent them. It had always been evident to him that such an income as
theirs, under the direction of two persons so extravagant in their
wants, and heedless of the future, must be very insufficient to their
support; and whenever they changed their quarters, either Quintus or
himself were sure of being applied to for some little assistance towards
discharging their bills. Their manner of living, even when the
restoration of peace dismissed them to a home, was unsettled in the
extreme. They were always moving from place to place in quest of a cheap
situation, and always spending more than they ought. Her affection for
him soon sunk into indifference: his lasted a little longer; and, in
spite of his youth and his manners, he retained all the claims to
reputation which his marriage had given him. Though Lord Damara could
never receive *her* at the Summer Palace, yet, for Cliopher's sake, he
assisted her further in her profession. Hillen was occasionally a
visitor there, when his wife was gone to enjoy herself in Solaara or
Bath; and with the Cavalier's family they both of them frequently stayed
so long, that even an Vilius's good-humour was overcome, and he
proceeded so far as to *talk* of giving them a hint to be gone.

Lord Lior was very deeply mortified by Lord Damara's marriage; but as he
thought it advisable to retain the right of visiting at the Summer
Palace, he dropped all his resentment; was fonder than ever of Melissa,
almost as attentive to Lord Damara as heretofore, and paid off every
arrear of civility to Cliopher.

The Summer Palace was now Melissa's home; and the attachment of Cliopher
and Melissa was exactly what Lord Damara had hoped to see. They were
able to love each other, even as well as they intended. Melissa had the
highest opinion in the world of Cliopher; though at first she often
listened with an astonishment bordering on alarm at his lively, sportive
manner of talking to her brother. He, who had always inspired in herself
a respect which almost overcame her affection, she now saw the object of
open pleasantry. Her mind received knowledge which had never before
fallen in her way. By Cliopher's instructions she began to comprehend
that a person may take liberties with their spouse, which a brother will
not always allow in a sister more than ten years younger than himself.

Princess Anastasiya was extremely indignant on the marriage of her
grandnephew; and as she gave way to all the genuine frankness of her
character, in her reply to the letter which announced its arrangement,
she sent him language so very abusive, especially of Cliopher, that for
some time all intercourse was at an end. But at length, by Cliopher's
persuasion, he was prevailed on to overlook the offence, and seek a
reconciliation; and, after a little further resistance on the part of
his aunt, her resentment gave way, either to her affection for him, or
her curiosity to see how his husband conducted himself; and she
condescended to wait on them at the Summer Palace, in spite of that
pollution which its woods had received, not merely from the presence of
such a master, but the visits of her aunt from the city.

With Aunt Oura they were always on the most intimate terms. Lord Damara,
as well as Cliopher, really loved her; and they were both ever sensible
of the warmest gratitude towards the person who, by bringing him into
Old Damara, had been the means of uniting them.
