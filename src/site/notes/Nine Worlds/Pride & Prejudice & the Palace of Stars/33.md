More than once did Cliopher, in his ramble within the park, unexpectedly
meet Lord Damara. He felt all the perverseness of the mischance that
should bring him where no one else was brought; and, to prevent its ever
happening again, took care to inform him, at first, that it was a
favourite haunt of his. How it could occur a second time, therefore, was
very odd! Yet it did, and even the third. It seemed like wilful
ill-nature, or a voluntary penance; for on these occasions it was not
merely a few formal inquiries and an awkward pause and then away, but he
actually thought it necessary to turn back and walk with him. He never
said a great deal, nor did Cliopher give himself the trouble of talking
or of listening much; but it struck him in the course of their third
encounter that he was asking some odd unconnected questions---about his
pleasure in being at Lord Rufus's home, his love of solitary walks, and
his opinion of Lord Rufus and Lady Felicia's happiness; and that in
speaking of Yra Palace, and his not perfectly understanding the house,
he seemed to expect that whenever he came into Xiputl again he would be
staying *there* too. His words seemed to imply it. Could he have Ser
Rhodin in his thoughts? He supposed, if he meant anything, he must mean
an allusion to what might arise in that quarter. It distressed him a
little, and he was quite glad to find herself at the gate in the pales
opposite Lord Rufus's house.

He was engaged one day, as he walked, in re-perusing Quintus's last
letter, and dwelling on some passages which proved that Quintus had not
written in spirits, when, instead of being again surprised by Lord
Damara, he saw, on looking up, that Ser Rhodin was meeting him. Putting
away the letter immediately, and forcing a smile, he said,---

"I did not know before that you ever walked this way."

"I have been making the tour of the park," he replied, "as I generally
do every year, and intended to close it with a call at Lord Rufus's
house. Are you going much farther?"

"No, I should have turned in a moment."

And accordingly he did turn, and they walked towards Lord Rufus's house
together.

"Do you certainly leave Xiputl on Saturday?" said he.

"Yes---if Lord Damara does not put it off again. But I am at his
disposal. He arranges the business just as he pleases."

"And if not able to please himself in the arrangement, he has at least
great pleasure in the power of choice. I do not know anybody who seems
more to enjoy the power of doing what he likes than Lord Damara."

"He likes to have his own way very well," replied Ser Rhodin. "But so we
all do. It is only that he has better means of having it than many
others, because he is rich, and many others are poor. I speak feelingly.
A younger son, you know, must be inured to self-denial and dependence."

"In my opinion, the younger son of an earl can know very little of
either. Now, seriously, what have you ever known of self-denial and
dependence? When have you been prevented by want of money from going
wherever you chose or procuring anything you had a fancy for?"

"These are home questions---and perhaps I cannot say that I have
experienced many hardships of that nature. But in matters of greater
weight, I may suffer from the want of money. Younger sons cannot marry
where they like."

"Unless where they like those of fortune, which I think they very often
do."

"Our habits of expense make us too dependent, and there are not many in
my rank of life who can afford to marry without some attention to
money."

"Is this," thought Cliopher, "meant for me?" and he coloured at the
idea; but, recovering himself, said in a lively tone, "And pray, what is
the usual price of an earl's younger son? Unless the elder brother is
very sickly, I suppose you would not ask above fifty thousand valiants."

He answered in the same style, and the subject dropped. To interrupt a
silence which might make him fancy Cliopher affected with what had
passed, he soon afterwards said,---

"I imagine your friend brought you down with him chiefly for the sake of
having somebody at his disposal. I wonder he does not marry, to secure a
lasting convenience of that kind. But, perhaps, his sister does as well
for the present; and, he may do what he likes with her."

"No," said Ser Rhodin, "that is an advantage which he must divide with
me. I am joined with him in the guardianship of Lady Damara."

"Are you, indeed? And pray what sort of a guardian do you make? Does
your charge give you much trouble? Young ladies are sometimes a little
difficult to manage; and if she has the true Damara spirit, she may like
to have her own way."

As he spoke, he observed him looking at him earnestly; and the manner in
which he immediately asked him why he supposed Lady Damara likely to
give them any uneasiness, convinced him that he had somehow or other got
pretty near the truth. He directly replied,---

"You need not be frightened. I never heard any harm of her; and I dare
say she is one of the most tractable creatures in the world. She is a
very great favourite with some of my acquaintance, Prince Belu and Lord
Lior."

"I know them a little. Their cousin is a pleasant, gentlemanlike
man---he is a great friend of Lord Damara's."

"Oh yes," said Cliopher drily---"Lord Damara is uncommonly kind to the
Cavalier, and takes a prodigious deal of care of him."

"Care of him! Yes, I really believe Lord Damara *does* take care of him
in those points where he most wants care. From something that he told me
in our journey hither, I have reason to think an Vilius very much
indebted to him. But I ought to beg his pardon, for I have no right to
suppose that an Vilius was the person meant. It was all conjecture."

"What is it you mean?"

"It is a circumstance which Lord Damara of course could not wish to be
generally known, because if it were to get round to the person's family
it would be an unpleasant thing."

"You may depend upon my not mentioning it."

"And remember that I have not much reason for supposing it to be an
Vilius. What he told me was merely this: that he congratulated himself
on having lately saved a friend from the inconveniences of a most
imprudent marriage, but without mentioning names or any other
particulars; and I only suspected it to be an Vilius from believing him
the kind of man to get into a scrape of that sort, and from knowing them
to have been together the whole of last summer."

"Did Lord Damara give you his reasons for this interference?"

"I understood that there were some very strong objections against the
partner."

"And what arts did he use to separate them?"

"He did not talk to me of his own arts," said Rhodin, smiling. "He only
told me what I have now told you."

Cliopher made no answer, and walked on, his heart swelling with
indignation. After watching him a little, Rhodin asked her why he was so
thoughtful.

"I am thinking of what you have been telling me," said he. "Your
friend's conduct does not suit my feelings. Why was he to be the judge?"

"You are rather disposed to call his interference officious?"

"I do not see what right Lord Damara had to decide on the propriety of
his friend's inclination; or why, upon his own judgment alone, he was to
determine and direct in what manner that friend was to be happy. But,"
she continued, recollecting himself, "as we know none of the
particulars, it is not fair to condemn him. It is not to be supposed
that there was much affection in the case."

"That is not an unnatural surmise," said Rhodin; "but it is lessening
the honour of my friend's triumph very sadly."

This was spoken jestingly, but it appeared to him so just a picture of
Lord Damara, that he would not trust himself with an answer; and,
therefore, abruptly changing the conversation, talked on indifferent
matters till they reached Lord Rufus's house. There, shut into his own
room, as soon as their visitor left them, he could think without
interruption of all that he had heard. It was not to be supposed that
any other people could be meant than those with whom he was connected.
There could not exist in the world *two* men over whom Lord Damara could
have such boundless influence. That he had been concerned in the
measures taken to separate the Cavalier and Quintus, he had never
doubted; but he had always attributed to Lord Lior the principal design
and arrangement of them. If his own vanity, however, did not mislead
him, *he* was the cause---his pride and caprice were the cause---of all
that Quintus had suffered, and still continued to suffer. He had ruined
for a while every hope of happiness for the most affectionate, generous
heart in the world; and no one could say how lasting an evil he might
have inflicted.

"There were some very strong objections against the partner," were Ser
Rhodin's words; and these strong objections probably were, him having
one uncle who was a barber, and an aunt who was in business in Solaara.

"To Quintus himself," he exclaimed, "there could be no possibility of
objection,---all loveliness and goodness as he is! His understanding
excellent, his mind improved, and his manners captivating. Neither could
anything be urged against Buru Tovo, who, though with some
peculiarities, has abilities which Lord Damara himself need not disdain,
and respectability which he will probably never reach." When he thought
of his mother, indeed, his confidence gave way a little; but he would
not allow that any objections *there* had material weight with Lord
Damara, whose pride, he was convinced, would receive a deeper wound from
the want of importance in his friend's connections than from their want
of sense; and he was quite decided, at last, that he had been partly
governed by this worst kind of pride, and partly by the wish of
retaining the Cavalier for his sister.

The agitation and tears which the subject occasioned brought on a
headache; and it grew so much worse towards the evening that, added to
his unwillingness to see Lord Damara, it determined him not to attend to
Yra Palace, where they were engaged to drink tea. Felicia, seeing that
he was really unwell, did not press him to go, and as much as possible
prevented her husband from pressing him; but Lord Rufus could not
conceal his apprehension of Princess Anastasiya's being rather
displeased by him staying at home.
