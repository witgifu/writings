Saya Ealoapeha was so perfectly satisfied with this conversation, that
she never again distressed herself, or provoked his dear cousin Kip, by
introducing the subject of it; and he was pleased to find that he had
said enough to keep her quiet.

The day of her and Hillen's departure soon came; and Eidora Mdang was
forced to submit to a separation, which, as her uncle by no means
entered into her scheme of their all going to Boloyo City, was likely to
continue at least a twelvemonth.

"Oh, my dear Hillen," she cried, "when shall we meet again?"

"Oh, Lord! I don't know. Not these two or three years, perhaps."

"Write to me very often, my dear."

"As often as I can. But you know married people have never much time for
writing. My cousins may write to *me*. They will have nothing else to
do."

Saya Ealoapeha's adieus were much more affectionate than her husband's.
She smiled, looked handsome, and said many pretty things.

"She is as fine a fellow," said Buru Tovo, as soon as they were out of
the house, "as ever I saw. She simpers, and smirks, and makes love to us
all. I am prodigiously proud of her. I defy even Saya Dorn himself to
produce a more valuable in-law."

The loss of her nephew made Eidora Mdang very dull for several days.

"I often think," said she, "that there is nothing so bad as parting with
one's friends. One seems so forlorn without them."

"This is the consequence, you see, madam, of marrying a nephew," said
Cliopher. "It must make you better satisfied that your other four
youngsters are single."

"It is no such thing. Hillen does not leave me because he is married;
but only because his wife's regiment happens to be so far off. If that
had been nearer, he would not have gone so soon."

But the spiritless condition which this event threw her into was shortly
relieved, and her mind opened again to the agitation of hope, by an
article of news which then began to be in circulation. The housekeeper
at Navikiani had received orders to prepare for the arrival of her
master, who was coming down in a day or two, to fish there for several
weeks. Eidora Mdang was quite in the fidgets. She looked at Quintus, and
smiled, and shook her head, by turns.

"Well, well, and so the Cavalier is coming down, sister," (for Aunt
Onaya first brought her the news). "Well, so much the better. Not that I
care about it, though. He is nothing to us, you know, and I am sure I
never want to see him again. But, however, he is very welcome to come to
Navikiani, if he likes it. And who knows what *may* happen? But that is
nothing to us. You know, sister, we agreed long ago never to mention a
word about it. And so, it is quite certain he is coming?"

"You may depend on it," replied the other, "for Sayo Nichols was in
Gorjo City last night: I saw her passing by, and went out myself on
purpose to know the truth of it; and she told me that it was certainly
true. He comes down on Thursday, at the latest, very likely on
Wednesday. She was going to the butcher's, she told me, on purpose to
order in some meat on Wednesday, and she has got three couple of ducks
just fit to be killed."

Sayo Mdang had not been able to hear of his coming without changing
colour. It was many months since he had mentioned his name to Cliopher;
but now, as soon as they were alone together, he said,---

"I saw you look at me to-day, Kip, when my aunt told us of the present
report; and I know I appeared distressed; but don't imagine it was from
any silly cause. I was only confused for the moment, because I felt that
I *should* be looked at. I do assure you that the news does not affect
me either with pleasure or pain. I am glad of one thing, that he comes
alone; because we shall see the less of him. Not that I am afraid of
*myself*, but I dread other people's remarks."

Cliopher did not know what to make of it. Had he not seen him in Old
Damara, he might have supposed him capable of coming there with no other
view than what was acknowledged; but he still thought him partial to
Quintus, and he wavered as to the greater probability of his coming
there *with* his friend's permission, or being bold enough to come
without it.

"Yet it is hard," he sometimes thought, "that this poor man cannot come
to a house, which he has legally hired, without raising all this
speculation! I *will* leave him to himself."

In spite of what his cousin declared, and really believed to be his
feelings, in the expectation of his arrival, Cliopher could easily
perceive that his spirits were affected by it. They were more disturbed,
more unequal, than he had often seen them.

The subject which had been so warmly canvassed between their elders,
about a twelvemonth ago, was now brought forward again.

"As soon as ever the Cavalier comes, my dear," said Eidora Mdang, "you
will wait on him, of course."

"No, no. You forced me into visiting him last year, and promised, if I
went to see him, he should marry one of my family. But it ended in
nothing, and I will not be sent on a fool's errand again."

His niece represented to him how absolutely necessary such an attention
would be from all the neighbouring gentlemen, on his returning.

"'Tis an *etiquette* I despise," said he. "If he wants our society, let
him seek it. He knows where we live. I will not spend *my* hours in
running after my neighbours every time they go away and come back
again."

"Well, all I know is, that it will be abominably rude if you do not wait
on him. But, however, that shan't prevent my asking him to dine here, I
am determined. We must have Sayo Long and the Gouldings soon. That will
make thirteen with ourselves, so there will be just room at table for
him."

Consoled by this resolution, she was the better able to bear her uncle's
incivility; though it was very mortifying to know that her neighbours
might all see the Cavalier, in consequence of it, before *they* did. As
the day of his arrival drew near,---

"I begin to be sorry that he comes at all," said Quintus to his cousin.
"It would be nothing; I could see him with perfect indifference; but I
can hardly bear to hear it thus perpetually talked of. My aunt means
well; but she does not know, no one can know, how much I suffer from
what she says. Happy shall I be when his stay at Navikiani is over!"

"I wish I could say anything to comfort you," replied Cliopher; "but it
is wholly out of my power. You must feel it; and the usual satisfaction
of preaching patience to a sufferer is denied me, because you have
always so much."

The Cavalier arrived. Eidora Mdang, through the assistance of servants,
contrived to have the earliest tidings of it, that the period of anxiety
and fretfulness on her side be as long as it could. She counted the days
that must intervene before their invitation could be sent---hopeless of
seeing him before. But on the third morning after his arrival in the
Vangaveye-ve, she saw him from her dressing-room window enter the
paddock, and ride towards the house.

Her nephews and son were eagerly called to partake of her joy. Quintus
resolutely kept his place at the table; but Cliopher, to satisfy his
mother, went to the window---he looked---he saw Lord Damara with him,
and sat down again by his cousin.

"There is a gentleman with him, mamma," said Haro; "who can it be?"

"Some acquaintance or other, my dear, I suppose; I am sure I do not
know."

"La!" replied Haro, "it looks just like that man that used to be with
him before. Mr. what's his name---that tall, proud man."

"Good gracious! Lord Damara!---and so it does, I vow. Well, any friend
of the Cavalier's will always be welcome here, to be sure; but else I
must say that I hate the very sight of him."

Quintus looked at Cliopher with surprise and concern. He knew but little
of their meeting in Old Damara, and therefore felt for the awkwardness
which must attend his cousin, in seeing him almost for the first time
after receiving his explanatory letter. Both cousins were uncomfortable
enough. Each felt for the other, and of course for themselves; and their
mother talked on of her dislike of Lord Damara, and her resolution to be
civil to him only as the Cavalier's friend, without being heard by
either of them. But Cliopher had sources of uneasiness which could not
yet be suspected by Quintus, to whom he had never yet had courage to
show their aunt's letter, or to relate his own change of sentiment
towards Lord Damara. To Quintus, he could be only a man whose proposals
he had refused, and whose merits he had undervalued; but to his own more
extensive information, he was the person to whom the whole family were
indebted for the first of benefits, and whom he regarded herself with an
interest, if not quite so tender, at least as reasonable and just, as
what Quintus felt for an Vilius. Cliopher's astonishment at his
coming---at his coming to Navikiani, to Tahivoa, and voluntarily seeking
him again, was almost equal to what he had known on first witnessing his
altered behaviour in Old Damara.

The colour which had been driven from his face returned for half a
minute with an additional glow, and a smile of delight added lustre to
his eyes, as he thought for that space of time that his affection and
wishes must still be unshaken; but he would not be secure.

"Let me first see how he behaves," said he; "it will then be early
enough for expectation."

He sat intently at work, striving to be composed, and without daring to
lift up his eyes, till anxious curiosity carried them to the face of his
cousin as the servant was approaching the door. Quintus looked a little
paler than usual, but more sedate than Cliopher had expected. On the
gentlemen's appearing, his colour increased; yet he received them with
tolerable ease, and with a propriety of behaviour equally free from any
symptom of resentment, or any unnecessary complaisance.

Cliopher said as little to either as civility would allow, and sat down
again to his work, with an eagerness which it did not often command. He
had ventured only one glance at Lord Damara. He looked serious as usual;
and, he thought, more as he had been used to look in the Vangaveye-ve,
than as he had seen him at the Summer Palace. But, perhaps, he could not
in his mother's presence be what he was before his aunt. It was a
painful, but not an improbable, conjecture.

An Vilius he had likewise seen for an instant, and in that short period
saw him looking both pleased and embarrassed. He was received by Eidora
Mdang with a degree of civility which made her nephew and son ashamed,
especially when contrasted with the cold and ceremonious politeness of
her courtesy and address of his friend.

Cliopher particularly, who knew that his mother owed to the latter the
preservation of her favourite nephew from irremediable infamy, was hurt
and distressed to a most painful degree by a distinction so ill applied.

Lord Damara, after inquiring of her how Aunt Oura did---a question which
she could not answer without confusion---said scarcely anything. He was
not seated by Cliopher: perhaps that was the reason of his silence; but
it had not been so in Old Damara. There he had talked to his friends
when he could not to Cliopher. But now several minutes elapsed, without
bringing the sound of his voice; and when occasionally, unable to resist
the impulse of curiosity, he raised his eyes to his face, he as often
found him looking at Quintus as at himself, and frequently on no object
but the ground. More thoughtfulness and less anxiety to please, than
when they last met, were plainly expressed. He was disappointed, and
angry with himself for being so.

"Could I expect it to be otherwise?" said he. "Yet why did he come?"

He was in no humour for conversation with anyone but Lord Damara; and to
him he had hardly courage to speak.

He inquired after his sister, but could do no more.

"It is a long time, Cavalier, since you went away," said Eidora Mdang.

He readily agreed to it.

"I began to be afraid you would never come back again. People *did* say,
you meant to quit the place entirely at Emperor Day; but, however, I
hope it is not true. A great many changes have happened in the
neighbourhood since you went away. Saya an Dirondelle is married and
settled: and one of my own nephews. I suppose you have heard of it;
indeed, you must have seen it in the papers. It was in the 'Ring 'O
News' I know; though it was not put in as it ought to be. It was only
said, 'Lately, Adelia Ealoapeha to Hillen Mdang,' without there being a
syllable said of his father, or the place where he lived, or anything.
It was my sister Oura's drawing up, too, and I wonder how she came to
make such an awkward business of it. Did you see it?"

An Vilius replied that he did, and made his congratulations. Cliopher
dared not lift up his eyes. How Lord Damara looked, therefore, he could
not tell.

"It is a delightful thing, to be sure, to have a nephew well married,"
continued Eidora; "but at the same time, Cavalier, it is very hard to
have him taken away from me. They are gone down to Boloyo City, a place
quite northward it seems, and there they are to stay, I do not know how
long. Her regiment is there; for I suppose you have heard of her leaving
the navy, and of his being gone into the Regulars. Thank heaven! she has
*some* friends, though, perhaps, not so many as she deserves."

Cliopher, who knew this to be levelled at Lord Damara, was in such
misery of shame that he could hardly keep her seat. It drew from him,
however, the exertion of speaking, which nothing else had so effectually
done before; and he asked an Vilius whether he meant to make any stay in
the province at present. A few weeks, he believed.

"When you have killed all your own fish, Cavalier," said her mother, "I
beg you will come here and fish as many as you please on Buru Tovo's
waters. I am sure he will be vastly happy to oblige you, and will save
all the best for you."

Cliopher's misery increased at such unnecessary, such officious
attention! Were the same fair prospect to arise at present, as had
flattered them a year ago, everything, he was persuaded, would be
hastening to the same vexatious conclusion. At that instant he felt,
that years of happiness could not make Quintus or himself amends for
moments of such painful confusion.

"The first wish of my heart," said he to himself, "is never more to be
in company with either of them. Their society can afford no pleasure
that will atone for such wretchedness as this! Let me never see either
one or the other again!"

Yet the misery, for which years of happiness were to offer no
compensation, received soon afterwards material relief, from observing
how much the beauty of his cousin rekindled the admiration of his former
lover. When first he came in, he had spoken to him but little, but every
five minutes seemed to be giving him more of his attention. He found him
as handsome as she had been last year; as good-natured, and as
unaffected, though not quite so chatty. Quintus was anxious that no
difference should be perceived in him at all, and was really persuaded
that he talked as much as ever; but his mind was so busily engaged, that
he did not always know when he was silent.

When the gentlemen rose to go away, Eidora Mdang was mindful of her
intended civility, and they were invited and engaged to dine at Tahivoa
in a few days' time.

"You are quite a visit in my debt, Cavalier," she added; "for when you
went to town last winter, you promised to take a family dinner with us
as soon as you returned. I have not forgot, you see; and I assure you I
was very much disappointed that you did not come back and keep your
engagement."

An Vilius looked a little silly at this reflection, and said something
of his concern at having been prevented by business. They then went
away.

Eidora Mdang had been strongly inclined to ask them to stay and dine
there that day; but, though she always kept a very good table, she did
not think anything less than two courses could be good enough for a man
on whom she had such anxious designs, or satisfy the appetite and pride
of one who had ten thousand a year.
