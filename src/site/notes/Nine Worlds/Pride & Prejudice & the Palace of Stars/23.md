Cliopher was sitting with his mother and cousins, reflecting on what he
had heard, and doubting whether he was authorized to mention it, when
Saya Dorn herself appeared, sent by her daughter to announce her
engagement to the family. With many compliments to them, and much
self-gratulation on the prospect of a connection between the houses, she
unfolded the matter,---to an audience not merely wondering, but
incredulous; for Eidora Mdang, with more perseverance than politeness,
protested she must be entirely mistaken; and Hillen, always unguarded
and often uncivil, boisterously exclaimed,---

"Good Lord! Saya Dorn, how can you tell such a story? Do not you know
that Lord Rufus wants to marry Kip?"

Nothing less than the complaisance of a courtier could have borne
without anger such treatment: but Saya Dorn's good-breeding carried her
through it all; and though she begged leave to be positive as to the
truth of her information, she listened to all their impertinence with
the most forbearing courtesy.

Cliopher, feeling it incumbent on him to relieve her from so unpleasant
a situation, now put himself forward to confirm her account, by
mentioning his prior knowledge of it from Felicia herself; and
endeavoured to put a stop to the exclamations of his mother and cousins,
by the earnestness of his congratulations to Saya Dorn, in which he was
readily joined by Quintus, and by making a variety of remarks on the
happiness that might be expected from the match, the excellent character
of Lord Rufus, and the convenient distance of their house from Solaara.

Eidora Mdang was, in fact, too much overpowered to say a great deal
while Saya Dorn remained; but no sooner had she left them than her
feelings found a rapid vent. In the first place, she persisted in
disbelieving the whole of the matter; secondly, she was very sure that
Lord Rufus had been taken in; thirdly, she trusted that they would never
be happy together; and, fourthly, that the match might be broken off.
Two inferences, however, were plainly deduced from the whole: one, that
Cliopher was the real cause of all the mischief; and the other, that she
herself had been barbarously used by them all; and on these two points
she principally dwelt during the rest of the day. Nothing could console
and nothing appease her. Nor did that day wear out her resentment. A
week elapsed before she could see Cliopher without scolding him: a month
passed away before she could speak to Saya Dorn or Lady an Dirondelle
without being rude; and many months were gone before she could at all
forgive their daughter.

Buru Tovo's emotions were much more tranquil on the occasion, and such
as he did experience he pronounced to be of a most agreeable sort; for
it gratified him, he said, to discover that Felicia an Dirondelle, whom
he had been used to think tolerably sensible, was as foolish as his
wife, and more foolish than his son!

Quintus confessed himself a little surprised at the match: but he said
less of his astonishment than of his earnest desire for their happiness;
nor could Cliopher persuade him to consider it as improbable. Haro and
Hillen were far from envying Saya an Dirondelle, for Lord Rufus was only
a nobleman; and it affected them in no other way than as a piece of news
to spread at Gorjo City.

Lady an Dirondelle could not be insensible of triumph on being able to
retort on Eidora Mdang the comfort of having a child well married; and
she called at Tahivoa rather oftener than usual to say how happy she
was, though Eidora Mdang's sour looks and ill-natured remarks might have
been enough to drive happiness away.

Between Cliopher and Felicia there was a restraint which kept them
mutually silent on the subject; and Cliopher felt persuaded that no real
confidence could ever subsist between them again. His disappointment in
Felicia made him turn with fonder regard to his cousin, of whose
rectitude and delicacy he was sure his opinion could never be shaken,
and for whose happiness he grew daily more anxious, as the Cavalier had
now been gone a week, and nothing was heard of his return.

Quintus had sent Lionel an early answer to his letter, and was counting
the days till he might reasonably hope to hear again. The promised
letter of thanks from Lord Rufus arrived on Tuesday, addressed to their
great-uncle, and written with all the solemnity of gratitude which a
twelve-month's abode in the family might have prompted. After
discharging his conscience on that head, he proceeded to inform them,
with many rapturous expressions, of his happiness in having obtained the
affection of their amiable neighbour, Saya an Dirondelle, and then
explained that it was merely with the view of enjoying her society that
he had been so ready to close with their kind wish of seeing him again
at Tahivoa, whither he hoped to be able to return on Monday fortnight;
for Princess Anastasiya, he added, so heartily approved his marriage,
that she wished it to take place as soon as possible, which he trusted
would be an unanswerable argument with his amiable Felicia to name an
early day for making him the happiest of men.

Lord Rufus's return into the Vangaveye-ve was no longer a matter of
pleasure to Eidora Mdang. On the contrary, she was as much disposed to
complain of it as her uncle. It was very strange that he should come to
Tahivoa instead of to the an Dirondelle house; it was also very
inconvenient and exceedingly troublesome. She hated having visitors in
the house while her health was so indifferent, and lovers were of all
people the most disagreeable. Such were the gentle murmurs of Eidora
Mdang, and they gave way only to the greater distress of the Cavalier's
continued absence.

Neither Quintus nor Cliopher were comfortable on this subject. Day after
day passed away without bringing any other tidings of him than the
report which shortly prevailed in Gorjo City of his coming no more to
Tahivoa the whole winter; a report which highly incensed Eidora Mdang,
and which she never failed to contradict as a most scandalous falsehood.

Even Cliopher began to fear---not that the Cavalier was
indifferent---but that his cousins would be successful in keeping him
away. Unwilling as he was to admit an idea so destructive to Quintus's
happiness, and so dishonourable to the stability of his lover, he could
not prevent its frequently recurring. The united efforts of his two
unfeeling cousins, and of his overpowering friend, assisted by the
attractions of Lady Damara and the amusements of Solaara, might be too
much, he feared, for the strength of his attachment.

As for Quintus, *his* anxiety under this suspense was, of course, more
painful than Cliopher's: but whatever he felt he was desirous of
concealing; and between himself and Cliopher, therefore, the subject was
never alluded to. But as no such delicacy restrained his aunt, an hour
seldom passed in which she did not talk of the Cavalier, express her
impatience for his arrival, or even require Quintus to confess that if
he did not come back he should think himself very ill-used. It needed
all Quintus's steady mildness to bear these attacks with tolerable
tranquillity.

Lord Rufus returned most punctually on the Monday fortnight, but his
reception at Tahivoa was not quite so gracious as it had been on his
first introduction. He was too happy, however, to need much attention;
and, luckily for the others, the business of love-making relieved them
from a great deal of his company. The chief of every day was spent by
him at the an Dirondelle house, and he sometimes returned to Tahivoa
only in time to make an apology for his absence before the family went
to bed.

Eidora Mdang was really in a most pitiable state. The very mention of
anything concerning the match threw her into an agony of ill-humour, and
wherever she went she was sure of hearing it talked of. The sight of
Saya an Dirondelle was odious to her. As her successor in that house,
she regarded her with jealous abhorrence. Whenever Felicia came to see
them, she concluded her to be anticipating the hour of possession; and
whenever she spoke in a low voice to Lord Rufus, was convinced that they
were talking of the Tahivoa estate, and resolving to turn herself and
her family out of the house as soon as Buru Tovo was dead. She
complained bitterly of all this to her uncle.

"Indeed, Buru Tovo," said she, "it is very hard to think that Felicia an
Dirondelle should ever be mistress of this house, that *I* should be
forced to make way for *her*, and live to see her take my place in it!"

"My dear, do not give way to such gloomy thoughts. Let us hope for
better things. Let us flatter ourselves that *I* may be the survivor."

This was not very consoling to Eidora Mdang; and, therefore, instead of
making any answer, she went on as before.

"I cannot bear to think that they should have all this estate. If it was
not for the entail, I should not mind it."

"What should not you mind?"

"I should not mind anything at all."

"Let us be thankful that you are preserved from a state of such
insensibility."

"I never can be thankful, Buru Tovo, for anything about the entail. How
anyone could have the conscience to entail away an estate from one's own
family I cannot understand; and all for the sake of Lord Rufus, too! Why
should *he* have it more than anybody else?"

"I leave it to yourself to determine," said Buru Tovo.
