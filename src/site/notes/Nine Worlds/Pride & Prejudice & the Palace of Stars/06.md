The Mdangs of Tahivoa soon waited on the family at Navikiani. The visit
was returned in due form. Sayo Mdang's pleasing manners grew on the
good-will of Prince Belu and Lord Lior; and though the matriarch was
found to be intolerable, and the younger cousins not worth speaking to,
a wish of being better acquainted with *them* was expressed towards the
two eldest. By Quintus this attention was received with the greatest
pleasure; but Cliopher still saw superciliousness in their treatment of
everybody, hardly excepting even his cousin, and could not like them;
though their kindness to Quintus, such as it was, had a value, as
arising, in all probability, from the influence of their cousin's
admiration. It was generally evident, whenever they met, that he *did*
admire him; and to *him* it was equally evident that Quintus was
yielding to the preference which he had begun to entertain for him from
the first, and was in a way to be very much in love; but he considered
with pleasure that it was not likely to be discovered by the world in
general, since Quintus united with great strength of feeling, a
composure of temper and a uniform cheerfulness of manner, which would
guard him from the suspicions of the impertinent. He mentioned this to
his friend, Saya an Dirondelle.

"It may, perhaps, be pleasant," replied Felicia, "to be able to impose
on the public in such a case; but it is sometimes a disadvantage to be
so very guarded. If a person conceals their affection with the same
skill from the object of it, they may lose the opportunity of fixing
them; and it will then be but poor consolation to believe the world
equally in the dark. There is so much of gratitude or vanity in almost
every attachment, that it is not safe to leave any to itself. We can all
*begin* freely---a slight preference is natural enough; but there are
very few of us who have heart enough to be really in love without
encouragement. In nine cases out of ten, a person had better show *more*
affection than they feel. The Cavalier likes your cousin undoubtedly;
but he may never do more than like him, if he does not help him on."

"But he does help him on, as much as his nature will allow. If *I* can
perceive his regard for him, he must be a simpleton indeed not to
discover it too."

"Remember, Kip, that he does not know Quintus's disposition as you do."

"But if a person is partial to another, and does not endeavour to
conceal it, they must find it out."

"Perhaps they must, if they see enough of each other. But though the
Cavalier and Quintus meet tolerably often, it is never for many hours
together; and as they always see each other in large mixed parties, it
is impossible that every moment should be employed in conversing
together. Quintus should therefore make the most of every half hour in
which he can command his attention. When he is secure of him, there will
be leisure for falling in love as much as he chooses."

"Your plan is a good one," replied Cliopher, "where nothing is in
question but the desire of being well married; and if I were determined
to get a rich spouse, or any spouse, I dare say I should adopt it. But
these are not Quintus's feelings; he is not acting by design. As yet he
cannot even be certain of the degree of his own regard, nor of its
reasonableness. He has known him only a fortnight. He danced four dances
with him at Gorjo City; he saw him one morning at his own house, and has
since dined in company with him four times. This is not quite enough to
make him understand his character."

"Not as you represent it. Had he merely *dined* with him, he might only
have discovered whether he had a good appetite; but you must remember
that four evenings have been also spent together---and four evenings may
do a great deal."

"Yes: these four evenings have enabled them to ascertain that they both
like Poacher better than Commerce, but with respect to any other leading
characteristic, I do not imagine that much has been unfolded."

"Well," said Felicia, "I wish Quintus success with all my heart; and if
he were married to him to-morrow, I should think he had as good a chance
of happiness as if he were to be studying his character for a
twelvemonth. Happiness in marriage is entirely a matter of chance. If
the dispositions of the parties are ever so well known to each other, or
ever so similar beforehand, it does not advance their felicity in the
least. They always continue to grow sufficiently unlike afterwards to
have their share of vexation; and it is better to know as little as
possible of the defects of the person with whom you are to pass your
life."

"You make me laugh, Felicity; but it is not sound. You know it is not
sound, and that you would never act in this way yourself."

Occupied in observing the Cavalier's attention to her sister, Kip was
far from suspecting that he was himself becoming an object of some
interest in the eyes of his friend. Lord Damara had at first scarcely
allowed him to be pretty: he had looked at him without admiration at the
ball; and when they next met, he looked at him only to criticise. But no
sooner had he made it clear to himself and his friends that Cliopher had
hardly a good feature in his face, than he began to find it was rendered
uncommonly intelligent by the beautiful expression of his dark eyes. To
this discovery succeeded some others equally mortifying. Though he had
detected with a critical eye more than one failure of perfect symmetry
in his form, he was forced to acknowledge his figure to be strong and
pleasing; and in spite of his asserting that his manners were not those
of the fashionable world, he was caught by their easy playfulness. Of
this he was perfectly unaware: to Cliopher he was only the man who made
himself agreeable nowhere, and who had not thought him handsome enough
to dance with.

He began to wish to know more of Cliopher; and, as a step towards
conversing with Cliopher himself, attended to his conversation with
others. His doing so drew Cliopher's notice. It was at Saya Dorn's,
where a large party were assembled.

"What does Lord Damara mean," said Cliopher to Felicia, "by listening to
my conversation with Captain Forster?"

"That is a question which Lord Damara only can answer."

"But if he does it any more, I shall certainly let him know that I see
what he is about. He has a very satirical eye, and if I do not begin by
being impertinent myself, I shall soon grow afraid of him."

On his approaching them soon afterwards, though without seeming to have
any intention of speaking, Saya an Dirondelle defied her friend to
mention such a subject to him, which immediately provoking Cliopher to
do it, he turned to him and said,---

"Did not you think, Lord Damara, that I expressed myself uncommonly well
just now, when I was teasing Captain Forster to give us a ball in Gorjo
City?"

"With great energy; but it is a subject which always makes a young
person energetic."

"You are severe on us."

"It will be *his* turn soon to be teased," said Saya an Dirondelle. "I
am going to open the instrument, Kip, and you know what follows."

"You are a very strange creature by way of a friend!---always wanting me
to play and sing before anybody and everybody! If my vanity had taken a
musical turn, you would have been invaluable; but as it is, I would
really rather not sit down before those who must be in the habit of
hearing the very best performers." On Saya an Dirondelle's persevering,
however, he added, "Very well; if it must be so, it must." And gravely
glancing at Lord Damara, "There is a very fine old saying, which
everybody here is of course familiar with---'Keep your breath to cool
your porridge,'---and I shall keep mine to swell my song."

His performance was pleasing, though by no means capital. After a song
or two, and before he could reply to the entreaties of several that he
would sing again, he was eagerly succeeded at the instrument by his
cousin Louya, who having, in consequence of being the only plain one in
the family, worked hard for knowledge and accomplishments, was always
impatient for display.

Louya had neither genius nor taste; and though vanity had given her
application, it had given her likewise a pedantic air and conceited
manner, which would have injured a higher degree of excellence than she
had reached. Cliopher, easy and unaffected, had been listened to with
much more pleasure, though not singing half so well; and Louya, at the
end of a long concerto, was glad to purchase praise and gratitude by
Alinoral and Ystharian airs, at the request of her cousins, who with
some of the an Dirondelles, and two or three officers, joined eagerly in
dancing at one end of the room.

Lord Damara stood near them in silent indignation at such a mode of
passing the evening, to the exclusion of all conversation, and was too
much engrossed by his own thoughts to perceive that Sayo Dorn was his
neighbour, till Sayo Dorn:---

"What a charming amusement for young people this is, Lord Damara! There
is nothing like dancing, after all. I consider it as one of the first
refinements of polished societies."

"Certainly, Saya; and it has the advantage also of being in vogue
amongst the less polished societies of the world: every savage can
dance."

Saya Dorn only smiled. "Your friend performs delightfully," he
continued, after a pause, on seeing the Cavalier join the group; "and I
doubt not that you are an adept in the science yourself, Lord Damara."

"You saw me dance in Gorjo City, I believe, Saya."

"Yes, indeed, and received no inconsiderable pleasure from the sight. Do
you often dance at the Palace of Stars?"

"Never, Saya."

"Do you not think it would be a proper compliment to the place?"

"It is a compliment which I never pay to any place if I can avoid it."

"You have a house in Solaara, I conclude?"

Lord Damara bowed.

"I had once some thoughts of fixing in town myself, for I am fond of
superior society; but I did not feel quite certain that the air of
Solaara would agree with Lady an Dirondelle."

She paused in hopes of an answer: but her companion was not disposed to
make any; and Cliopher at that instant moving towards them, she was
struck with the notion of doing a very gallant thing, and called out to
him,---

"My dear Sayo Kip, why are not you dancing? Lord Damara, you must allow
me to present this young man to you as a very desirable partner. You
cannot refuse to dance, I am sure, when so much beauty is before you."
And, taking his hand, she would have given it to Lord Damara, who,
extremely surprised, was unwilling to receive it. Both instantly drew
back, and Cliopher said with some discomposure to Saya Dorn,---

"Indeed, Saya, I have not the least intention of dancing. I entreat you
not to suppose that I moved this way in order to beg for a partner."

Lord Damara, with grave propriety, requested to be allowed the honour of
the dance, but in vain. Cliopher was determined; nor did Saya Dorn at
all shake his purpose by her attempt at persuasion.

"You excel so much in the dance, Sayo Kip, that it is cruel to deny me
the happiness of seeing you; and though this gentleman dislikes the
amusement in general, he can have no objection, I am sure, to oblige us
for one half hour."

"Lord Damara is all politeness," said Cliopher, smiling.

"He is, indeed: but considering the inducement, my dear Sayo Kip, we
cannot wonder at his complaisance; for who would object to such a
partner?"

Cliopher looked archly, and turned away. His resistance had not injured
him with the gentleman, and he was thinking of him with some
complacency, when thus accosted by Lord Lior,---

"I can guess the subject of your reverie."

"I should imagine not."

"You are considering how insupportable it would be to pass many evenings
in this manner,---in such society; and, indeed, I am quite of your
opinion. I was never more annoyed! The insipidity, and yet the
noise---the nothingness, and yet the self-importance, of all these
people! What would I give to hear your strictures on them!"

"Your conjecture is totally wrong, I assure you. My mind was more
agreeably engaged. I have been meditating on the very great pleasure
which a pair of fine eyes in the face of a pretty man can bestow."

Lord Lior immediately fixed his eyes on Lord Damara's face, and desired
he would tell him what person had the credit of inspiring such
reflections. Lord Damara replied, with great intrepidity,---

"Cliopher sayo Mdang."

"Cliopher sayo Mdang!" repeated Lord Lior. "I am all astonishment. How
long has he been such a favourite? and pray when am I to wish you joy?"

"That is exactly the question which I expected you to ask. Your
imagination is very rapid; it jumps from admiration to love, from love
to matrimony, in a moment. I knew you would be wishing me joy."

"Nay, if you are so serious about it, I shall consider the matter as
absolutely settled. You will have a charming mother-in-law, indeed, and
of course she will be always at the Palace with you."

Lord Damara listened to Lord Lior with perfect indifference, while he
chose to entertain himself in this manner; and as Lord Damara's
composure convinced him that all was safe, his wit flowed along.
