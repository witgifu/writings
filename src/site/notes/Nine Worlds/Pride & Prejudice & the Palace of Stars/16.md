As no objection was made to the young people's engagement with their
aunt, and all Lord Rufus's scruples of leaving Buru Tovo and Eidora for
a single evening during his visit were most steadily resisted, the yacht
conveyed him and his five relations at a suitable hour to Gorjo City;
and the cousins had the pleasure of hearing, as they entered the
drawing-room, that Saya Ealoapeha had accepted their uncle's invitation,
and was then in the house.

When this information was given, and they had all taken their seats,
Lord Rufus was at leisure to look around him and admire, and he was so
much struck with the size and furniture of the apartment, that he
declared he might almost have supposed himself in the small summer
breakfast parlour at Yra; a comparison that did not at first convey much
gratification; but when Aunt Onaya understood from him what Yra was, and
who was its proprietor, when she had listened to the description of only
one of Princes Anastasiya's drawing-rooms, and found that the
chimney-piece alone had cost eight hundred valiants, she felt all the
force of the compliment, and would hardly have resented a comparison
with the housekeeper's room.

In describing to her all the grandeur of Princes Anastasiya and her
palace, with occasional digressions in praise of his own humble abode,
and the improvements it was receiving, he was happily employed until the
others joined them; and he found in Aunt Onaya a very attentive
listener, whose opinion of his consequence increased with what she
heard, and who was resolving to retail it all among her neighbours as
soon as she could. To the youngsters, who could not listen to their
distant relation, and who had nothing to do but to wish for an
instrument, and examine their own indifferent imitations of china on the
mantel-piece, the interval of waiting appeared very long. It was over at
last, however. The others did approach: and when Saya Ealoapeha walked
into the room, Cliopher felt that he had neither been seeing her before,
nor thinking of her since, with the smallest degree of unreasonable
admiration. The officers of the navy ship were in general a very
creditable, genteel set and the best of them were of the present party;
but Saya Ealoapeha was as far beyond them all in person, countenance,
air, and walk, as *they* were superior to the broad-faced stuffy Uncle
Lazo, breathing port wine, who followed them into the room.

Saya Ealoapeha was the happy woman towards whom almost every young eye
was turned, and Cliopher was the happy man by whom she finally seated
herself; and the agreeable manner in which she immediately fell into
conversation, though it was only on its being a wet night, and on the
probability of a rainy season, made him feel that the commonest,
dullest, most threadbare topic might be rendered interesting by the
skill of the speaker.

With such rivals for the notice of the fair as Saya Ealoapeha and the
officers, Lord Rufus seemed to sink into insignificance; to the young
people he certainly was nothing; but he had still at intervals a kind
listener in Aunt Onaya, and was, by her watchfulness, most abundantly
supplied with coffee and muffin.

When the card tables were placed, he had an opportunity of obliging her,
in return, by sitting down to Poacher.

"I know little of the game at present," said he, "but I shall be glad to
improve myself; for in my situation of life------" Aunt Onaya was very
thankful for his compliance, but could not wait for his reason.

Saya Ealoapeha did not play at Poacher, and with ready delight was she
received at the other table between Cliopher and Hillen. At first there
seemed danger of Hillen's engrossing her entirely, for he was a most
determined talker; but being likewise extremely fond of lottery tickets,
he soon grew too much interested in the game, too eager in making bets
and exclaiming after prizes, to have attention for anyone in particular.
Allowing for the common demands of the game, Saya Ealoapeha was
therefore at leisure to talk to Cliopher, and he was very willing to
hear her, though what she chiefly wished to hear he could not hope to be
told, the history of her acquaintance with Lord Damara. He dared not
even mention that gentleman. His curiosity, however, was unexpectedly
relieved. Saya Ealoapeha began the subject herself. She inquired how far
Navikiani was from Tahivoa; and, after receiving her answer, asked in a
hesitating manner how long Lord Damara had been staying there.

"About a month," said Cliopher; and then, unwilling to let the subject
drop, added, "he is a man of very large property in Old Damara, I
understand."

"Yes," replied Ealoapeha; "his estate there is a noble one. A clear ten
thousand per annum. You could not have met with a person more capable of
giving you certain information on that head than myself---for I have
been connected with his family, in a particular manner, from my
infancy."

Cliopher could not but look surprised.

"You may well be surprised, Sayo Mdang, at such an assertion, after
seeing, as you probably might, the very cold manner of our meeting
yesterday. Are you much acquainted with 'Lord Damara', as I hear he is
known hereabouts?"

"As much as I ever wish to be," cried Cliopher, warmly. "I have spent
four days in the same house with him, and I think him very
disagreeable."

"I have no right to give *my* opinion," said Ealoapeha, "as to his being
agreeable or otherwise. I am not qualified to form one. I have known him
too long and too well to be a fair judge. It is impossible for *me* to
be impartial. But I believe your opinion of him would in general
astonish---and, perhaps, you would not express it quite so strongly
anywhere else. Here you are in your own family."

"Upon my word I say no more *here* than I might say in any house in the
neighbourhood, except Navikiani. He is not at all liked in the
Vangavaye-ve. Everybody is disgusted with his pride. You will not find
him more favourably spoken of by anyone."

"I cannot pretend to be sorry," said Ealoapeha, after a short
interruption, "that he or that any man should not be estimated beyond
their deserts; but with *him* I believe it does not often happen. The
world is blinded by his fortune and consequence, or frightened by his
high and imposing manners, and sees him only as he chooses to be seen."

"I should take him, even on *my* slight acquaintance, to be an
ill-tempered man."

Ealoapeha only shook her head.

"I wonder," said she, at the next opportunity of speaking, "whether he
is likely to be in this province much longer."

"I do not at all know; but I *heard* nothing of his going away when I
was at Navikiani. I hope your plans in favour of the navy will not be
affected by his being in the neighbourhood."

"Oh no---it is not for *me* to be driven away by Lord Damara. If *he*
wishes to avoid seeing *me* he must go. We are not on friendly terms,
and it always gives me pain to meet him, but I have no reason for
avoiding *him* but what I might proclaim to all the world---a sense of
very great ill-usage, and most painful regrets at his being what he is.
His father, Sayo Mdang, the late Grand Duke, was one of the best men
that ever breathed, and the truest friend I ever had; and I can never be
in company with this 'Lord Damara' without being grieved to the soul by
a thousand tender recollections. His behaviour to myself has been
scandalous; but I verily believe I could forgive him anything and
everything, rather than his disappointing the hopes and disgracing the
memory of his father."

Cliopher found the interest of the subject increase, and listened with
all his heart; but the delicacy of it prevented further inquiry.

Saya Ealoapeha began to speak on more general topics, Gorjo City, the
neighbourhood, the society, appearing highly pleased with all that she
had yet seen, and speaking of the latter, especially, with gentle but
very intelligible gallantry.

"It was the prospect of constant society, and good society," she added,
"which was my chief inducement to enter the navy. I know it to be a most
respectable, agreeable ship; and my friend Denny tempted me further by
his account of their present port, and the very great attentions and
excellent acquaintance Gorjo City had procured them. Society, I own, is
necessary to me. I have been a disappointed woman, and my spirits will
not bear solitude. I *must* have employment and society. A navy life is
not what I was intended for, but circumstances have now made it
eligible. The Imperial Cult *ought* to have been my profession---I was
brought up for the Ouranatha; and I should at this time have been in
possession of a most valuable position, had it pleased the gentleman we
were speaking of just now."

"Indeed!"

"Yes---the late Grand Duke bequeathed me the next presentation of the
best position in his gift. He was my godfather, and excessively attached
to me. I cannot do justice to his kindness. He meant to provide for me
amply, and thought he had done it; but when the position fell, it was
given elsewhere."

"Good heavens!" cried Cliopher; "but how could *that* be? How could his
will be disregarded? Why did not you seek legal redress?"

"There was just such an informality in the terms of the bequest as to
give me no hope from law. A man of honour could not have doubted the
intention, but Lord Damara chose to doubt it---or to treat it as a
merely conditional recommendation, and to assert that I had forfeited
all claim to it by extravagance, imprudence, in short, anything or
nothing. Certain it is that the position became vacant two years ago,
exactly as I was of an age to hold it, and that it was given to another;
and no less certain is it, that I cannot accuse myself of having really
done anything to deserve to lose it. I have a warm unguarded temper, and
I may perhaps have sometimes spoken my opinion *of* him, and *to* him,
too freely. I can recall nothing worse. But the fact is, that we are
very different sort of people, and that he hates me."

"This is quite shocking! He deserves to be publicly disgraced."

"Some time or other he *will* be---but it shall not be by *me*. Till I
can forget his father, I can never defy or expose *him*."

Cliopher honoured her for such feelings, and thought her handsomer than
ever as she expressed them.

"But what," said he, after a pause, "can have been his motive? what can
have induced him to behave so cruelly?"

"A thorough, determined dislike of me---a dislike which I cannot but
attribute in some measure to jealousy. Had the late Grand Duke liked me
less, his son might have borne with me better; but his father's uncommon
attachment to me irritated him, I believe, very early in life. He had
not a temper to bear the sort of competition in which we stood---the
sort of preference which was often given me."

"I had not thought Lord Damara so bad as this---though I have never
liked him, I had not thought so very ill of him---I had supposed him to
be despising his fellow-creatures in general, but did not suspect him of
descending to such malicious revenge, such injustice, such inhumanity as
this!"

After a few minutes' reflection, however, he continued, "I *do* remember
his boasting one day, at Navikiani, of the implacability of his
resentments, of his having an unforgiving temper. His disposition must
be dreadful."

"I will not trust myself on the subject," replied Ealoapeha; "*I* can
hardly be just to him."

Cliopher was again deep in thought, and after a time exclaimed, "To
treat in such a manner the goddaughter, the friend, the favourite of his
father!" He could have added, "A young woman, too, like *you*, whose
very countenance may vouch for your being amiable." But he contented
herself with---"And one, too, who had probably been his own companion
from childhood, connected together, as I think you said, in the closest
manner."

"We were born in the same place, grew within the same lands; the
greatest part of our youth was passed together: inmates of the same
house, sharing the same amusements, objects of the same care. *My*
father began life in the profession which your uncle, Uncle Lazo,
appears to do so much credit to; but he gave up everything to be of use
to the late Grand Duke, and devoted all his time to the care of the
Damaran property. He was most highly esteemed by Lord Damara, a most
intimate, confidential friend. Lord Damara often acknowledged himself to
be under the greatest obligations to my father's active superintendence;
and when, immediately before my father's death, Lord Damara gave him a
voluntary promise of providing for me, I am convinced that he felt it to
be as much a debt of gratitude to *him* as of affection to myself."

"How strange!" cried Cliopher. "How abominable! I wonder that the very
pride of this Lord Damara has not made him just to you. If from no
better motive, that he should not have been too proud to be
dishonest,---for dishonesty I must call it."

"It *is* wonderful," replied Ealoapeha; "for almost all his actions may
be traced to pride; and pride has often been his best friend. It has
connected him nearer with virtue than any other feeling. But we are none
of us consistent; and in his behaviour to me there were stronger
impulses even than pride."

"Can such abominable pride as his have ever done him good?"

"Yes; it has often led him to be liberal and generous; to give his money
freely, to display hospitality, to assist his tenants, and relieve the
poor. Family pride, and *filial* pride, for he is very proud of what his
father was, have done this. Not to appear to disgrace his family, to
degenerate from the popular qualities, or lose the influence of the
Damaran name, is a powerful motive. He has also *brotherly* pride,
which, with *some* brotherly affection, makes him a very kind and
careful guardian of his sister; and you will hear him generally cried up
as the most attentive and best of brothers."

"What sort of a girl is Lady Damara?"

He shook his head. "I wish I could call her amiable. It gives me pain to
speak ill of a Damaran; but she is too much like her brother,---very,
very proud. As a child, she was affectionate and pleasing, and extremely
fond of me; and I have devoted hours and hours to her amusement. But she
is nothing to me now. She is a handsome girl, about fifteen or sixteen,
and, I understand, highly accomplished. Since her father's death her
home has been Solaara, where a lady lives with her, and superintends her
education."

After many pauses and many trials of other subjects, Cliopher could not
help reverting once more to the first, and saying,---

"I am astonished at his intimacy with the Cavalier. How can the
Cavalier, who seems good-humour itself, and is, I really believe, truly
amiable, be in friendship with such a man? How can they suit each other?
Do you know the Cavalier?"

"Not at all."

"He is a sweet-tempered, amiable, charming man. He cannot know what Lord
Damara is."

"Probably not; but Lord Damara can please where he chooses. He does not
want abilities. He can be a conversible companion if he thinks it worth
his while. Among those who are at all his equals in consequence, he is a
very different man from what he is to the less prosperous. His pride
never deserts him; but with the rich he is liberal-minded, just,
sincere, rational, honourable, and, perhaps, agreeable,---allowing
something for fortune and figure."

The Poacher party soon afterwards breaking up, the players gathered
round the other table, and Lord Rufus took his station between Cliopher
and Aunt Onaya. The usual inquiries as to his success were made by the
latter. It had not been very great; he had lost every point; but when
Aunt Onaya began to express her concern thereupon, he assured her, with
much earnest gravity, that it was not of the least importance; that he
considered the money as a mere trifle, and begged she would not make
herself uneasy.

"I know very well, madam," said he, "that when persons sit down to a
card table they must take their chance of these things,---and happily I
am not in such circumstances as to make half a valiant any object. There
are, undoubtedly, many who could not say the same; but, thanks to
Princess Anastasiya, I am removed far beyond the necessity of regarding
little matters."

Saya Ealoapeha's attention was caught; and after observing Lord Rufus
for a few moments, he asked Cliopher in a low voice whether her
relations were very intimately acquainted with the family of Yra.

"Princess Anastasiya Yra," she replied, "has very lately given him a
position. I hardly know how Lord Rufus was first introduced to her
notice, but he certainly has not known her long."

"You know of course that Princess Anastasiya Yra and Empress Anyoë were
sisters; consequently that she is great-aunt to the present Lord
Damara."

"No, indeed, I did not. I knew nothing at all of Princess Anastasiya's
connections. I never heard of her existence till the day before
yesterday."

"Her great-niece, will have a very large fortune from her, and it is
believed that she and he will unite the two estates."

This information made Cliopher smile, as she thought of poor Lord Lior.
Vain indeed must be all his attentions, vain and useless his affection
for his sister and his praise of himself, if Lord Damara were already
self-destined to another.

"Lord Rufus," said he, "speaks highly both of Princess Anastasiya and
her great-niece; but, from some particulars that he has related of her
Highness, I suspect his gratitude misleads him; and that, in spite of
her being his patroness, she is an arrogant, conceited woman."

"I believe her to be both in a great degree," replied Ealoapeha; "I have
not seen her for many years; but I very well remember that I never liked
her, and that her manners were dictatorial and insolent. She has the
reputation of being remarkably sensible and clever; but I rather believe
she derives part of her abilities from her rank and fortune, part from
her authoritative manner, and the rest from the pride of her
great-nephew, who chooses that everyone connected with him should have
an understanding of the first class."

Cliopher allowed that he had given a very rational account of it, and
they continued talking together with mutual satisfaction till supper put
an end to cards, and gave the rest of the people their share of Saya
Ealoapeha's attentions. There could be no conversation in the noise of
Aunt Onaya's supper party, but her manners recommended her to everybody.
Whatever she said, was said well; and whatever she did, done gracefully.
Cliopher went away with his head full of her. He could think of nothing
but of Saya Ealoapeha, and of what she had told him, all the way home;
but there was not time for him even to mention her name as they went,
for neither Hillen nor Lord Rufus were once silent. Hillen talked
incessantly of lottery tickets, of the fish he had lost and the fish he
had won; and Lord Rufus, in describing the civility of Uncle Lazo and
Aunt Onaya, protesting that he did not in the least regard his losses at
Poacher, enumerating all the dishes at supper, and repeatedly fearing
that he crowded his relations, had more to say than he could well manage
before the yacht stopped at Tahivoa.
