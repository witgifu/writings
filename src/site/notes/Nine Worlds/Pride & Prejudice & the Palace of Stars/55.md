A few days after this visit, the Cavalier called again, and alone. His
friend had left him that morning for Solaara, but was to return home in
ten days' time. He sat with them above an hour, and was in remarkably
good spirits. Eidora Mdang invited him to dine with them; but, with many
expressions of concern, he confessed himself engaged elsewhere.

"Next time you call," said she, "I hope we shall be more lucky."

He should be particularly happy at any time, etc., etc.; and if she
would give him leave, would take an early opportunity of waiting on
them.

"Can you come to-morrow?"

Yes, he had no engagement at all for to-morrow; and her invitation was
accepted with alacrity.

He came, and in such very good time, that they were none of them
dressed. In ran Eidora Mdang to her nephew's room, in her dressing-gown,
and with her hair half finished, crying out,---

"My dear Quintus, make haste and hurry down. He is come---the Cavalier
is come. He is, indeed. Make haste, make haste. Here, Sarah, come to
Sayo Mdang this moment, and help him on with his robe's. Never mind
Kip's hair."

"We will be down as soon as we can," said Quintus; "but I dare say Haro
is forwarder than either of us, for he went upstairs half an hour ago."

"Oh! hang Haro! what has he to do with it? Come, be quick, be quick!
where is your sash, my dear?"

But when his aunt was gone, Quintus would not be prevailed on to go down
without one of his cousins.

The same anxiety to get them by themselves was visible again in the
evening. After tea, Buru Tovo retired to the library, as was his custom,
and Louya went upstairs to her instrument. Two obstacles of the five
being thus removed, Eidora Mdang sat looking and winking at Cliopher and
Haro for a considerable time, without making any impression on them.
Cliopher would not observe her; and when at last Haro did, he very
innocently said, "What is the matter, aunty? What do you keep winking at
me for? What am I to do?"

"Nothing, child, nothing. I did not wink at you." She then sat still
five minutes longer; but unable to waste such a precious occasion, she
suddenly got up, and saying to Haro,---

"Come here, my love, I want to speak to you," took him out of the room.
Quintus instantly gave a look at Cliopher which spoke his distress at
such premeditation, and his entreaty that *he* would not give in to it.
In a few minutes, Eidora Mdang half opened the door and called out,---

"Kip, my dear, I want to speak with you."

Cliopher was forced to go.

"We may as well leave them by themselves, you know," said his mother as
soon as she was in the hall. "Haro and I are going upstairs to sit in my
dressing-room."

Cliopher made no attempt to reason with his mother, but remained quietly
in the hall till she and Haro were out of sight, then returned into the
drawing-room.

Eidora Mdang's schemes for this day were ineffectual. An Vilius was
everything that was charming, except the professed lover of her nephew.
His ease and cheerfulness rendered him a most agreeable addition to
their evening party; and he bore with the ill-judged officiousness of
the mother, and heard all her silly remarks with a forbearance and
command of countenance particularly grateful to the nephew.

He scarcely needed an invitation to stay supper; and before he went away
an engagement was formed, chiefly through his own and Eidora Mdang's
means, for his coming next morning to fish with her uncle.

After this day, Quintus said no more of his indifference. Not a word
passed between the cousins concerning an Vilius; but Cliopher went to
bed in the happy belief that all must speedily be concluded, unless Lord
Damara returned within the stated time. Seriously, however, he felt
tolerably persuaded that all this must have taken place with that
gentleman's concurrence.

An Vilius was punctual to his appointment; and he and Buru Tovo spent
the morning together, as had been agreed on. The latter was much more
agreeable than his companion expected. There was nothing of presumption
or folly in an Vilius that could provoke his ridicule, or disgust him
into silence; and he was more communicative, and less eccentric, than
the other had ever seen him. An Vilius of course returned with him to
dinner; and in the evening Eidora Mdang's invention was again at work to
get everybody away from him and her nephew. Cliopher, who had a letter
to write, went into the breakfast-room for that purpose soon after tea;
for as the others were all going to sit down to cards, he could not be
wanted to counteract his mother's schemes.

But on his returning to the drawing-room, when his letter was finished,
he saw, to her infinite surprise, there was reason to fear that his
mother had been too ingenious. On opening the door, he perceived his
cousin and an Vilius standing together over the hearth, as if engaged in
earnest conversation; and had this led to no suspicion, the faces of
both, as they hastily turned round and moved away from each other, would
have told it all. *Their* situation was awkward enough; but *his* he
thought was still worse. Not a syllable was uttered by either; and
Cliopher was on the point of going away again, when an Vilius, who as
well as the other had sat down, suddenly rose, and, whispering a few
words to his cousin, ran out of the room.

Quintus could have no reserves from Cliopher, where confidence would
give pleasure; and, instantly embracing him, acknowledged, with the
liveliest emotion, that he was the happiest creature in the world.

"'Tis too much!" he added, "by far too much. I do not deserve it. Oh,
why is not everybody as happy?"

Cliopher's congratulations were given with a sincerity, a warmth, a
delight, which words could but poorly express. Every sentence of
kindness was a fresh source of happiness to Quintus. But he would not
allow himself to stay with his cousin, or say half that remained to be
said, for the present.

"I must go instantly to my aunt," he cried. "I would not on any account
trifle with her affectionate solicitude, or allow her to hear it from
anyone but myself. He is gone to Uncle Tovo already. Oh, Kip, to know
that what I have to relate will give such pleasure to all my dear
family! how shall I bear so much happiness?"

He then hastened away to his aunt, who had purposely broken up the
card-party, and was sitting upstairs with Haro.

Cliopher, who was left by himself, now smiled at the rapidity and ease
with which an affair was finally settled, that had given them so many
previous months of suspense and vexation.

"And this," said he, "is the end of all his friend's anxious
circumspection! of all his cousin's falsehood and contrivance! the
happiest, wisest, and most reasonable end!"

In a few minutes he was joined by an Vilius, whose conference with his
great-uncle had been short and to the purpose.

"Where is your cousin?" said he hastily, as he opened the door.

"With my mother upstairs. He will be down in a moment, I dare say."

He then shut the door, and, coming up to Cliopher, claimed the good
wishes and affection of a cousin. Cliopher honestly and heartily
expressed his delight in the prospect of their relationship. They shook
hands with great cordiality; and then, till his cousin came down, he had
to listen to all he had to say of his own happiness, and of Quintus's
perfections; and in spite of his being a lover, Cliopher really believed
all his expectations of felicity to be rationally founded, because they
had for basis the excellent understanding and super-excellent
disposition of Quintus, and a general similarity of feeling and taste
between him and himself.

It was an evening of no common delight to them all; the satisfaction of
Sayo Mdang's mind gave such a glow of sweet animation to his face, as
made him look handsomer than ever. Haro simpered and smiled, and hoped
his turn was coming soon. Eidora Mdang could not give her consent, or
speak her approbation in terms warm enough to satisfy her feelings,
though she talked to an Vilius of nothing else, for half an hour; and
when Buru Tovo joined them at supper, his voice and manner plainly
showed how really happy he was.

Not a word, however, passed his lips in allusion to it, till their
visitor took his leave for the night; but as soon as he was gone, he
turned to his grand-nephew and said,---

"Quintus, I congratulate you. You will be a very happy man."

Quintus went to him instantly, kissed him, and thanked him for his
goodness.

"You are a good boy," he replied, "and I have great pleasure in thinking
you will be so happily settled. I have not a doubt of your doing very
well together. Your tempers are by no means unlike. You are each of you
so complying, that nothing will ever be resolved on; so easy, that every
servant will cheat you; and so generous, that you will always exceed
your income."

"I hope not so. Imprudence or thoughtlessness in money matters would be
unpardonable in *me*."

"Exceed their income! My dear Buru Tovo," cried his niece, "what are you
talking of? Why, he has four or five thousand a year, and very likely
more." Then addressing her nephew, "Oh, my dear, dear Quintus, I am so
happy! I am sure I shan't get a wink of sleep all night. I knew how it
would be. I always said it must be so, at last. I was sure you could not
be so beautiful for nothing! I remember, as soon as ever I saw him, when
he first came into the Vangaveye-ve last year, I thought how likely it
was that you should come together. Oh, he is the handsomest man that
ever was seen!"

Ealoapeha, Hillen, were all forgotten. Quintus was beyond competition
her favourite. At that moment she cared for no other. Her younger
sisters soon began to make interest with her for objects of happiness
which she might in future be able to dispense.

Louya petitioned for the use of the library at Navikiani; and Haro
begged very hard for a few balls there every winter.

An Vilius, from this time, was of course a daily visitor at Tahivoa;
coming frequently before breakfast, and always remaining till after
supper; unless when some barbarous neighbour, who could not be enough
detested, had given him an invitation to dinner, which he thought
himself obliged to accept.

Cliopher had now but little time for conversation with his cousin; for
while he was present Quintus had no attention to bestow on anyone else:
but he found herself considerably useful to both of them, in those hours
of separation that must sometimes occur. In the absence of Quintus, he
always attached himself to Cliopher for the pleasure of talking of him;
and when an Vilius was gone, Quintus constantly sought the same means of
relief.

"He has made me so happy," said he, one evening, "by telling me that he
was totally ignorant of my being in town last spring! I had not believed
it possible."

"I suspected as much," replied Cliopher. "But how did he account for
it?"

"It must have been his cousins' doing. They were certainly no friends to
his acquaintance with me, which I cannot wonder at, since he might have
chosen so much more advantageously in many respects. But when they see,
as I trust they will, that their cousin is happy with me, they will
learn to be contented, and we shall be on good terms again: though we
can never be what we once were to each other."

"That is the most unforgiving speech," said Cliopher, "that I ever heard
you utter. Good boy! It would vex me, indeed, to see you again the dupe
of Lord Lior's pretended regard."

"Would you believe it, Kip, that when he went to town last November he
really loved me, and nothing but a persuasion of *my* being indifferent
would have prevented his coming down again?"

"He made a little mistake, to be sure; but it is to the credit of his
modesty."

This naturally introduced a panegyric from Quintus on his diffidence,
and the little value he put on his own good qualities.

Cliopher was pleased to find that he had not betrayed the interference
of his friend; for, though Quintus had the most generous and forgiving
heart in the world, he knew it was a circumstance which must prejudice
him against an Vilius.

"I am certainly the most fortunate creature that ever existed!" cried
Quintus. "Oh, Kip, why am I thus singled from my family, and blessed
above them all? If I could but see you as happy! If there were but such
another for you!"

"If you were to give me forty such I never could be so happy as you.
Till I have your disposition, your goodness, I never can have your
happiness. No, no, let me shift for myself; and, perhaps, if I have very
good luck, I may meet with another Lord Rufus in time."

The situation of affairs in the Tahivoa family could not be long a
secret. Eidora Mdang was privileged to whisper it to Aunt Onaya, and she
ventured, without any permission, to do the same by all her neighbours
in Gorjo City.

The Mdangs were speedily pronounced to be the luckiest family in the
world; though only a few weeks before, when Hillen had first run away,
they had been generally proved to be marked out for misfortune.
