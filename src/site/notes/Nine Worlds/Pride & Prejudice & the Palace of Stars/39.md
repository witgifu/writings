It was the second week in May, in which the three young people set out
together, in the Vangaveye-ve; and, as they drew near the appointed inn
where Buru Tovo's yacht was to meet them, they quickly perceived, in
token of the sailor's punctuality, both Haro and Hillen looking out of a
dining-room upstairs. These two had been above an hour in the place,
happily employed in visiting an opposite milliner, watching the sentinel
on guard, and dressing a salad and cucumber.

After welcoming their cousins, they triumphantly displayed a table set
out with such cold meat as an inn larder usually affords, exclaiming,
"Is not this nice? is not this an agreeable surprise?"

"And we mean to treat you all," added Hillen; "but you must lend us the
money, for we have just spent ours at the shop out there." Then showing
his purchases,---"Look here, I have bought this hat. I do not think it
is very pretty; but I thought I might as well buy it as not. I shall
pull it to pieces as soon as I get home, and see if I can make it up any
better."

And when his cousins abused it as ugly, he added, with perfect
unconcern, "Oh, but there were two or three much uglier in the shop; and
when I have bought some prettier-coloured satin to trim it with fresh, I
think it will be very tolerable. Besides, it will not much signify what
one wears this summer, after the navy have left Gorjo City, and they are
going in a fortnight."

"Are they, indeed?" cried Cliopher, with the greatest satisfaction.

"They are going to be encamped near Nijan; and I do so want Uncle Tovo
to take us all there for the summer! It would be such a delicious
scheme, and I dare say would hardly cost anything at all. Auntie would
like to go, too, of all things! Only think what a miserable summer else
we shall have!"

"Yes," thought Cliopher; "*that* would be a delightful scheme, indeed,
and completely do for us at once. Good Heaven! Nijan and a whole harbour
of navy sailors, to us, who have been overset already by one poor ship,
and the monthly parties of Gorjo City!"

"Now I have got some news for you," said Hillen, as they sat down to
table. "What do you think? It is excellent news, capital news, and about
a certain person that we all like."

Quintus and Cliopher looked at each other, and the waiter was told that
he need not stay. Hillen laughed, and said,---

"Ay, that is just like your formality and discretion. You thought the
waiter must not hear, as if he cared! I dare say he often hears worse
things said than I am going to say. But he is an ugly fellow! I am glad
he is gone. I never saw such a long chin in my life. Well, but now for
my news: it is about dear Ealoapeha; too good for the waiter, is not it?
There is no danger of Ealoapeha's marrying Ouya saya King---there's for
you! She is gone down to her uncle in the Isolates; gone to stay.
Ealoapeha is safe."

"And Ouya saya King is safe!" added Cliopher; "safe from a connection
imprudent as to fortune."

"She is a great fool for going away, if she liked her."

"But I hope there is no strong attachment on either side," said Quintus.

"I am sure there is not on *hers*. I will answer for it, she never cared
three straws about her. Who *could* about such a nasty little freckled
thing?"

Cliopher was shocked to think that, however incapable of such coarseness
of *expression* himself, the coarseness of the *sentiment* was little
other than his own breast had formerly harboured and fancied liberal!

As soon as all had ate, and the elder ones paid, the yacht was ordered;
and, after some contrivance, the whole party, with all their boxes,
workbags, and parcels, and the unwelcome addition of Haro's and Hillen's
purchases, were seated in it.

"How nicely we are crammed in!" cried Hillen. "I am glad I brought my
hat, if it is only for the fun of having another band-box! Well, now let
us be quite comfortable and snug, and talk and laugh all the way home.
And in the first place, let us hear what has happened to you all since
you went away. Have you seen any pleasant people? Have you had any
flirting? I was in great hopes that one of you would have got a spouse
before you came back. Quintus will be quite an old man soon, I declare.
He is almost three-and-twenty! Lord! how ashamed I should be of not
being married before three-and-twenty! My Aunt Oura wants you so to get
spouses you can't think. She says Kip had better have taken Lord Rufus;
but *I* do not think there would have been any fun in it. Lord! how I
should like to be married before any of you! and then I would *chaperon*
you about to all the balls. Dear me! we had such a good piece of fun the
other day at Captain Forster's! Haro and me were to spend the day there,
and his husband promised to have a little dance in the evening;
(by-the-bye, Sayo Forster and me are *such* friends!) and so he asked
the two Harringtons to come: but Harriet was ill, and so Pen was forced
to come by herself; and then, what do you think we did? We dressed up
Chamberlayne in woman's clothes, on purpose to pass for a lady,---only
think what fun! Not a soul knew of it, but Colonel and Sayo Forster, and
Haro and me, except my aunt, for we were forced to borrow one of her
gowns; and you cannot imagine how well he looked! When Denny, and
Ealoapeha, and Pratt, and two or three more of the officers came in,
they did not know him in the least. Lord! how I laughed! and so did Sayo
Forster. I thought I should have died. And *that* made the men suspect
something, and then they soon found out what was the matter."

With such kind of histories of their parties and good jokes did Hillen,
assisted by Haro's hints and additions, endeavour to amuse his
companions all the way to Tahivoa. Cliopher listened as little as he
could, but there was no escaping the frequent mention of Ealoapeha's
name.

Their reception at home was most kind. Eidora Mdang rejoiced to see
Quintus in undiminished beauty; and more than once during dinner did
Buru Tovo say voluntarily to Cliopher,------

"I am glad you are come back, Kip."

Their party in the dining-room was large, for almost all the an
Dirondelles came to meet Rusticiana and hear the news; and various were
the subjects which occupied them: Lady an Dirondelle was inquiring of
Rusticiana, across the table, after the welfare and poultry of her
eldest daughter; Eidora Mdang was doubly engaged, on one hand collecting
an account of the present fashions from Quintus, who sat some way below
her, and on the other, retailing them all to the younger Miss an
Dirondelles; and Hillen, in a voice rather louder than any other
person's, was enumerating the various pleasures of the morning to
anybody who would hear him.

"Oh, Louya," said he, "I wish you had gone with us, for we had such fun!
as we went along Haro and me went under the deck, and pretended there
was nobody in the yacht; and I should have gone so all the way, if Haro
had not been sick; and when we got to the Adelia, I do think we behaved
very handsomely, for we treated the other three with the nicest cold
luncheon in the world, and if you would have gone, we would have treated
you too. And then when we came away it was such fun! I thought we never
should have got into the yacht. I was ready to die of laughter. And then
we were so merry all the way home! we talked and laughed so loud, that
anybody might have heard us ten miles off!"

To this, Louya very gravely replied, "Far be it from me, my dear cousin,
to depreciate such pleasures. They would doubtless be congenial with the
generality of your minds. But I confess they would have no charms for
*me*. I should infinitely prefer a book."

But of this answer Hillen heard not a word. He seldom listened to
anybody for more than half a minute, and never attended to Louya at all.

In the afternoon Hillen was urgent with the rest to walk to Gorjo City,
and see how everybody went on; but Cliopher steadily opposed the scheme.
It should not be said, that the Sayo Mdangs could not be at home half a
day before they were in pursuit of the officers. There was another
reason, too, for his opposition. He dreaded seeing Ealoapeha again, and
was resolved to avoid it as long as possible. The comfort to *him*, of
the ship's approaching removal, was indeed beyond expression. In a
fortnight they were to go, and once gone, he hoped there could be
nothing more to plague him on her account.

He had not been many hours at home, before he found that the Nijan
scheme, of which Hillen had given them a hint at the inn, was under
frequent discussion between his elders. Cliopher saw directly that his
great-uncle had not the smallest intention of yielding; but his answers
were at the same time so vague and equivocal, that his mother, though
often disheartened, had never yet despaired of succeeding at last.
