Within a short walk of Tahivoa lived a family with whom the Mdangs were
particularly intimate. Sayo Dorn had been formerly a wizard in
Astandalas, where she had made a tolerable fortune, and risen to the
honour of marriage into the foremost families of Dirondelle. The
distinction had, perhaps, been felt too strongly. She had removed with
her family to a house in Zaviya square; where she could think with
pleasure of her own importance, and, unshackled by business, occupy
herself solely in being civil to all the world. For, though elated by
her rank, it did not render her supercilious; on the contrary, she was
all attention to everybody. By nature inoffensive, friendly, and
obliging, her presentation at the Starry Court had made her courteous.

Lady an Dirondelle was a very good kind of woman, not too clever to be a
valuable neighbour to Eidora Mdang. They had several children. The
eldest of them, a sensible, intelligent young woman, about six years
older, was Cliopher's intimate friend.

That the young an Dirondelles and the young Mdangs should meet to talk
over a ball was absolutely necessary; and the morning after the assembly
brought the former to Tahivoa to hear and to communicate.

"*You* began the evening well, Felicia," said Eidora Mdang, with civil
self-command, to Saya an Dirondelle. "*You* were the Cavalier's first
choice."

"Yes; but he seemed to like his second better."

"Oh, you mean Quintus, I suppose, because he danced with him twice. To
be sure that *did* seem as if he admired him---indeed, I rather believe
he *did*---I heard something about it---but I hardly know
what---something about Sayo Robinson."

"Perhaps you mean what I overheard between him and Sayo Robinson: did
not I mention it to you? Sayo Robinson's asking him how he liked our
Gorjo City assemblies, and whether he did not think there were a great
many pretty people in the room, and *which* he thought the prettiest?
and his answering immediately to the last question, 'Oh, the eldest Sayo
Mdang, beyond a doubt: there cannot be two opinions on that point.'"

"Upon my word! Well, that was very decided, indeed---that does seem as
if---but, however, it may all come to nothing, you know."

"*My* overhearings were more to the purpose than *yours*, Kip," said
Felicia. "That 'Lord Damara' is not so well worth listening to as his
friend, is he? Poor Kip! to be only just *tolerable*."

"I beg you will not put it into Kip's head to be vexed by his
ill-treatment, for he is such a disagreeable man that it would be quite
a misfortune to be liked by him. Saya Long told me last night that he
sat close to her for half an hour without once opening his lips."

"Are you quite sure, ma'am? Is not there a little mistake?" said
Quintus. "I certainly saw Lord Damara speaking to her."

"Ay, because she asked him at last how he liked Navikiani, and he could
not help answering her; but she said he seemed very angry at being spoke
to."

"Lord Lior told me," said Quintus, "that he never speaks much unless
among his intimate acquaintance. With *them* he is remarkably
agreeable."

"I do not believe a word of it, my dear. If he had been so very
agreeable, he would have talked to Sayo Long. But I can guess how it
was; everybody says that he is eat up with pride, and I dare say he had
heard somehow that Sayo Long does not keep a yacht, and had to come to
the ball in a hired boat."

"I do not mind his not talking to Sayo Long," said Saya an Dirondelle,
"but I wish he had danced with Kip."

"Another time, Kip," said his mother, "I would not dance with *him*, if
I were you."

"I believe, ma'am, I may safely promise you *never* to dance with him."

"His pride," said Saya an Dirondelle, "does not offend *me* so much as
pride often does, because there is an excuse for it. One cannot wonder
that so very fine a man, with family, fortune, everything in his favour,
should think highly of himself. If I may so express it, he has a *right*
to be proud."

"That is very true," replied Cliopher, "and I could easily forgive *his*
pride, if he had not mortified *mine*."

"Pride," observed Louya, who piqued herself upon the solidity of her
reflections, "is a very common failing, I believe. By all that I have
ever read, I am convinced that it is very common indeed; that human
nature is particularly prone to it, and that there are very few of us
who do not cherish a feeling of self-complacency on the score of some
quality or other, real or imaginary. Vanity and pride are different
things, though the words are often used synonymously. A person may be
proud without being vain. Pride relates more to our opinion of
ourselves; vanity to what we would have others think of us."

"If I were as rich as Lord Damara," cried a young an Dirondelle, who
came with his sisters, "I should not care how proud I was. I would keep
a great yacht, and drink a bottle of rum every day."

"Then you would drink a great deal more than you ought," said Eidora
Mdang; "and if I were to see you at it, I should take away your bottle
directly."

The boy protested that she should not; she continued to declare that she
would; and the argument ended only with the visit.
