After a week spent in professions of love and schemes of felicity, Lord
Rufus was called from his amiable Felicia by the arrival of Saturday.
The pain of separation, however, might be alleviated on his side by
preparations for the reception of his bride, as he had reason to hope,
that shortly after his next return into the Vangaveye-ve, the day would
be fixed that was to make him the happiest of men. He took leave of his
relations at Tahivoa with as much solemnity as before; wished his
relations health and happiness again, and promised their father another
letter of thanks.

On the following Monday, Eidora Mdang had the pleasure of receiving her
sister and her husband, who came, as usual, to spend Silverheart at
Tahivoa. Aunt Oura was an intelligent, amiable woman, greatly superior
to her sister, as well by nature as education. The youngest Tahivoa
cousins would have had difficulty in believing that a woman who lived by
trade, and within view of her own warehouses, could have been so
well-bred and agreeable. But between the two eldest and herself
especially, there subsisted a very particular regard. They had
frequently been staying with her in town.

The first part of Aunt Oura's business, on her arrival, was to
distribute her presents and describe the newest fashions. When this was
done, she had a less active part to play. It became her turn to listen.
Eidora Mdang had many grievances to relate, and much to complain of.
They had all been very ill-used since she last saw her sister. Two of
her family had been on the point of marriage, and after all there was
nothing in it.

"I do not blame Quintus," she continued, "for Quintus would have got the
Cavalier if he could. But, Kip! Oh, sister! it is very hard to think
that he might have been Lord Rufus's spouse by this time, had not it
been for his own perverseness. He made him an offer in this very room,
and he refused him. The consequence of it is, that Lady an Dirondelle
will have a child married before I have, and that Tahivoa estate is just
as much entailed as ever. The an Dirondelles are very artful people,
indeed, sister. They are all for what they can get. I am sorry to say it
of them, but so it is. It makes me very nervous and poorly, to be
thwarted so in my own family, and to have neighbours who think of
themselves before anybody else. However, your coming just at this time
is the greatest of comforts, and I am very glad to hear what you tell us
of long sleeves."

Aunt Oura, to whom the chief of this news had been given before, in the
course of Quintus and Cliopher's correspondence with her, made her
sister a slight answer, and, in compassion to her nephews, turned the
conversation.

When alone with Cliopher afterwards, she spoke more on the subject. "It
seems likely to have been a desirable match for Quintus," said she. "I
am sorry it went off. But these things happen so often! A man, such as
you describe the Cavalier, so easily falls in love with a pretty face
for a few weeks, and, when accident separates them, so easily forgets
them, that these sort of inconstancies are very frequent."

"An excellent consolation in its way," said Cliopher; "but it will not
do for *us*. We do not suffer by accident. It does not often happen that
the interference of friends will persuade a man of independent fortune
to think no more of one whom he was violently in love with only a few
days before."

"But that expression of 'violently in love' is so hackneyed, so
doubtful, so indefinite, that it gives me very little idea. It is as
often applied to feelings which arise only from a half hour's
acquaintance, as to a real, strong attachment. Pray, how *violent was*
the Cavalier's love?"

"I never saw a more promising inclination; he was growing quite
inattentive to other people, and wholly engrossed by Quintus. Every time
they met, it was more decided and remarkable. At his own ball he
offended two or three young people by not asking them to dance; and I
spoke to him twice myself without receiving an answer. Could there be
finer symptoms? Is not general incivility the very essence of love?"

"Oh, yes! of that kind of love which I suppose him to have felt. Poor
Quintus! I am sorry for him, because, with his disposition, he may not
get over it immediately. It had better have happened to *you*, Kip; you
would have laughed yourself out of it sooner. But do you think he would
be prevailed on to go back with us? Change of scene might be of
service---and perhaps a little relief from home may be as useful as
anything."

Cliopher was exceedingly pleased with this proposal, and felt persuaded
of his cousin's ready acquiescence.

"I hope," added Aunt Oura, "that no consideration with regard to this
young man will influence him. We live in so different a part of the
city, all our connections are so different, and, as you well know, we go
out so little, that it is very improbable they should meet at all,
unless he really comes to see him."

"And *that* is quite impossible; for he is now in the custody of his
friend, and Lord Damara would no more suffer him to call on Quintus in
such a part of Solaara! My dear aunt, how could you think of it? Lord
Damara may, perhaps, have *heard* of such a place as Gracechurch Street,
but he would hardly think a month's ablution enough to cleanse him from
its impurities, were he once to enter it; and, depend upon it, the
Cavalier never stirs without him."

"So much the better. I hope they will not meet at all. But does not
Quintus correspond with his cousin? *He* will not be able to help
calling."

"He will drop the acquaintance entirely."

But, in spite of the certainty in which Cliopher affected to place this
point, as well as the still more interesting one of the Cavalier's being
withheld from seeing Quintus, he felt a solicitude on the subject which
convinced him, on examination, that he did not consider it entirely
hopeless. It was possible, and sometimes he thought it probable, that
his affection might be re-animated, and the influence of his friends
successfully combated by the more natural influence of Quintus's
attractions.

Sayo Mdang accepted his aunt's invitation with pleasure; and the
Cavalier's family were not otherwise in his thoughts at the same time
than as he hoped, by Lionel's not living in the same house with his
cousin, he might occasionally spend a morning with the Cavalier, without
any danger of seeing him.

Aunt Oura stayed a week at Tahivoa; and what with Uncle Lazo, the an
Dirondellees, and the officers, there was not a day without its
engagement. Eidora Mdang had so carefully provided for the entertainment
of her sister, that they did not once sit down to a family dinner. When
the engagement was for home, some of the officers always made part of
it, of which officers Saya Ealoapeha was sure to be one; and on these
occasions Aunt Oura, rendered suspicious by Cliopher's warm commendation
of her, narrowly observed them both. Without supposing them, from what
she saw, to be very seriously in love, their preference of each other
was plain enough to make her a little uneasy; and she resolved to speak
to Cliopher on the subject before she left the Vangaveye-ve, and
represent to him the imprudence of encouraging such an attachment.

To Aunt Oura, Ealoapeha had one means of affording pleasure, unconnected
with her general powers. About ten or a dozen years ago, she had spent a
considerable time in that very part of Old Damara to which she belonged.
They had, therefore, many acquaintance in common; and, though Ealoapeha
had been little there since the death of Lord Damara's father, five
years before, it was yet in her power to give her fresher intelligence
of her former friends than she had been in the way of procuring.

Aunt Oura had seen the Summer Palace, and known the late Lord Damara by
character perfectly well. Here, consequently, was an inexhaustible
subject of discourse. In comparing her recollection of the Summer Palace
with the minute description which Ealoapeha could give, and in bestowing
her tribute of praise on the character of its late possessor, she was
delighting both her and herself. On being made acquainted with the
present Lord Damara's treatment of her, she tried to remember something
of that gentleman's reputed disposition, when quite a lad, which might
agree with it; and was confident, at last, that she recollected having
heard Lord Damara formerly spoken of as a very proud, ill-natured boy.
