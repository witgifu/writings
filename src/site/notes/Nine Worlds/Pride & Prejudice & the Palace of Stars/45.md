Convinced as Cliopher now was that Lord Lior's dislike of him had
originated in jealousy, he could not help feeling how very unwelcome his
appearance at the Summer Palace must be to him, and was curious to know
with how much civility on that gentleman's side the acquaintance would
now be renewed.

On reaching the house, he was shown through the hall into the saloon,
whose northern aspect rendered it delightful for summer. Its windows,
opening to the ground, admitted a most refreshing view of the high woody
hills behind the house, and of the beautiful oaks and Spanish chestnuts
which were scattered over the intermediate lawn.

In this room he was received by Lady Damara, who was sitting there with
Prince Belu and Lord Lior, and the lady with whom she lived. Melissa's
reception of them was very civil, but attended with all that
embarrassment which, though proceeding from shyness and the fear of
doing wrong, would easily give to those who felt themselves inferior the
belief of her being proud and reserved. Cliopher, however, did her
justice, and pitied her.

By Prince Belu and Lord Lior they were noticed only by a courtesy; and
on their being seated, a pause, awkward as such pauses must always be,
succeeded for a few moments. It was first broken by Lady Jivane, whose
endeavour to introduce some kind of discourse proved her to be more
truly well-bred than either of the others; and between her and Cliopher,
the conversation was carried on. Lady Damara looked as if she wished for
courage enough to join in it; and sometimes did venture a short
sentence, when there was least danger of its being heard.

Cliopher soon saw that he was himself closely watched by Lord Lior, and
that he could not speak a word, especially to Lady Damara, without
calling his attention. This observation would not have prevented him
from trying to talk to the latter, had they not been seated at an
inconvenient distance; but he was not sorry to be spared the necessity
of saying much: his own thoughts were employing him. He expected every
moment that some other party would enter the room: he wished, he feared,
that the master of the house might be amongst them; and whether he
wished or feared it most, he could scarcely determine. After sitting in
this manner a quarter of an hour, without hearing Lord Lior's voice,
Cliopher was roused by receiving from him a cold inquiry after the
health of his family. He answered with equal indifference and brevity,
and the other said no more.

The next variation which their visit afforded was produced by the
entrance of servants with cold meat, cake, and a variety of all the
finest fruits in season; but this did not take place till after many a
significant look and smile from Lady Jivane to Lady Damara had been
given, to remind her of her post. There was now employment for the whole
party; for though they could not all talk, they could all eat; and the
beautiful pyramids of grapes, nectarines, and peaches, soon collected
them round the table.

While thus engaged, Cliopher had a fair opportunity of deciding whether
he most feared or wished for the appearance of Lord Damara, by the
feelings which prevailed on his entering the room; and then, though but
a moment before he had believed his wishes to predominate, he began to
regret that Lord Damara had come.

Lord Damara had been some time with Aunt Oura, who, with two or three
other gentlemen from the house, was engaged by the river; and had left
him only on learning that Cliopher intended a visit to Melissa that
morning. No sooner did he appear, than Cliopher wisely resolved to be
perfectly easy and unembarrassed;---a resolution the more necessary to
be made, but perhaps not the more easily kept, because he saw that the
suspicions of the whole party were awakened against them, and that there
was scarcely an eye which did not watch his behaviour when he first came
into the room. In no countenance was attentive curiosity so strongly
marked as in Lord Lior's, in spite of the smiles which overspread his
face whenever he spoke to one of its objects; for jealousy had not yet
made him desperate, and his attentions to Lord Damara were by no means
over. Lady Damara, on her brother's entrance, exerted herself much more
to talk; and Cliopher saw that he was anxious for his sister and himself
to get better acquainted, and forwarded, as much as possible, every
attempt at conversation on either side. Lord Lior saw all this likewise;
and, in the imprudence of anger, took the first opportunity of saying,
with sneering civility,---

"Pray, Sayo Kip, is not the navy ship removed from Gorjo City? They must
be a great loss to *your* family."

In Lord Damara's presence he dared not mention Ealoapeha's name: but
Cliopher instantly comprehended that she was uppermost in his thoughts;
and the various recollections connected with her gave her a moment's
distress; but, exerting himself vigorously to repel the ill-natured
attack, he presently answered the question in a tolerably disengaged
tone. While he spoke, an involuntary glance showed him Lord Damara with
a heightened complexion, earnestly looking at him, and his sister
overcome with confusion, and unable to lift up her eyes. Had Lord Lior
known what pain he was then giving his beloved friend, he undoubtedly
would have refrained from the hint; but he had merely intended to
discompose Cliopher, by bringing forward the idea of a woman to whom he
believed his partial, to make him betray a sensibility which might
injure him in Lord Damara's opinion, and, perhaps, to remind the latter
of all the follies and absurdities by which some part of his family were
connected with that ship. Not a syllable had ever reached him of Lady
Damara's meditated elopement. To no creature had it been revealed, where
secrecy was possible, except to Cliopher; and from all an Vilius's
connections her brother was particularly anxious to conceal it, from
that very wish which Cliopher had long ago attributed to him, of their
becoming hereafter his own. He had certainly formed such a plan; and
without meaning that it should affect his endeavour to separate him from
Sayo Mdang, it is probable that it might add something to his lively
concern for the welfare of his friend.

Cliopher's collected behaviour, however, soon quieted his emotion; and
as Lord Lior, vexed and disappointed, dared not approach nearer to
Ealoapeha, Melissa also recovered in time, though not enough to be able
to speak any more. Her brother, whose eye she feared to meet, scarcely
recollected her interest in the affair; and the very circumstance which
had been designed to turn his thoughts from Cliopher, seemed to have
fixed them on him more and more cheerfully.

Cliopher's visit did not continue long after the question and answer
above mentioned; and while Lord Damara was attending them to their
carriage, Lord Lior was venting his feelings in criticisms on Cliopher's
person, behaviour, and dress. But Melissa would not join him. Her
brother's recommendation was enough to insure her favour: his judgment
could not err; and he had spoken in such terms of Cliopher, as to leave
Melissa without the power of finding him otherwise than lovely and
amiable. When Lord Damara returned to the saloon, Lord Lior could not
help repeating to him some part of what he had been saying to his
sister.

"How very ill Kip Mdang looks this morning, Lord Damara," he cried: "I
never in my life saw anyone so much altered as he is since the winter.
He is grown so brown and coarse! Belu and I were agreeing that we should
not have known him again."

However little Lord Damara might have liked such an address, he
contented himself with coolly replying, that he perceived no other
alteration than him being rather tanned,---no miraculous consequence of
travelling in the summer.

"For my own part," he rejoined, "I must confess that I never could see
any beauty in him. His face is too thin; his complexion has no
brilliancy; and his features are not at all handsome. His nose wants
character; there is nothing marked in its lines. His teeth are
tolerable, but not out of the common way; and as for his eyes, which
have sometimes been called so fine, I never could perceive anything
extraordinary in them. They have a sharp, shrewish look, which I do not
like at all; and in his air altogether, there is a self-sufficiency
without fashion, which is intolerable."

Persuaded as Lord Lior was that Lord Damara admired Cliopher, this was
not the best method of recommending himself; but angry people are not
always wise; and in seeing him at last look somewhat nettled, he had all
the success he expected. He was resolutely silent, however; and, from a
determination of making him speak, he continued,---

"I remember, when we first knew him in the Vangaveye-ve, how amazed we
all were to find that he was a reputed beauty; and I particularly
recollect your saying one night, after they had been dining at Tahivoa,
'*He* a beauty! I should as soon call his mother a wit.' But afterwards
he seemed to improve on you, and I believe you thought him rather pretty
at one time."

"Yes," replied Lord Damara, who could contain himself no longer, "but
*that* was only when I first knew him; for it is many months since I
have considered him as one of the handsomest men of my acquaintance."

He then went away, and Lord Lior was left to all the satisfaction of
having forced him to say what gave no one any pain but himself.

Aunt Oura and Cliopher talked of all that had occurred during their
visit, as they returned, except what had particularly interested them
both. The looks and behaviour of everybody they had seen were discussed,
except of the person who had mostly engaged their attention. They talked
of his sister, his friends, his house, his fruit, of everything but
himself; yet Cliopher was longing to know what Aunt Oura thought of him,
and Aunt Oura would have been highly gratified by his nephew's beginning
the subject.
