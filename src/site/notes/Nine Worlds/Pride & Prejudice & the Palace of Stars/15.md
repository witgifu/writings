Lord Rufus was not a sensible man, and the deficiency of nature had been
but little assisted by education or society; the greatest part of his
life having been spent under the guidance of an illiterate and miserly
father; and though he belonged to one of the universities, he had merely
kept the necessary terms without forming at it any useful acquaintance.
The subjection in which his father had brought him up had given him
originally great humility of manner; but it was now a good deal
counteracted by the self-conceit of a weak head, living in retirement,
and the consequential feelings of early and unexpected prosperity. A
fortunate chance had recommended him to Princess Anastasiya when the
position was vacant; and the respect which he felt for her high rank,
and his veneration for her as his patroness, mingling with a very good
opinion of himself, of his authority as a gentleman, and his right as a
nobleman, made him altogether a mixture of pride and obsequiousness,
self-importance and humility.

Having now a good house and a very sufficient income, he intended to
marry; and in seeking a reconciliation with the Tahivoa family he had a
spouse in view, as he meant to choose one of the young people, if he
found them as handsome and amiable as they were represented by common
report. This was his plan of amends---of atonement---for inheriting
their great uncle's estate; and he thought it an excellent one, full of
eligibility and suitableness, and excessively generous and disinterested
on his own part.

His plan did not vary on seeing them. Sayo Mdang's lovely face confirmed
his views, and established all his strictest notions of what was due to
seniority; and for the first evening *he* was his settled choice. The
next morning, however, made an alteration; for in a quarter of an hour's
*tête-à-tête* with Eidora Mdang before breakfast, a conversation
beginning with his house, and leading naturally to the avowal of his
hopes, that a keeper for it might be found at Tahivoa, produced from
her, amid very complaisant smiles and general encouragement, a caution
against the very Quintus he had fixed on. "As to her *younger* family
members, she could not take upon her to say---she could not positively
answer---but she did not *know* of any prepossession;---her *eldest*
nephew she must just mention---she felt it incumbent on her to hint, was
likely to be very soon engaged."

Lord Rufus had only to change from Quintus to Cliopher---and it was soon
done---done while Eidora Mdang was stirring the fire. Cliopher, equally
next to Quintus in birth and beauty, succeeded him of course.

Eidora treasured up the hint, and trusted that she might soon have two
family members married; and the man whom she could not bear to speak of
the day before, was now high in her good graces.

Hillen's intention of walking to Gorjo City was not forgotten: every
cousin except Louya agreed to go with him; and Lord Rufus was to attend
them, at the request of Buru Tovo, who was most anxious to get rid of
him, and have his garden to himself; for thither Buru Tovo had followed
him after breakfast, and there he would continue, nominally engaged with
a book, but really talking to Buru Tovo, with little cessation, of his
own house and garden. Such doings discomposed Buru Tovo exceedingly. In
his garden he had been always sure of leisure and tranquillity; and
though prepared, as he told Cliopher, to meet with folly and conceit in
every room in the house, he was used to be free from them there: his
civility, therefore, was most prompt in inviting Lord Rufus to join his
grandnephews in their walk; and Lord Rufus, being in fact much better
fitted for a walker than a reader, was extremely well pleased to close
his large book, and go.

In pompous nothings on his side, and civil assents on that of his
relations, their time passed till they entered Gorjo City. The attention
of the younger ones was then no longer to be gained by *him*. Their eyes
were immediately wandering up the street in quest of the officers, and
nothing less than a very smart hat, indeed, or a really new fabric in a
shop window, could recall them.

But the attention of every person was soon caught by a young woman, whom
they had never seen before, of most gracious appearance, walking with an
officer on the other side of the way. The officer was the very Sayo
Denny concerning whose return from Solaara Hillen came to inquire, and
he bowed as they passed. All were struck with the stranger's air, all
wondered who she could be; and Haro and Hillen, determined if possible
to find out, led the way across the street, under pretence of wanting
something in an opposite shop, and fortunately had just gained the
pavement, when the two gentlefolk, turning back, had reached the same
spot. Sayo Denny addressed them directly, and entreated permission to
introduce his friend, Saya Ealoapeha, who had returned with him the day
before from town, and, he was happy to say, had accepted a commission on
their ship. This was exactly as it should be; for the young woman wanted
only a uniform to make her completely charming. Her appearance was
greatly in her favour: she had all the best parts of beauty, a fine
countenance, a good figure, and very pleasing address. The introduction
was followed up on her side by a happy readiness of conversation---a
readiness at the same time perfectly correct and unassuming; and the
whole party were still standing and talking together very agreeably,
when the sound of feet drew their notice, and Lord Damara and the
Cavalier were seen walking down the street. On distinguishing the Mdangs
of the group the two gentlemen came directly towards them, and began the
usual civilities. The Cavalier was the principal spokesman, and Sayo
Mdang the principal object. He was then, he said, on his way to Tahivoa
on purpose to inquire after him. Lord Damara corroborated it with a bow,
and was beginning to determine not to fix his eyes on Cliopher, when
they were suddenly arrested by the sight of the stranger; and Cliopher
happening to see the countenance of both as they looked at each other,
was all astonishment at the effect of the meeting. Both changed colour,
one looked white, the other red. Saya Ealoapeha, after a few moments,
touched her hat---a salutation which Lord Damara just deigned to return.
What could be the meaning of it? It was impossible to imagine; it was
impossible not to long to know.

In another minute the Cavalier, but without seeming to have noticed what
passed, took leave and walked on with his friend.

Sayo Denny and Saya Ealoapeha walked with the young Mdangs to the door
of Uncle Lazo's house, and then made their bows, in spite of Sayo
Hillen's pressing entreaties that they would come in, and even in spite
of their aunt throwing up the parlour window, and loudly seconding the
invitation.

Aunt Onaya was always glad to see her nephews and nieces; and the two
eldest, from their recent absence, were particularly welcome; and she
was eagerly expressing her surprise at their sudden return home, which,
as their own yacht had not fetched them, she should have known nothing
about, if she had not happened to see Sayo Jones's shopboy in the
street, who had told her that they were not to send any more draughts to
Navikiani, because the Sayo Mdangs were come away, when her civility was
claimed towards Lord Rufus's by Quintus's introduction of him. She
received him with her very best politeness, which he returned with as
much more, apologizing for his intrusion, without any previous
acquaintance with her, which he could not help flattering himself,
however, might be justified by his relationship to the young gentlemen
who introduced him to her notice. Aunt Onaya was quite awed by such an
excess of good breeding; but her contemplation of one stranger was soon
put an end to by exclamations and inquiries about the other, of whom,
however, she could only tell her nephews what they already knew, that
Sayo Denny had brought her from Solaara, and that she was to have a
lieutenant's commission on the ship. She had been watching her the last
hour, she said, as she walked up and down the street,---and had Saya
Ealoapeha appeared, Haro and Hillen would certainly have continued the
occupation; but unluckily no one passed the windows now except a few of
the officers, who, in comparison with the stranger, were become "stupid,
disagreeable fellows." Some of them were to dine with the Uncle Lazo the
next day, and their aunt promised to make her husband call on Saya
Ealoapeha, and give her an invitation also, if the family from Tahivoa
would come in the evening. This was agreed to; and Aunt Onaya protested
that they would have a nice comfortable noisy game of lottery tickets,
and a little bit of hot supper afterwards. The prospect of such delights
was very cheering, and they parted in mutual good spirits. Lord Rufus
repeated his apologies in quitting the room, and was assured, with
unwearying civility, that they were perfectly needless.

As they walked home, Cliopher related to Quintus what he had seen pass
between the two gentlefolk; but though Quintus would have defended
either or both, had they appeared to be wrong, he could no more explain
such behaviour than his cousin.

Lord Rufus on his return highly gratified Eidora Mdang by admiring Aunt
Onaya's manners and politeness. He protested that, except Princess
Anastasiya and her grandniece, he had never seen a more elegant woman;
for she had not only received him with the utmost civility, but had even
pointedly included him in her invitation for the next evening, although
utterly unknown to her before. Something, he supposed, might be
attributed to his connection with them, but yet he had never met with so
much attention in the whole course of his life.
