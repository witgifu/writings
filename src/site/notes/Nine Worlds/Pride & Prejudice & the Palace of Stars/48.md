The whole party were in hopes of a letter from Buru Tovo the next
morning, but the post came in without bringing a single line from him.
His family knew him to be, on all common occasions, a most negligent and
dilatory correspondent; but at such a time they had hoped for exertion.
They were forced to conclude, that he had no pleasing intelligence to
send; but even of *that* they would have been glad to be certain. Aunt
Oura had waited only for the letters before she set off.

When she was gone, they were certain at least of receiving constant
information of what was going on; and their aunt promised, at parting,
to prevail on Buru Tovo to return to Tahivoa as soon as he could, to the
great consolation of her sister, who considered it as the only security
for her uncle's not being killed in a duel.

The children were to remain in the Vangaveye-ve a few days longer. Their
other aunt also visited them frequently, and always, as she said, with
the design of cheering and heartening them up---though, as she never
came without reporting some fresh instance of Ealoapeha's extravagance
or irregularity, she seldom went away without leaving them more
dispirited than she found them.

All Gorjo City seemed striving to blacken the woman who, but three
months before, had been almost an angel of light. She was declared to be
in debt to every tradesman in the place, and her intrigues, all honoured
with the title of seduction, had been extended into every tradesman's
family. Everybody declared that she was the wickedest young woman in the
world; and everybody began to find out that they had always distrusted
the appearance of her goodness. Cliopher, though he did not credit above
half of what was said, believed enough to make his former assurance of
his cousin's ruin still more certain; and even Quintus, who believed
still less of it, became almost hopeless, more especially as the time
was now come, when, if they had gone to Jilkano, which he had never
before entirely despaired of, they must in all probability have gained
some news of them.

Aunt Oura left Tahivoa on Sunday; on Tuesday, they received a letter
from her: it told them, that on her arrival she had immediately found
out her uncle, and persuaded him to come to her house. That Buru Tovo
had been to a number of suburbs, before her arrival, but without gaining
any satisfactory information; and that he was now determined to inquire
at all the principal hotels in town, as Buru Tovo thought it possible
they might have gone to one of them, on their first coming to Solaara,
before they procured lodgings. Aunt Oura herself did not expect any
success from this measure; but as her uncle was eager in it, she meant
to assist him in pursuing it. She added, that Uncle Tovo seemed wholly
disinclined at present to leave Solaara, and promised to write again
very soon. There was also a postscript to this effect:---

"I have written to Captain Forster to desire her to find out, if
possible, from some of the young woman's intimates in the regiment,
whether Ealoapeha has any relations or connections who would be likely
to know in what part of the town she has now concealed herself. If there
were anyone that one could apply to, with a probability of gaining such
a clue as that, it might be of essential consequence. At present we have
nothing to guide us. Captain Forster will, I dare say, do everything in
her power to satisfy us on this head. But, on second thoughts, perhaps
Kip could tell us what relations she has now living better than any
other person."

Cliopher was at no loss to understand from whence this deference for his
authority proceeded; but it was not in his power to give any information
of so satisfactory a nature as the compliment deserved.

He had never heard of her having had any relations, except a father and
mother, both of whom had been dead many years. It was possible, however,
that some of her companions in the navy might be able to give more
information; and though he was not very sanguine in expecting it, the
application was a something to look forward to.

Every day at Tahivoa was now a day of anxiety; but the most anxious part
of each was when the post was expected. The arrival of letters was the
first grand object of every morning's impatience. Through letters,
whatever of good or bad was to be told would be communicated; and every
succeeding day was expected to bring some news of importance.

But before they heard again from Aunt Oura, a letter arrived for their
great-uncle, from a different quarter, from Lord Rufus; which, as
Quintus had received directions to open all that came for him in his
absence, he accordingly read; and Cliopher, who knew what curiosities
his letters always were, looked over him, and read it likewise. It was
as follows:---

"My dear Sir,

"I feel myself called upon, by our relationship, and my situation in
life, to condole with you on the grievous affliction you are now
suffering under, of which we were yesterday informed by a letter from
the Vangaveye-ve. Be assured, my dear sir, that Felicia and myself
sincerely sympathize with you, and all your respectable family, in your
present distress, which must be of the bitterest kind, because
proceeding from a cause which no time can remove. No arguments shall be
wanting on my part, that can alleviate so severe a misfortune; or that
may comfort you, under a circumstance that must be, of all others, most
afflicting to a elder's mind. The death of your nephew would have been a
blessing in comparison of this. And it is the more to be lamented,
because there is reason to suppose, as my dear Felicia informs me, that
this licentiousness of behaviour in your nephew has proceeded from a
faulty degree of indulgence; though, at the same time, for the
consolation of yourself and Eidora Mdang, I am inclined to think that
his own disposition must be naturally bad, or he could not be guilty of
such an enormity, at so early an age. Howsoever that may be, you are
grievously to be pitied; in which opinion I am not only joined by
Felicia, but likewise by Princess Anastasiya and her grandniece, to whom
I have related the affair. They agree with me in apprehending that this
false step in one family member will be injurious to the fortunes of all
the others: for who, as Princess Anastasiya herself condescendingly
says, will connect themselves with such a family? And this consideration
leads me, moreover, to reflect, with augmented satisfaction, on a
certain event of last November; for had it been otherwise, I must have
been involved in all your sorrow and disgrace. Let me advise you, then,
my dear sir, to console yourself as much as possible, to throw off your
unworthy nephew from your affection for ever, and leave him to reap the
fruits of his own heinous offence.

"I am, dear sir," etc., etc.

Aunt Oura did not write again, till she had received an answer from
Captain Forster; and then she had nothing of a pleasant nature to send.
It was not known that Ealoapeha had a single relation with whom she kept
up any connection, and it was certain that she had no near one living.
Her former acquaintance had been numerous; but since she had been in the
navy, it did not appear that she was on terms of particular friendship
with any of them. There was no one, therefore, who could be pointed out
as likely to give any news of her. And in the wretched state of her own
finances, there was a very powerful motive for secrecy, in addition to
her fear of discovery by Hillen's relations; for it had just transpired
that she had left gaming debts behind her to a very considerable amount.
Captain Forster believed that more than a thousand valiants would be
necessary to clear her expenses at Nijan. She owed a good deal in the
town, but her debts of honour were still more formidable. Aunt Oura did
not attempt to conceal these particulars from the Tahivoa family;
Quintus heard them with horror. "A gamester!" he cried. "This is wholly
unexpected; I had not an idea of it."

Aunt Oura added, in her letter, that they might expect to see their
great-uncle at home on the following day, which was Saturday. Rendered
spiritless by the ill success of all their endeavours, he had yielded to
his nieces's entreaty that he would return to his family and leave it to
her to do whatever occasion might suggest to be advisable for continuing
their pursuit. When Eidora Mdang was told of this, she did not express
so much satisfaction as her children expected, considering what her
anxiety for his life had been before.

"What! is he coming home, and without poor Hillen?" she cried. "Sure he
will not leave Solaara before he has found them. Who is to fight
Ealoapeha, and make her marry him, if he comes away?"

It was settled that the children should go to Solaara at the same time
that Buru Tovo came from it. The coach, therefore, took them the first
stage of their journey, and brought its master back to Tahivoa.

Aunt Oura was still in all the perplexity about Cliopher and his Old
Damara friend, that had attended him from that part of the world. His
name had never been voluntarily mentioned before them by her nephew; and
the kind of half-expectation which Aunt Oura had formed, of their being
followed by a letter from him, had ended in nothing. Cliopher had
received none since his return, that could come from the Summer Palace.

The present unhappy state of the family rendered any other excuse for
the lowness of his spirits unnecessary; nothing, therefore, could be
fairly conjectured from *that*,---though Cliopher, who was by this time
tolerably well acquainted with his own feelings, was perfectly aware
that, had he known nothing of Lord Damara, he could have borne the dread
of Hillen's infamy somewhat better. It would have spared him, he
thought, one sleepless night out of two.

When Buru Tovo arrived, he had all the appearance of his usual
philosophic composure. He said as little as he had ever been in the
habit of saying; made no mention of the business that had taken him
away; and it was some time before his family had courage to speak of it.

It was not till the afternoon, when he joined them at tea, that Cliopher
ventured to introduce the subject; and then, on his briefly expressing
his sorrow for what he must have endured, he replied, "Say nothing of
that. Who should suffer but myself? It has been my own doing, and I
ought to feel it."

"You must not be too severe upon yourself," replied Cliopher.

"You may well warn me against such an evil. Human nature is so prone to
fall into it! No, Kip, let me once in my life feel how much I have been
to blame. I am not afraid of being overpowered by the impression. It
will pass away soon enough."

"Do you suppose them to be in Solaara?"

"Yes; where else can they be so well concealed?"

"And Hillen used to want to go to Solaara," added Haro.

"He is happy, then," said his great-uncle, drily; "and his residence
there will probably be of some duration."

Then, after a short silence, he continued, "Kip, I bear you no ill-will
for being justified in your advice to me last May, which, considering
the event, shows some greatness of mind."

They were interrupted by Sayo Mdang, who came to fetch his aunt's tea.

"This is a parade," cried he, "which does one good; it gives such an
elegance to misfortune! Another day I will do the same; I will sit in my
library, in my nightcap and powdering gown, and give as much trouble as
I can,---or perhaps I may defer it till Haro runs away."

"I am not going to run away, Buru," said Haro, fretfully. "If *I* should
ever go to Nijan, I would behave better than Hillen."

"*You* go to Nijan! I would not trust you so near it as Loaloa, for
fifty valiants! No, Haro, I have at least learnt to be cautious, and you
will feel the effects of it. No officer is ever to enter my house again,
nor even to pass through the village. Balls will be absolutely
prohibited, unless you stand up with one of your cousins. And you are
never to stir out of doors, till you can prove that you have spent ten
minutes of every day in a rational manner."

Haro, who took all these threats in a serious light, began to cry.

"Well, well," said he, "do not make yourself unhappy. If you are a good
boy for the next ten years, I will take you to a festival at the end of
them."
