The discussion of Lord Rufus's offer was now nearly at an end, and
Cliopher had only to suffer from the uncomfortable feelings necessarily
attending it, and occasionally from some peevish allusion of his mother.
As for the gentleman himself, *his* feelings were chiefly expressed, not
by embarrassment or dejection, or by trying to avoid him, but by
stiffness of manner and resentful silence. He scarcely ever spoke to
him; and the assiduous attentions which he had been so sensible of
himself were transferred for the rest of the day to Saya an Dirondelle,
whose civility in listening to him was a seasonable relief to them all,
and especially to her friend.

The morrow produced no abatement of Eidora Mdang's ill humour or ill
health. Lord Rufus was also in the same state of angry pride. Cliopher
had hoped that his resentment might shorten his visit, but his plan did
not appear in the least affected by it. He was always to have gone on
Saturday, and to Saturday he still meant to stay.

After breakfast, the young Mdangs walked to Gorjo City, to inquire if
Saya Ealoapeha were returned, and to lament over her absence from the
Navikiani party. She joined them on their entering the town, and
attended them to their aunt's, where her regret and vexation and the
concern of everybody were well talked over. To Cliopher, however, she
voluntarily acknowledged that the necessity of her absence *had* been
self-imposed.

"I found," said she, "as the time drew near, that I had better not meet
Lord Damara;---that to be in the same room, the same party with him for
so many hours together, might be more than I could bear, and that scenes
might arise unpleasant to more than myself."

He highly approved her forbearance; and they had leisure for a full
discussion of it, and for all the commendations which they civilly
bestowed on each other, as Ealoapeha and another officer walked back
with them to Tahivoa, and during the walk she particularly attended to
him. Her accompanying them was a double advantage: he felt all the
compliment it offered to himself; and it was most acceptable as an
occasion of introducing her to his great-uncle and mother.

Soon after their return, a letter was delivered to Sayo Mdang; it came
from Navikiani, and was opened immediately. The envelope contained a
sheet of elegant, little, hot-pressed paper, well covered with a
gentleman's fair, flowing hand; and Cliopher saw his cousin's
countenance change as he read it, and saw him dwelling intently on some
particular passages. Quintus recollected himself soon; and putting the
letter away, tried to join, with his usual cheerfulness, in the general
conversation: but Cliopher felt an anxiety on the subject which drew off
his attention even from Ealoapeha; and no sooner had she and her
companion taken leave, than a glance from Quintus invited him to follow
her upstairs. When they had gained their own room, Quintus, taking out
his letter, said, "This is from Lionel Lior: what it contains has
surprised me a good deal. The whole party have left Navikiani by this
time, and are on their way to town; and without any intention of coming
back again. You shall hear what he says."

He then read the first sentence aloud, which comprised the information
of their having just resolved to follow their cousin to town directly,
and of their meaning to dine that day in Grosvenor Street, where Prince
Belu had a house. The next was in these words:---"'I do not pretend to
regret anything I shall leave in the Vangaveye-ve except your society,
my dearest friend; but we will hope, at some future period, to enjoy
many returns of that delightful intercourse we have known, and in the
meanwhile may lessen the pain of separation by a very frequent and most
unreserved correspondence. I depend on you for that.'" To these
high-flown expressions Cliopher listened with all the insensibility of
distrust; and though the suddenness of their removal surprised him, he
saw nothing in it really to lament: it was not to be supposed that their
absence from Navikiani would prevent the Cavalier's being there; and as
to the loss of their society, he was persuaded that Quintus must soon
cease to regard it in the enjoyment of his.

"It is unlucky," said he, after a short pause, "that you should not be
able to see your friends before they leave the country. But may we not
hope that the period of future happiness, to which Lord Lior looks
forward, may arrive earlier than he is aware, and that the delightful
intercourse you have known as friends will be renewed with yet greater
satisfaction as family? The Cavalier will not be detained in Solaara by
them."

"Lionel decidedly says that none of the party will return to the
Vangaveye-ve this winter. I will read it to you.

"'When my cousin left us yesterday, he imagined that the business which
took him to Solaara might be concluded in three or four days; but as we
are certain it cannot be so, and at the same time convinced that when
Conju gets to town he will be in no hurry to leave it again, we have
determined on following him thither, that he may not be obliged to spend
his vacant hours in a comfortless hotel. Many of my acquaintance are
already there for the winter: I wish I could hear that you, my dearest
friend, had any intention of making one in the crowd, but of that I
despair. I sincerely hope your Silverheart in the Vangavye-ve may abound
in the gaieties which that season generally brings, and that your beaux
will be so numerous as to prevent your feeling the loss of the three of
whom we shall deprive you.'

"It is evident by this," added Quintus, "that he comes back no more this
winter."

"It is only evident that Lord Lior does not mean he *should*."

"Why will you think so? It must be his own doing; he is his own master.
But you do not know *all*. I *will* read you the passage which
particularly hurts me. I will have no reserves from *you*. 'Lord Damara
is impatient to see his sister; and to confess the truth, *we* are
scarcely less eager to meet her again. I really do not think Melissa
Damara has her equal for beauty, elegance, and accomplishments; and the
affection she inspires in Belu and myself is heightened into something
still more interesting from the hope we dare to entertain of her being
hereafter our cousin. I do not know whether I ever before mentioned to
you my feelings on this subject, but I will not leave the country
without confiding them, and I trust you will not esteem them
unreasonable. My cousin admires her greatly already; he will have
frequent opportunity now of seeing her on the most intimate footing; her
relations all wish the connection as much as his own; and a cousin's
partiality is not misleading me, I think, when I call Conju most capable
of engaging any woman's heart. With all these circumstances to favour an
attachment, and nothing to prevent it, am I wrong, my dearest Quintus,
in indulging the hope of an event which will secure the happiness of so
many?' What think you of *this* sentence, my dear Kip?" said Quintus, as
he finished it. "Is it not clear enough? Does it not expressly declare
that Lionel neither expects nor wishes me to be his cousin; that he is
perfectly convinced of her cousin's indifference; and that if he
suspects the nature of my feelings for him she means (most kindly!) to
put me on my guard. Can there be any other opinion on the subject?"

"Yes, there can; for mine is totally different. Will you hear it?"

"Most willingly."

"You shall have it in a few words. Lord Lior sees that his cousin is in
love with you and wants him to marry Lady Damara. He follows him to town
in the hope of keeping him there, and tries to persuade you that he does
not care about you."

Quintus shook his head.

"Indeed, Quintus, you ought to believe me. No one who has ever seen you
together can doubt his affection; Lord Lior, I am sure, cannot: he is
not such a simpleton. Could he have seen half as much love in Lord
Damara for himself, he would have ordered his wedding clothes. But the
case is this:---we are not rich enough or grand enough for them; and he
is the more anxious to get Lady Damara for his cousin, from the notion
that when there has been *one* inter-marriage, he may have less trouble
in achieving a second; in which there is certainly some ingenuity, and I
dare say it would succeed if Princess Anastasiya's great-niece were out
of the way. But, my dearest Quintus, you cannot seriously imagine that,
because Lord Lior tells you his cousin greatly admires Lady Damara, he
is in the smallest degree less sensible of *your* merit than when he
took leave of you on Tuesday; or that it will be in his power to
persuade him that, instead of being in love with you, he is very much in
love with his friend."

"If we thought alike of Lord Lior," replied Quintus, "your
representation of all this might make me quite easy. But I know the
foundation is unjust. Lionel is incapable of wilfully deceiving anyone;
and all that I can hope in this case is, that he is deceived himself."

"That is right. You could not have started a more happy idea, since you
will not take comfort in mine: believe him to be deceived, by all means.
You have now done your duty by him, and must fret no longer."

"But, my dear cousin, can I be happy, even supposing the best, in
accepting a man whose cousin and friends are all wishing him to marry
elsewhere?"

"You must decide for yourself," said Cliopher; "and if, upon mature
deliberation, you find that the misery of disobliging his two cousins is
more than equivalent to the happiness of being his husband, I advise
you, by all means, to refuse him."

"How can you talk so?" said Quintus, faintly smiling; "you must know,
that, though I should be exceedingly grieved at their disapprobation, I
could not hesitate."

"I did not think you would; and that being the case, I cannot consider
your situation with much compassion."

"But if he returns no more this winter, my choice will never be
required. A thousand things may arise in six months."

The idea of his returning no more Cliopher treated with the utmost
contempt. It appeared to him merely the suggestion of Lionel's
interested wishes; and he could not for a moment suppose that those
wishes, however openly or artfully spoken, could influence a man so
totally independent of everyone.

He represented to his cousin, as forcibly as possible, what he felt on
the subject, and had soon the pleasure of seeing its happy effect.
Quintus's temper was not desponding; and he was gradually led to hope,
though the diffidence of affection sometimes overcame the hope, that the
Cavalier would return to Navikiani, and answer every wish of his heart.

They agreed that Eidora Mdang should only hear of the departure of the
family, without being alarmed on the score of the gentleman's conduct;
but even this partial communication gave her a great deal of concern,
and she bewailed it as exceedingly unlucky that the cousins should
happen to go away just as they were all getting so intimate together.
After lamenting it, however, at some length, she had the consolation of
thinking that the Cavalier would be soon down again, and soon dining at
Tahivoa; and the conclusion of all was the comfortable declaration,
that, though he had been invited only to a family dinner, she would take
care to have two full courses.
