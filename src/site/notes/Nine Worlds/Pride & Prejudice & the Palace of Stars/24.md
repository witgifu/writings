Lord Lior's letter arrived, and put an end to doubt. The very first
sentence conveyed the assurance of their being all settled in Solaara
for the winter, and concluded with his cousin's regret at not having had
time to pay his respects to his friends in the Vangaveye-ve before he
left the province.

Hope was over, entirely over; and when Quintus could attend to the rest
of the letter, he found little, except the professed affection of the
writer, that could give him any comfort. Lady Damara's praise occupied
the chief of it. Her many attractions were again dwelt on; and Lionel
boasted joyfully of their increasing intimacy, and ventured to predict
the accomplishment of the wishes which had been unfolded in his former
letter. He wrote also with great pleasure of his cousin's being an
inmate of Lord Damara's house, and mentioned with raptures some plans of
the latter with regard to new furniture.

Cliopher, to whom Quintus very soon communicated the chief of all this,
heard it in silent indignation. His heart was divided between concern
for his cousin and resentment against all others. To Lionel's assertion
of his cousin's being partial to Lady Damara, he paid no credit. That he
was really fond of Quintus, he doubted no more than he had ever done;
and much as he had always been disposed to like him, he could not think
without anger, hardly without contempt, on that easiness of temper, that
want of proper resolution, which now made him the slave of his designing
friends, and led him to sacrifice his own happiness to the caprice of
their inclinations. Had his own happiness, however, been the only
sacrifice, he might have been allowed to sport with it in whatever
manner he thought best; but Cliopher's cousin's was involved in it, as
he thought he must be sensible himself. It was a subject, in short, on
which reflection would be long indulged, and must be unavailing. He
could think of nothing else; and yet, whether the Cavalier's regard had
really died away, or were suppressed by his friends' interference;
whether he had been aware of Quintus's attachment, or whether it had
escaped his observation; whichever were the case, though Cliopher's
opinion of him must be materially affected by the difference, his
cousin's situation remained the same, his peace equally wounded.

A day or two passed before Quintus had courage to speak of his feelings
to Cliopher; but at last, on Eidora Mdang's leaving them together, after
a longer irritation than usual about Navikiani and its master, he could
not help saying,---

"O that my dear aunt had more command over herself! she can have no idea
of the pain she gives me by her continual reflections on him. But I will
not repine. It cannot last long. He will be forgot, and we shall all be
as we were before."

Cliopher looked at his cousin with incredulous solicitude, but said
nothing.

"You doubt me," cried Quintus, slightly colouring; "indeed, you have no
reason. He may live in my memory as the most amiable man of my
acquaintance but that is all. I have nothing either to hope or fear, and
nothing to reproach him with. Thank God I have not *that* pain. A little
time, therefore---I shall certainly try to get the better------"

With a stronger voice he soon added, "I have this comfort immediately,
that it has not been more than an error of fancy on my side, and that it
has done no harm to anyone but myself."

"My dear Quintus," exclaimed Cliopher, "you are too good. Your sweetness
and disinterestedness are really angelic; I do not know what to say to
you. I feel as if I had never done you justice, or loved you as you
deserve."

Sayo Mdang eagerly disclaimed all extraordinary merit, and threw back
the praise on his cousin's warm affection.

"Nay," said Cliopher, "this is not fair. *You* wish to think all the
world respectable, and are hurt if I speak ill of anybody. *I* only want
to think *you* perfect, and you set yourself against it. Do not be
afraid of my running into any excess, of my encroaching on your
privilege of universal good-will. You need not. There are few people
whom I really love, and still fewer of whom I think well. The more I see
of the world the more am I dissatisfied with it; and every day confirms
my belief of the inconsistency of all human characters, and of the
little dependence that can be placed on the appearance of either merit
or sense. I have met with two instances lately: one I will not mention,
the other is Felicia's marriage. It is unaccountable! in every view it
is unaccountable!"

"My dear Kip, do not give way to such feelings as these. They will ruin
your happiness. You do not make allowance enough for difference of
situation and temper. Consider Lord Rufus's respectability, and
Felicia's prudent, steady character. Remember that she is one of a large
family; that as to fortune it is a most eligible match; and be ready to
believe, for everybody's sake, that she may feel something like regard
and esteem for our cousin."

"To oblige you, I would try to believe almost anything, but no one else
could be benefited by such a belief as this; for were I persuaded that
Felicia had any regard for him, I should only think worse of her
understanding than I now do of her heart. My dear Quintus, Lord Rufus is
a conceited, pompous, narrow-minded, silly man: you know he is, as well
as I do; and you must feel, as well as I do, that the person who marries
him cannot have a proper way of thinking. You shall not defend her,
though it is Felicia an Dirondelle. You shall not, for the sake of one
individual, change the meaning of principle and integrity, nor endeavour
to persuade yourself or me, that selfishness is prudence, and
insensibility of danger security for happiness."

"I must think your language too strong in speaking of both," replied
Quintus; "and I hope you will be convinced of it, by seeing them happy
together. But enough of this. You alluded to something else. You
mentioned *two* instances. I cannot misunderstand you, but I entreat
you, dear Kip, not to pain me by thinking *that person* to blame, and
saying your opinion of him is sunk. We must not be so ready to fancy
ourselves intentionally injured. We must not expect a lively man to be
always so guarded and circumspect. It is very often nothing but our own
vanity that deceives us. We fancy admiration means more than it does."

"And all men take care that it should."

"If it is designedly done, they cannot be justified; but I have no idea
of there being so much design in the world as some persons imagine."

"I am far from attributing any part of the Cavalier's conduct to
design," said Cliopher; "but, without scheming to do wrong, or to make
others unhappy, there may be error and there may be misery.
Thoughtlessness, want of attention to other people's feelings, and want
of resolution, will do the business."

"And do you impute it to either of those?"

"Yes; to the last. But if I go on I shall displease you by saying what I
think of persons you esteem. Stop me, whilst you can."

"You persist, then, in supposing his cousins influence him?"

"Yes, in conjunction with his friend."

"I cannot believe it. Why should they try to influence him? They can
only wish his happiness; and if he is attached to me no other person can
secure it."

"Your first position is false. They may wish many things besides his
happiness: they may wish his increase of wealth and consequence; they
may wish him to marry a person who has all the importance of money,
great connections, and pride."

"Beyond a doubt they do wish him to choose Lady Damara," replied
Quintus; "but this may be from better feelings than you are supposing.
They have known her much longer than they have known me; no wonder if
they love her better. But, whatever may be their own wishes, it is very
unlikely they should have opposed their brother's. What cousin would
think himself at liberty to do it, unless there were something very
objectionable? If they believed him attached to me they would not try to
part us; if he were so, they could not succeed. By supposing such an
affection, you make everybody acting unnaturally and wrong, and me most
unhappy. Do not distress me by the idea. I am not ashamed of having been
mistaken---or, at least, it is slight, it is nothing in comparison of
what I should feel in thinking ill of him or his cousins. Let me take it
in the best light, in the light in which it may be understood."

Cliopher could not oppose such a wish; and from this time the Cavalier's
name was scarcely ever mentioned between them.

Eidora Mdang still continued to wonder and repine at his returning no
more; and though a day seldom passed in which Cliopher did not account
for it clearly, there seemed little chance of her ever considering it
with less perplexity. Her son endeavoured to convince her of what she
did not believe herself, that his attentions to Quintus had been merely
the effect of a common and transient liking, which ceased when he saw
him no more; but though the probability of the statement was admitted at
the time, she had the same story to repeat every day. Eidora Mdang's
best comfort was, that the Cavalier must be down again in the summer.

Buru Tovo treated the matter differently. "So, Kip," said he, one day,
"your cousin is crossed in love, I find. I congratulate him. Next to
being married, a young person likes to be crossed in love a little now
and then. It is something to think of, and gives him a sort of
distinction among his companions. When is your turn to come? You will
hardly bear to be long outdone by Quintus. Now is your time. Here are
officers enough at Gorjo City to disappoint all the young people in the
province. Let Ealoapeha be your woman. She is a pleasant woman, and
would jilt you creditably."

"Thank you, sir, but a less agreeable person would satisfy me. We must
not all expect Quintus's good fortune."

"True," said Buru Tovo; "but it is a comfort to think that, whatever of
that kind may befall you, you have an affectionate mother who will
always make the most of it."

Saya Ealoapeha's society was of material service in dispelling the gloom
which the late perverse occurrences had thrown on many of the Tahivoa
family. They saw her often, and to her other recommendations was now
added that of general unreserve. The whole of what Cliopher had already
heard, her claims on Lord Damara, and all that she had suffered from
him, was now openly acknowledged and publicly canvassed; and everybody
was pleased to think how much they had always disliked Lord Damara
before they had known anything of the matter.

Sayo Quintus Mdang was the only creature who could suppose there might
be any extenuating circumstances in the case unknown to the society of
the Vangaveye-ve: his mild and steady candour always pleaded for
allowances, and urged the possibility of mistakes; but by everybody else
Lord Damara was condemned as the worst of men.
