Buru Tovo was among the earliest of those who waited on the Cavalier. He
had always intended to visit him, though to the last always assuring his
niece that he should not go; and till the evening after the visit was
paid she had no knowledge of it. It was then disclosed in the following
manner. Observing his nephew employed in trimming a headdress, he
suddenly addressed him with,---

"I hope the Cavalier will like it, Kip."

"We are not in a way to know *what* the Cavalier an Vilius likes," said
his mother, resentfully, "since we are not to visit."

"But you forget, mamma," said Cliopher, "that we shall meet him at the
assemblies, and that Saya Long has promised to introduce him."

"I do not believe Saya Long will do any such thing. She has two nieces
of her own. She is a selfish, hypocritical woman, and I have no opinion
of her."

"No more have I," said Buru Tovo; "and I am glad to find that you do not
depend on her serving you."

Eidora Mdang deigned not to make any reply; but, unable to contain
herself, began scolding one of her nephews.

"Don't keep coughing so, Haro, for heaven's sake! Have a little
compassion on my nerves. You tear them to pieces."

"Haro has no discretion in his coughs," said his great-uncle; "he times
them ill."

"I do not cough for my own amusement," replied Haro, fretfully. "When is
your next ball to be, Kip?"

"To-morrow fortnight."

"Ay, so it is," cried his mother, "and Sayo Long does not come back till
the day before; so, it will be impossible for her to introduce him, for
she will not know him herself."

"Then, my dear, you may have the advantage of your friend, and introduce
the Cavalier to *her*."

"Impossible, Uncle Tovo, impossible, when I am not acquainted with him
myself; how can you be so teasing?"

"I honour your circumspection. A fortnight's acquaintance is certainly
very little. One cannot know what a man really is by the end of a
fortnight. But if *we* do not venture, somebody else will; and after
all, Saya Long and her nieces must stand their chance; and, therefore,
as she will think it an act of kindness, if you decline the office, I
will take it on myself."

The youngsters stared at their great-uncle. Eidora said only, "Nonsense,
nonsense!"

"What can be the meaning of that emphatic exclamation?" cried he. "Do
you consider the forms of introduction, and the stress that is laid on
them, as nonsense? I cannot quite agree with you *there*. What say you,
Louya? For you are a young lady of deep reflection, I know, and read
great books, and make extracts."

Louya wished to say something very sensible, but knew not how.

"While Louya is adjusting her ideas," he continued, "let us return to
the Cavalier."

"I am sick of the Cavalier an Vilius," cried his niece.

"I am sorry to hear *that*; but why did you not tell me so before? If I
had known as much this morning, I certainly would not have called on
him. It is very unlucky; but as I have actually paid the visit, we
cannot escape the acquaintance now."

The astonishment of the others was just what he wished---that of Eidora
perhaps surpassing the rest; though when the first tumult of joy was
over, she began to declare that it was what she had expected all the
while.

"How good it was in you, my dear Uncle Tovo! But I knew I should
persuade you at last. I was sure you loved your family too well to
neglect such an acquaintance. Well, how pleased I am! And it is such a
good joke, too, that you should have gone this morning, and never said a
word about it till now."

"Now, Haro, you may cough as much as you choose," said Buru Tovo; and,
as he spoke, he left the room, fatigued with the raptures of his niece.

"What an excellent great-uncle you have," said she, when the door was
shut. "I do not know how you will ever make him amends for his kindness;
or me either, for that matter. At our time of life, it is not so
pleasant, I can tell you, to be making new acquaintances every day; but
for your sakes we would do anything. Hillen, my love, though you *are*
the youngest, I dare say an Vilius will dance with you at the next
ball."

"Oh," said Hillen, stoutly, "I am not afraid; for though I *am* the
youngest, I'm the tallest."

The rest of the evening was spent in conjecturing how soon he would
return the Cavalier's visit, and determining when they should ask him to
dinner.
