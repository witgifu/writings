On Saturday morning Cliopher and Lord Rufus met for breakfast a few
minutes before the others appeared; and Rufus took the opportunity of
paying the parting civilities which he deemed indispensably necessary.

"I know not, Sayo Cliopher," said he, "whether Felicia has yet expressed
her sense of your kindness in coming to us; but I am very certain you
will not leave the house without receiving her thanks for it. The favour
of your company has been much felt, I assure you. We know how little
there is to tempt anyone to our humble abode. Our plain manner of
living, our small rooms, and few domestics, and the little we see of the
world, must make our home extremely dull to a young man like yourself;
but I hope you will believe us grateful for the condescension, and that
we have done everything in our power to prevent you spending your time
unpleasantly."

Cliopher was eager with his thanks and assurances of happiness. He had
spent six weeks with great enjoyment; and the pleasure of being with
Felicia, and the kind attention he had received, must make *him* feel
the obliged. Lord Rufus was gratified; and with a more smiling solemnity
replied,---

"It gives me the greatest pleasure to hear that you have passed your
time not disagreeably. We have certainly done our best; and most
fortunately having it in our power to introduce you to very superior
society, and from our connection with Yra Palace, the frequent means of
varying the humble home scene, I think we may flatter ourselves that
your visit cannot have been entirely irksome. Our situation with regard
to Princess Anastasiya's family is, indeed, the sort of extraordinary
advantage and blessing which few can boast. You see on what a footing we
are. You see how continually we are engaged there. In truth, I must
acknowledge, that, with all the disadvantages of this humble position, I
should not think anyone having it an object of compassion, while they
are sharers of our intimacy at Yra Palace."

Words were insufficient for the elevation of his feelings; and he was
obliged to walk about the room, while Cliopher tried to unite civility
and truth in a few short sentences.

"You may, in fact, carry a very favourable report of us into the
Vangaveye-ve, my dear cousin. I flatter myself, at least, that you will
be able to do so. Princess Anastasiya's great attentions to Felicia you
have been a daily witness of; and altogether I trust it does not appear
that your friend has drawn an unfortunate---but on this point it will be
as well to be silent. Only let me assure you, my dear Sayo Cliopher,
that I can from my heart most cordially wish you equal felicity in
marriage. My dear Felicia and I have but one mind and one way of
thinking. There is in everything a most remarkable resemblance of
character and ideas between us. We seem to have been designed for each
other."

Cliopher could safely say that it was a great happiness where that was
the case, and with equal sincerity could add, that he firmly believed
and rejoiced in his domestic comforts. He was not sorry, however, to
have the recital of them interrupted by the entrance of the lady from
whom they sprang. Poor Felicia! it was melancholy to leave her to such
society! But she had chosen it with her eyes open; and though evidently
regretting that her visitors were to go, she did not seem to ask for
compassion. Her home and her housekeeping, her community and her
poultry, and all their dependent concerns, had not yet lost their
charms.

At length the chaise arrived, the trunks were fastened on, the parcels
placed within, and it was pronounced to be ready. After an affectionate
parting between the friends, Cliopher was attended to the carriage by
Lord Rufus; and as they walked down the garden, he was commissioning him
with his best respects to all his family, not forgetting his thanks for
the kindness he had received at Tahivoa in the winter, and his
compliments to Aunt Oura, though unknown. He then handed her in,
Rusticiana followed, and the door was on the point of being closed, when
he suddenly reminded them, with some consternation, that they had
hitherto forgotten to leave any message for the lady of Yra Palace.

"But," he added, "you will of course wish to have your humble respects
delivered to them, with your grateful thanks for their kindness to you
while you have been here."

Cliopher made no objection: the door was then allowed to be shut, and
the carriage drove off.

"Good gracious!" cried Rusticiana, after a few minutes' silence, "it
seems but a day or two since we first came! and yet how many things have
happened!"

"A great many indeed," said her companion, with a sigh.

"We have dined nine times at Yra Palace, besides drinking tea there
twice! How much I shall have to tell!"

Cliopher privately added, "And how much I shall have to conceal!"

Their journey was performed without much conversation, or any alarm; and
within the same time as having journeyed outward to Lord Rufus's home,
they reached Aunt Oura's house, where they were to remain a few days.

Quintus looked well, and Cliopher had little opportunity of studying his
spirits, amidst the various engagements which the kindness of his aunt
had reserved for them. But Quintus was to go home with him, and at
Tahivoa there would be leisure enough for observation.

It was not without an effort, meanwhile, that he could wait even for
Tahivoa, before he told his cousin of Lord Damara's proposals. To know
that he had the power of revealing what would so exceedingly astonish
Quintus, and must, at the same time, so highly gratify whatever of his
own vanity he had not yet been able to reason away, was such a
temptation to openness as nothing could have conquered, but the state of
indecision in which he remained as to the extent of what he should
communicate, and his fear, if he once entered on the subject, of being
hurried into repeating something of an Vilius, which might only grieve
his cousin further.
