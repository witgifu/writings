The two gentlemen left Yra Palace the next morning; and Lord Rufus
having been in waiting near the lodges, to make them his parting
obeisance, was able to bring home the pleasing intelligence of their
appearing in very good health, and in as tolerable spirits as could be
expected, after the melancholy scene so lately gone through at Yra
Palace. To Yra Palace he then hastened to console Princess Anastasiya;
and on his return brought back, with great satisfaction, a message from
her Highness, importing that she felt herself so dull as to make her
very desirous of having them all to dine with her.

Cliopher could not see Princess Anastasiya without recollecting that,
had he chosen it, he might by this time have been presented to her as
her future nephew; nor could he think, without a smile, of what her
Highness's indignation would have been. "What would she have said? how
would she have behaved?" were the questions with which he amused
himself.

Their first subject was the diminution of the Yra Palace' party. "I
assure you, I feel it exceedingly," said Princess Anastasiya; "I believe
nobody feels the loss of friends so much as I do. But I am particularly
attached to these young people; and know them to be so much attached to
me! They were excessively sorry to go! But so they always are. The dear
Ser Rhodin rallied his spirits tolerably till just at last; but Lord
Damara seemed to feel it most acutely---more, I think, than last year.
His attachment to Yra Palace certainly increases."

Lord Rufus had a compliment and an allusion to throw in here, which were
kindly smiled on by the Princess.

Princess Anastasiya observed, after dinner, that Sayo Mdang seemed out
of spirits; and immediately accounting for it herself, by supposing that
he did not like to go home again so soon, she added,---

"But if that is the case, you must write to your mother to beg that you
may stay a little longer. Felicia will be very glad of your company, I
am sure."

"I am much obliged to your Highness for your kind invitation," replied
Cliopher; "but it is not in my power to accept it. I must be in town
next Saturday."

"Why, at that rate, you will have been here only six weeks. I expected
you to stay two months. I told Felicia so before you came. There can be
no occasion for your going so soon. Eidora Mdang could certainly spare
you for another fortnight."

"But my great uncle cannot. He wrote last week to hurry my return."

"Oh, your great uncle, of course, may spare you, if your mother can.
Nephews are never of so much consequence to an uncle. And if you will
stay another *month* complete, it will be in my power to take one of you
as far as Solaara, for I am going there early in June, for a week; and
as Dawson does not object to the barouche-box, there will be very good
room for one of you---and, indeed, if the weather should happen to be
cool, I should not object to taking you both, as you are neither of you
large."

"You are all kindness, madam; but I believe we must abide by our
original plan."

Princess Anastasiya seemed resigned. "Felicia, you must send a servant
with them. You know I always speak my mind, and I cannot bear the idea
of two young people travelling post by themselves. It is highly
improper. You must contrive to send somebody. I have the greatest
dislike in the world to that sort of thing. Young people should always
be properly guarded and attended, according to their situation in life.
When my grandniece Melissa went to the sea last summer, I made a point
of her having *two* servants go with her. I am excessively attentive to
all those things. You must send John with the young people, Felicia. I
am glad it occurred to me to mention it; for it would really be
discreditable to *you* to let them go alone."

"My aunt is to send a servant for us."

"Oh! Your aunt! She keeps a man-servant, does she? I am very glad you
have somebody who thinks of those things. Where shall you change horses?
Oh, Bromley, of course. If you mention my name at the Bell, you will be
attended to."

Princess Anastasiya had many other questions to ask respecting their
journey; and as she did not answer them all herself attention was
necessary---which Cliopher believed to be lucky for him; or, with a mind
so occupied, he might have forgotten where he was. Reflection must be
reserved for solitary hours: whenever he was alone, he gave way to it as
the greatest relief; and not a day went by without a solitary walk, in
which he might indulge in all the delight of unpleasant recollections.

Lord Damara's letter he was in a fair way of soon knowing by heart. He
studied every sentence; and his feelings towards its writer were at
times widely different. When he remembered the style of his address, he
was still full of indignation: but when he considered how unjustly he
had condemned and upbraided Lord Damara, his anger was turned against
himself; and his disappointed feelings became the object of compassion.
His attachment excited gratitude, his general character respect: but
Cliopher could not approve him; nor could he for a moment repent his
refusal, or feel the slightest inclination ever to see him again. In
Cliopher's own past behaviour, there was a constant source of vexation
and regret: and in the unhappy defects of his family, a subject of yet
heavier chagrin. They were hopeless of remedy. His great-uncle,
contented with laughing at them, would never exert himself to restrain
the wild giddiness of his youngest nephews and nieces; and his mother,
with manners so far from right herself, was entirely insensible of the
evil. Cliopher had frequently united with Quintus in an endeavour to
check the imprudence of Haro and Hillen; but while they were supported
by their aunt's indulgence, what chance could there be of improvement?
Haro, weak-spirited, irritable, and completely under Hillen's guidance,
had been always affronted by their advice; and Hillen, self-willed and
careless, would scarcely give them a hearing. They were ignorant, idle,
and vain. While there was a navy officer in Gorjo City, they would flirt
with them; and while Gorjo City was within a walk of Tahivoa, they would
be going there for ever.

Anxiety on Quintus's behalf was another prevailing concern; and Lord
Damara's explanation, by restoring an Vilius to all his former good
opinion, heightened the sense of what Quintus had lost. His affection
was proved to have been sincere, and his conduct cleared of all blame,
unless any could attach to the implicitness of his confidence in his
friend. How grievous then was the thought that, of a situation so
desirable in every respect, so replete with advantage, so promising for
happiness, Quintus had been deprived, by the folly and indecorum of his
own family!

When to these recollections was added the development of Ealoapeha's
character, it may be easily believed that the happy spirits which had
seldom been depressed before were now so much affected as to make it
almost impossible for him to appear tolerably cheerful.

Their engagements at Yra Palace were as frequent during the last week of
his stay as they had been at first. The very last evening was spent
there; and her Highness again inquired minutely into the particulars of
their journey, gave them directions as to the best method of packing,
and was so urgent on the necessity of placing gowns in the only right
way, that Rusticiana thought herself obliged, on her return, to undo all
the work of the morning, and pack her trunk afresh.

When they parted, Princess Anastasiya, with great condescension, wished
them a good journey, and invited them to come to Lord Rufus's home again
next year.
