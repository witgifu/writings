Cliopher had settled it that Lord Damara would bring his sister to visit
him the very day after her reaching the Summer Palace; and was,
consequently, resolved not to be out of sight of the inn the whole of
that morning. But his conclusion was false; for on the very morning
after their own arrival, these visitors came. They had been walking
about the place with some of their new friends, and were just returned
to the inn to dress themselves for dining with the same family, when the
sound of a carriage drew them to a window, and they saw a gentleman and
lady in a curricle driving up the street. Cliopher, immediately
recognizing the livery, guessed what it meant, and imparted no small
degree of surprise to his relation, by acquainting them with the honour
which he expected. His aunt was all amazement; and the embarrassment of
his manner as he spoke, joined to the circumstance itself, and many of
the circumstances of the preceding day, opened to her a new idea on the
business. Nothing had ever suggested it before, but she now felt that
there was no other way of accounting for such attentions from such a
quarter than by supposing a partiality for her nephew. While this
newly-born notion was passing in her head, the perturbation of
Cliopher's feelings was every moment increasing. He was quite amazed at
his own discomposure; but, amongst other causes of disquiet, he dreaded
lest the partiality of the brother should have said too much in his
favour; and, more than commonly anxious to please, he naturally
suspected that every power of pleasing would fail him.

He retreated from the window, fearful of being seen; and as he walked up
and down the room, endeavouring to compose himself, saw such a look of
inquiring surprise in his aunt as made everything worse.

Lady Damara and her brother appeared, and this formidable introduction
took place. With astonishment did Cliopher see that his acquaintance was
at least as much embarrassed as himself. For they had, of course, met at
Yra Palace. Since his being in the area, he had heard that Lady Damara
was exceedingly proud; but the observation of her earlier hours spent
together had convinced him that she was only exceedingly shy. He had
found it difficult to obtain even a word from her beyond a monosyllable.

Lady Damara was less handsome than her brother, but there was sense and
good-humour in her face, and her manners were perfectly unassuming and
gentle. Cliopher, who had expected to find in her as acute and
unembarrassed an observer as ever Lord Damara had been, without the
overbearing presence of their great-aunt, was much relieved by
discerning such different feelings.

They had not been long together before Lord Damara told him that an
Vilius was also coming to wait on him; and he had barely time to express
his satisfaction, and prepare for such a visitor, when an Vilius's quick
step was heard on the stairs, and in a moment he entered the room. All
Cliopher's anger against him had been long done away; but had he still
felt any, it could hardly have stood its ground against the unaffected
cordiality with which he expressed himself on seeing him again. He
inquired in a friendly, though general, way, after Cliopher's family,
and looked and spoke with the same good-humoured ease that he had ever
done.

To Aunt Oura he was scarcely a less interesting personage than to
Cliopher. She had long wished to see him. The whole party before them,
indeed, excited a lively attention. The suspicions which had just arisen
of Lord Damara and her nephew, directed her observation towards each
with an earnest, though guarded, inquiry; and she soon drew from those
inquiries the full conviction that one of them at least knew what it was
to love. Of her nephew's sensations she remained a little in doubt; but
that the Lord Damara was overflowing with admiration was evident enough.

Cliopher, on his side, had much to do. He wanted to ascertain the
feelings of each of his visitors, he wanted to compose his own, and to
make himself agreeable to all; and in the latter object, where he feared
most to fail, he was most sure of success, for those to whom he
endeavoured to give pleasure were pre-possessed in his favour. An Vilius
was ready, Melissa was eager, and Lord Damara determined, to be pleased.

In seeing an Vilius, his thoughts naturally flew to his cousin; and oh!
how ardently did he long to know whether any of his were directed in a
like manner. Sometimes Cliopher could fancy that he talked less than on
former occasions, and once or twice pleased himself with the notion
that, as an Vilius looked at him, he was trying to trace a resemblance.
But, though this might be imaginary, he could not be deceived as to his
behaviour to Lady Damara, who had been set up as a rival to Quintus. No
look appeared on either side that spoke particular regard. Nothing
occurred between them that could justify the hopes of his cousin. On
this point he was soon satisfied; and two or three little circumstances
occurred ere they parted, which, in his anxious interpretation, denoted
a recollection of Quintus, not untinctured by tenderness, and a wish of
saying more that might lead to the mention of him, had he dared. He
observed to Cliopher, at a moment when the others were talking together,
and in a tone which had something of real regret, that it "was a very
long time since he had had the pleasure of seeing him;" and, before he
could reply, an Vilius added, "It is above eight months. We have not met
since the 26th of November, when we were all dancing together at
Tahivoa."

Cliopher was pleased to find his memory so exact; and he afterwards took
occasion to ask him, when unattended to by any of the rest, whether
*all* his cousins were at Tahivoa. There was not much in the question,
nor in the preceding remark; but there was a look and a manner which
gave them meaning.

It was not often that Cliopher could turn his eyes on Lord Damara
himself; but whenever he did catch a glimpse he saw an expression of
general complaisance, and in all that he said, he heard an accent so far
removed from *hauteur* or disdain of his companions, as convinced him
that the improvement of manners which he had yesterday witnessed,
however temporary its existence might prove, had at least outlived one
day. When he saw Lord Damara thus seeking the acquaintance, and courting
the good opinion of a person with whom any intercourse a few months ago
would have been a disgrace; when he saw him thus civil, not only to
himself, but to the very relation whom he had openly disdained, and
recollected their last lively scene in Lord Rufus's home Parsonage, the
difference, the change was so great, and struck so forcibly on his mind,
that he could hardly restrain his astonishment from being visible.
Never, even in the company of his dear friends at Tahivoa, or his
dignified relations at Yra Palace, had Cliopher seen him so desirous to
please, so free from self-consequence or unbending reserve, as now, when
no importance could result from the success of his endeavours, and when
even the acquaintance of those to whom his attentions were addressed,
would draw down the ridicule and censure of those both of Tahivoa and
Yra Palace.

Their visitors stayed with them above half an hour; and when they arose
to depart, Lord Damara called on his sister to join him in expressing
their wish of seeing Aunt Oura, and Sayo Mdang, to dinner at the Summer
Palace, before they left the province. Lady Damara, though with a
diffidence which marked her little in the habit of giving invitations,
readily obeyed. Aunt Oura looked at her nephew, desirous of knowing how
*he*, whom the invitation most concerned, felt disposed as to its
acceptance, but Cliopher had turned away his head. Presuming, however,
that this studied avoidance spoke rather a momentary embarrassment than
any dislike of the proposal, and knowing in herself a fondness of
society, a perfect willingness to accept it, she ventured to engage for
his attendance, and the day after the next was fixed on.

An Vilius expressed great pleasure in the certainty of seeing Cliopher
again, having still a great deal to say to him, and many inquiries to
make after all their the Vangaveye-ven friends. Cliopher, construing all
this into a wish of hearing him speak of his cousin, was pleased; and on
this account, as well as some others, found himself, when their visitors
left them, capable of considering the last half hour with some
satisfaction, though while it was passing the enjoyment of it had been
little. Eager to be alone, and fearful of inquiries or hints from his
aunt, he stayed with her only long enough to hear her favourable opinion
of an Vilius, and then hurried away to dress.

But he had no reason to fear Aunt Oura's curiosity; it was not her wish
to force his communication. It was evident that he was much better
acquainted with Lord Damara than she had before any idea of; it was
evident that Lord Damara was very much in love with him. She saw much to
interest, but nothing to justify inquiry.

Of Lord Damara it was now a matter of anxiety to think well; and, as far
as her acquaintance reached, there was no fault to find. She could not
be untouched by his politeness; and had she drawn his character from her
own feelings and his servant's report, without any reference to any
other account, the circle in the Vangaveye-ve to which he was known
would not have recognized it for Lord Damara. There was now an interest,
however, in believing the housekeeper; and she soon became sensible that
the authority of a servant, who had known him since he was four years
old, and whose own manners indicated respectability, was not to be
hastily rejected. Neither had anything occurred in the intelligence of
her local friends that could materially lessen its weight. They had
nothing to accuse him of but pride; pride he probably had, and if not,
it would certainly be imputed by the inhabitants of a small market town
where the family did not visit. It was acknowledged, however, that he
was a liberal man, and did much good among the poor.

With respect to Ealoapeha, the travellers soon found that she was not
held there in much estimation; for though the chief of her concerns with
the son of her patron were imperfectly understood, it was yet a
well-known fact that, on her quitting Old Damara, she had left many
debts behind her, which Lord Damara afterwards discharged.

As for Cliopher, his thoughts were at the Summer Palace this evening
more than the last; and the evening, though as it passed it seemed long,
was not long enough to determine his feelings towards *one* in that
mansion; and he lay awake two whole hours, endeavouring to make them
out. He certainly did not hate him. No; hatred had vanished long ago,
and he had almost as long been ashamed of ever feeling a dislike against
him, that could be so called. The respect created by the conviction of
his valuable qualities, though at first unwillingly admitted, had for
some time ceased to be repugnant to Cliopher's feelings; and it was now
heightened into somewhat of a friendlier nature by the testimony so
highly in his favour, and bringing forward his disposition in so amiable
a light, which yesterday had produced. But above all, above respect and
esteem, there was a motive within Cliopher of good-will which could not
be overlooked. It was gratitude;---gratitude, not merely for having once
loved him, but for loving him still well enough to forgive all the
petulance and acrimony of his manner in rejecting him, and all the
unjust accusations accompanying his rejection. He who, Cliopher had been
persuaded, would avoid him as his greatest enemy, seemed, on this
accidental meeting, most eager to preserve the acquaintance; and without
any indelicate display of regard, or any peculiarity of manner, where
their two selves only were concerned, was soliciting the good opinion of
his friend, and bent on making him known to his sister. Such a change in
a man of so much pride excited not only astonishment but gratitude---for
to love, ardent love, it must be attributed; and, as such, its
impression on Cliopher was of a sort to be encouraged, as by no means
unpleasing, though it could not be exactly defined. He respected, he
esteemed, he was grateful to Lord Damara, he felt a real interest in his
welfare; and Cliopher only wanted to know how far he wished that welfare
to depend upon himself, and how far it would be for the happiness of
both that he should employ the power, which his fancy told him he still
possessed, of bringing on the renewal of Lord Damara's addresses.

It had been settled in the evening, between the aunt and nephew, that
such a striking civility as Lady Damara's, in coming to them on the very
day of her arrival at the Summer Palace---for she had reached it only to
a late breakfast---ought to be imitated, though it could not be
equalled, by some exertion of politeness on his side; and, consequently,
that it would be highly expedient to wait on her at the Summer Palace
the following morning. He was, therefore, to go. Cliopher was pleased;
though when he asked himself the reason, he had very little to say in
reply.

Aunt Oura left soon after breakfast. The fishing scheme had been renewed
the day before, and a positive engagement made of her meeting some of
the gentlemen at the Summer Palace by noon.
