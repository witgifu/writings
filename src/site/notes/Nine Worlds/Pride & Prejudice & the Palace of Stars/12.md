In consequence of an agreement between the cousins, Cliopher wrote the
next morning to his mother, to beg that the yacht might be sent for them
in the course of the day. But Eidora, who had calculated on her son and
nephew remaining at Navikiani till the following Tuesday, which would
exactly finish Quintus's week, could not bring herself to receive them
with pleasure before. Her answer, therefore, was not propitious, at
least not to Cliopher's wishes, for he was impatient to get home. Eidora
sent them word that they could not possibly have the yacht before
Tuesday; and in her postscript it was added, that if the Cavalier and
his cousins pressed them to stay longer, she could spare them very well.
Against staying longer, however, Cliopher was positively resolved---nor
did he much expect it would be asked; and fearful, on the contrary, of
being considered as intruding themselves needlessly long, she urged
Quintus to borrow the Cavalier's yacht immediately, and at length it was
settled that their original design of leaving Navikiani that morning
should be mentioned, and the request made.

The communication excited many professions of concern; and enough was
said of wishing them to stay at least till the following day to work on
Quintus; and till the morrow their going was deferred. Lord Lior was
then sorry that he had proposed the delay; for his jealousy and dislike
of one cousin much exceeded his affection for the other.

The master of the house heard with real sorrow that they were to go so
soon, and repeatedly tried to persuade Sayo Mdang that it would not be
safe for him---that he was not enough recovered; but Quintus was firm
where he felt himself to be right.

To Lord Damara it was welcome intelligence: Cliopher had been at
Navikiani long enough. He attracted him more than he liked; and Lord
Lior was uncivil to *him* and more teasing than usual to himself. He
wisely resolved to be particularly careful that no sign of admiration
should *now* escape him---nothing that could elevate him with the hope
of influencing his felicity; sensible that, if such an idea had been
suggested, his behaviour during the last day must have material weight
in confirming or crushing it. Steady to his purpose, he scarcely spoke
ten words to Cliopher through the whole of Saturday: and though they
were at one time left by themselves for half an hour, he adhered most
conscientiously to his book, and would not even look at Cliopher.

On Sunday, after morning service, the separation, so agreeable to almost
all, took place. Lord Lior's civility to Cliopher increased at last very
rapidly, as well as his affection for Quintus; and when they parted,
after assuring the latter of the pleasure it would always give Lord Lior
to see him either at Tahivoa or Navikiani, and embracing him most
tenderly, he even shook hands with the former. Cliopher took leave of
the whole party in the liveliest spirits.

They were not welcomed home very cordially by their matriarch. Eidora
Mdang wondered at their coming, and thought them very wrong to give so
much trouble, and was sure Quintus would have caught cold again. But
their great-uncle, though very laconic in his expressions of pleasure,
was really glad to see them; he had felt their importance in the family
circle. The evening conversation, when they were all assembled, had lost
much of its animation, and almost all its sense, by the absence of
Quintus and Cliopher.

They found Louya, as usual, deep in the study of thorough bass and human
nature; and had some new extracts to admire and some new observations of
threadbare morality to listen to. Haro and Hillen had information for
them of a different sort. Much had been done, and much had been said in
the navy since the preceding Wednesday; several of the officers had
dined lately with their uncle; a private had been flogged; and it had
actually been hinted that Captain Forster was going to be married.
