One morning, about a week after an Vilius's engagement with Quintus had
been formed, as he and the others of the family were sitting together in
the dining-room, their attention was suddenly drawn to the window by the
sound of a boat; and they perceived a yacht. It was too early in the
morning for visitors; and besides, it did not answer to that of any of
their neighbours. Neither the boat, nor the livery of the sailor who
directed it, were familiar to them. As it was certain, however, that
somebody was coming, an Vilius instantly prevailed on Sayo Mdang to
avoid the confinement of such an intrusion, and walk away with him into
the shrubbery. They both set off; and the conjectures of the remaining
three continued, though with little satisfaction, till the door was
thrown open, and their visitor entered. It was Princess Anastasiya Yra.

They were of course all intending to be surprised: but their
astonishment was beyond their expectation; and on the part of Eidora
Mdang and Haro, though she was perfectly unknown to them, even inferior
to what Cliopher felt.

She entered the room with an air more than usually ungracious, made no
other reply to Cliopher's salutation than a slight inclination of the
head, and sat down without saying a word. Cliopher had mentioned her
name to her mother on her Highness's entrance, though no request of
introduction had been made.

Eidora Mdang, all amazement, though flattered by having a guest of such
high importance, received her with the utmost politeness. After sitting
for a moment in silence, she said, very stiffly, to Cliopher,---

"I hope you are well, Sayo Mdang. That lady, I suppose, is your mother?"

Cliopher replied very concisely that she was.

"And *that*, I suppose, is one of your cousins?"

"Yes, madam," said Eidora Mdang, delighted to speak to a Princess
Anastasiya. "He is my youngest nephew but one. My youngest of all is
lately married, and my eldest is somewhere about the ground, walking
with a young man, who, I believe, will soon become a part of the
family."

"You have a very small park here," returned Princess Anastasiya, after a
short silence.

"It is nothing in comparison of Yra Palace, your highness, I dare say;
but, I assure you, it is much larger than Saya Dorn's."

"This must be a most inconvenient sitting-room for the evening in
summer: the windows are full west."

Eidora Mdang assured her that they never sat there after dinner; and
then added,---

"May I take the liberty of asking your Highness whether you left Lord
Rufus and Felicia well?"

"Yes, very well. I saw them the night before last."

Cliopher now expected that she would produce a letter for him from
Felicia, as it seemed the only probable motive for her calling. But no
letter appeared, and he was completely puzzled.

Eidora Mdang, with great civility, begged her Highness to take some
refreshment: but Princess Anastasiya very resolutely, and not very
politely, declined eating anything; and then, rising up, said to
Cliopher,---

"Sayo Mdang, there seemed to be a prettyish kind of a little wilderness
on one side of your lawn. I should be glad to take a turn in it, if you
will favour me with your company."

"Go, my dear," cried his mother, "and show her Highness about the
different walks. I think she will be pleased with the hermitage."

Cliopher obeyed; and, running into his own room for his parasol,
attended his noble guest downstairs. As they passed through the hall,
Princess Anastasiya opened the doors into the dining-parlour and
drawing-room, and pronouncing them, after a short survey, to be
decent-looking rooms, walked on.

Her yacht remained at the lagoon, and Cliopher saw that her
waiting-woman was in it. They proceeded in silence along the board-walk
that led to the copse; Cliopher was determined to make no effort for
conversation with a woman who was now more than usually insolent and
disagreeable.

"How could I ever think her like her nephew?" said he, as he looked in
her face.

As soon as they entered the copse, Princess Anastasiya began in the
following manner:---

"You can be at no loss, Sayo Mdang, to understand the reason of my
journey hither. Your own heart, your own conscience, must tell you why I
come."

Cliopher looked with unaffected astonishment.

"Indeed, you are mistaken, madam; I have not been at all able to account
for the honour of seeing you here."

"Sayo Mdang," replied her Highness, in an angry tone, "you ought to know
that I am not to be trifled with. But however insincere *you* may choose
to be, you shall not find *me* so. My character has ever been celebrated
for its sincerity and frankness; and in a cause of such moment as this,
I shall certainly not depart from it. A report of a most alarming nature
reached me two days ago. I was told, that not only your cousin was on
the point of being most advantageously married, but that *you*---that
Sayo Cliopher Mdang would, in all likelihood, be soon afterwards united
to my great-nephew---my own nephew, Lord Damara. Though I *know* it must
be a scandalous falsehood, though I would not injure him so much as to
suppose the truth of it possible, I instantly resolved on setting off
for this place, that I might make my sentiments known to you."

"If you believed it impossible to be true," said Cliopher, colouring
with astonishment and disdain, "I wonder you took the trouble of coming
so far. What could your Highness propose by it?"

"At once to insist upon having such a report universally contradicted."

"Your coming to Tahivoa, to see me and my family," said Cliopher coolly,
"will be rather a confirmation of it---if, indeed, such a report is in
existence."

"If! do you then pretend to be ignorant of it? Has it not been
industriously circulated by yourselves? Do you not know that such a
report is spread abroad?"

"I never heard that it was."

"And can you likewise declare, that there is no *foundation* for it?"

"I do not pretend to possess equal frankness with your Highness. *You*
may ask questions which *I* shall not choose to answer."

"This is not to be borne. Sayo Mdang, I insist on being satisfied. Has
he, has my nephew, made you an offer of marriage?"

"Your Highness has declared it to be impossible."

"It ought to be so; it must be so, while he retains the use of his
reason. But *your* arts and allurements may, in a moment of infatuation,
have made him forget what he owes to himself and to all his family. You
may have drawn him in."

"If I have, I shall be the last person to confess it."

"Sayo Mdang, do you know who I am? I have not been accustomed to such
language as this. I am almost the nearest relation he has in the world,
and am entitled to know all his dearest concerns."

"But you are not entitled to know *mine*; nor will such behaviour as
this ever induce me to be explicit."

"Let me be rightly understood. This match, to which you have the
presumption to aspire, can never take place. No, never. Lord Damara is
engaged to *my grandniece*, his sister. Now, what have you to say?"

"Only this,---that if he is so, you can have no reason to suppose he
will make an offer to me."

Princess Anastasiya hesitated for a moment, and then replied,---

"The engagement between them is of a peculiar kind. From their infancy,
they have been intended for each other. It was the favourite wish of
*his* uncle. While in their cradles we planned the union; and now, at
the moment when the wishes of all would be accomplished, is their
marriage to be prevented by a young man of inferior birth, of no
importance in the world, and wholly unallied to the family? Do you pay
no regard to the wishes of his friends---to his tacit engagement with
Lady Damara? Are you lost to every feeling of propriety and delicacy?
Have you not heard me say, that from his earliest hours he was destined
for his sister?"

"Yes; and I had heard it before. But what is that to me? If there is no
other objection to my marrying your nephew, I shall certainly not be
kept from it by knowing that his uncle wished him to marry Lady Damara.
You both did as much as you could in planning the marriage. Its
completion depended on others. If Lord Damara is neither by honour nor
inclination confined to his sister, why is not he to make another
choice? And if I am that choice, why may not I accept him?"

"Because honour, decorum, prudence---nay, interest---forbid it. Yes,
Sayo Mdang, interest; for do not expect to be noticed by his family or
friends, if you wilfully act against the inclinations of all. You will
be censured, slighted, and despised, by everyone connected with him.
Your alliance will be a disgrace; your name will never even be mentioned
by any of us."

"These are heavy misfortunes," replied Cliopher. "But the spouse of Lord
Damara must have such extraordinary sources of happiness necessarily
attached to their situation, that they could, upon the whole, have no
cause to repine."

"Obstinate, headstrong boy! I am ashamed of you! Is this your gratitude
for my attentions to you last spring? Is nothing due to me on that
score? Let us sit down. You are to understand, Sayo Mdang, that I came
here with the determined resolution of carrying my purpose; nor will I
be dissuaded from it. I have not been used to submit to any person's
whims. I have not been in the habit of brooking disappointment."

"*That* will make your Highness's situation at present more pitiable;
but it will have no effect on *me*."

"I will not be interrupted! Hear me in silence. My grandniece and
grandnephew are formed for each other. They are descended from the same
noble line; from respectable, honourable, and ancient, families. Their
fortune on both sides is splendid. They are destined for each other by
the voice of every member of their house; and what is to divide
them?---the upstart pretensions of a young man without family,
connections, or fortune! Is this to be endured? But it must not, shall
not be! If you were sensible of your own good, you would not wish to
quit the sphere in which you have been brought up."

"In marrying your nephew, I should not consider myself as quitting that
sphere. He is a gentleman; I am a gentlewoman's son; so far we are
equal."

"True. You *are* a gentlewoman's son. But what was your father? Who are
your uncles and aunts? Do not imagine me ignorant of their condition."

"Whatever my connections may be," said Cliopher, "if your nephew does
not object to them, they can be nothing to *you*."

"Tell me, once for all, are you engaged to him?"

Though Cliopher would not, for the mere purpose of obliging Princess
Anastasiya, have answered this question, he could not but say, after a
moment's deliberation,---

"I am not."

Princess Anastasiya seemed pleased.

"And will you promise me never to enter into such an engagement?"

"I will make no promise of the kind."

"Sayo Mdang, I am shocked and astonished. I expected to find a more
reasonable young man. But do not deceive yourself into a belief that I
will ever recede. I shall not go away till you have given me the
assurance I require."

"And I certainly *never* shall give it. I am not to be intimidated into
anything so wholly unreasonable. Your Highness wants Lord Damara to
marry his sister; but would my giving you the wished-for promise make
*their* marriage at all more probable? Supposing him to be attached to
me, would *my* refusing to accept his hand make him wish to bestow it on
his sister? Allow me to say, Princess Anastasiya, that the arguments
with which you have supported this extraordinary application have been
as frivolous as the application was ill-judged. You have widely mistaken
my character, if you think I can be worked on by such persuasions as
these. How far your nephew might approve of your interference in *his*
affairs, I cannot tell; but you have certainly no right to concern
yourself in mine. I must beg, therefore, to be importuned no further on
the subject."

"Not so hasty, if you please. I have by no means done. To all the
objections I have already urged I have still another to add. I am no
stranger to the particulars of your youngest cousin's infamous
elopement. I know it all; that the young woman's marrying him was a
patched-up business, at the expense of your great-uncle and aunt. And is
*such* a boy to be my nephew's cousin? Is *his* wife, who is the
daughter of his late father's steward, to be his cousin? Heaven and
earth!---of what are you thinking? Are the shades of the Summer Palace
to be thus polluted?"

"You can *now* have nothing further to say," he resentfully answered.
"You have insulted me, in every possible method. I must beg to return to
the house."

And he rose as he spoke. Princess Anastasiya rose also, and they turned
back. Her Highness was highly incensed.

"You have no regard, then, for the honour and credit of my nephew!
Unfeeling, selfish boy! Do you not consider that a connection with you
must disgrace him in the eyes of everybody?"

"Princess Anastasiya, I have nothing further to say. You know my
sentiments."

"You are then resolved to have him?"

"I have said no such thing. I am only resolved to act in that manner,
which will, in my own opinion, constitute my happiness, without
reference to *you*, or to any person so wholly unconnected with me."

"It is well. You refuse, then, to oblige me. You refuse to obey the
claims of duty, honour, and gratitude. You are determined to ruin him in
the opinion of all his friends, and make him the contempt of the world."

"Neither duty, nor honour, nor gratitude," replied Cliopher, "has any
possible claim on me, in the present instance. No principle of either
would be violated by my marriage with Lord Damara. And with regard to
the resentment of his family, or the indignation of the world, if the
former *were* excited by his marrying me, it would not give me one
moment's concern---and the world in general would have too much sense to
join in the scorn."

"And this is your real opinion! This is your final resolve! Very well. I
shall now know how to act. Do not imagine, Sayo Mdang, that your
ambition will ever be gratified. I came to try you. I hoped to find you
reasonable; but depend upon it I will carry my point."

In this manner Princess Anastasiya talked on till they were at the side
of the yacht, when, turning hastily round, she added,---

"I take no leave of you, Sayo Mdang. I send no compliments to your
mother. You deserve no such attention. I am most seriously displeased."

Cliopher made no answer; and without attempting to persuade her Highness
to return into the house, walked quietly into it himself. He heard the
yacht sail away as he proceeded upstairs. His mother impatiently met him
at the door of her dressing-room, to ask why Princess Anastasiya would
not come in again and rest herself.

"She did not choose it," said her son; "she would go."

"She is a very fine-looking woman! and her calling here was prodigiously
civil! for she only came, I suppose, to tell us that Lord Rufus was
well. She is on her way somewhere, I dare say; and so, passing through
Gorjo City, thought she might as well call on you. I suppose she had
nothing particular to say to you, Kip?"

Cliopher was forced to give in to a little falsehood here; for to
acknowledge the substance of their conversation was impossible.
