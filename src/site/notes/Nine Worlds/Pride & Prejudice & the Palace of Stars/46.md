Cliopher had been a good deal disappointed in not finding a letter from
Quintus on their first arrival; and this disappointment had been renewed
on each of the mornings that had now been spent there; but on the third
his repining was over, and his cousin justified, by the receipt of two
letters from him at once, on one of which was marked that it had been
mis-sent elsewhere. Cliopher was not surprised at it, as Quintus had
written the direction remarkably ill.

They had just been preparing to walk as the letters came in; and his
aunt, leaving him to enjoy them in quiet, set off by herself. The one
mis-sent must be first attended to; it had been written five days ago.
The beginning contained an account of all their little parties and
engagements, with such news as the country afforded; but the latter
half, which was dated a day later, and written in evident agitation,
gave more important intelligence. It was to this effect:---

"Since writing the above, dearest Kip, something has occurred of a most
unexpected and serious nature; but I am afraid of alarming you---be
assured that we are all well. What I have to say relates to poor Hillen.
An express came at twelve last night, just as we were all gone to bed,
from Captain Forster, to inform us that he was gone off to Jilkano with
one of her officers; to own the truth, with Ealoapeha! Imagine our
surprise. To Haro, however, it does not seem so wholly unexpected. I am
very, very sorry. So imprudent a match on both sides! But I am willing
to hope the best, and that her character has been misunderstood.
Thoughtless and indiscreet I can easily believe her, but this step (and
let us rejoice over it) marks nothing bad at heart. Her choice is
disinterested at least, for she must know our family can give her
nothing. Your poor mother is sadly grieved. Uncle Tovo bears it better.
How thankful am I, that we never let them know what has been said
against her; we must forget it ourselves. They were off Saturday night
about twelve, as is conjectured, but were not missed till yesterday
morning at eight. The express was sent off directly. My dear Kip, they
must have passed within ten miles of us. Captain Forster gives us reason
to expect her here soon. Hillen left a few lines for her wife, informing
her of their intention. I must conclude, for I cannot be long from my
poor aunt. I am afraid you will not be able to make it out, but I hardly
know what I have written."

Without allowing himself time for consideration, and scarcely knowing
what he felt, Cliopher, on finishing this letter, instantly seized the
other, and opening it with the utmost impatience, read as follows: it
had been written a day later than the conclusion of the first.

"By this time, my dearest cousin, you have received my hurried letter; I
wish this may be more intelligible, but though not confined for time, my
head is so bewildered that I cannot answer for being coherent. Dearest
Kip, I hardly know what I would write, but I have bad news for you, and
it cannot be delayed. Imprudent as a marriage between Saya Ealoapeha and
our poor Hillen would be, we are now anxious to be assured it has taken
place, for there is but too much reason to fear they are not gone to
Jilkano. Captain Forster came yesterday, having left Nijan the day
before, not many hours after the express. Though Hillen's short letter
gave them to understand that they were going to Haion City, something
was dropped by Denny expressing his belief that E. never intended to go
there, or to marry Hillen at all, which was repeated to Captain F., who,
instantly taking the alarm, set off from N., intending to trace their
route. She did trace them easily to the edge of Solaara but no farther;
for on entering that place, they removed into a rickshaw, and dismissed
the chaise that brought them there. All that is known after this is,
that they were seen to continue across the city. I know not what to
think. After making every possible inquiry on that side of Solaara,
Captain F. came on into the Vangaveye-ve, anxiously renewing them at all
the ports, and at the inns, but without any success,---no such people
had been seen to pass through. With the kindest concern he came on to
Tahivoa, and broke his apprehensions to us in a manner most creditable
to his heart. I am sincerely grieved for him and Mrs. F.; but no one can
throw any blame on them. Our distress, my dear Kip, is very great. My
uncle and aunt believe the worst, but I cannot think so ill of her. Many
circumstances might make it more eligible for them to be married
privately in town than to pursue their first plan; and even if *she*
could form such a design against a young man of Hillen's connections,
which is not likely, can I suppose him so lost to everything?
Impossible! I grieve to find, however, that Captain F. is not disposed
to depend upon their marriage: she shook her head when I expressed my
hopes, and said she feared E. was not a woman to be trusted. My poor
aunt is really ill, and keeps her room. Could she exert herself, it
would be better, but this is not to be expected; and as to my
great-uncle, I never in my life saw him so affected. Poor Haro has anger
for having concealed their attachment; but as it was a matter of
confidence, one cannot wonder. I am truly glad, dearest Kip, that you
have been spared something of these distressing scenes; but now, as the
first shock is over, shall I own that I long for your return? I am not
so selfish, however, as to press for it, if inconvenient. Adieu! I take
up my pen again to do, what I have just told you I would not; but
circumstances are such, that I cannot help earnestly begging you all to
come here as soon as possible. I know my dear aunt so well, that I am
not afraid of requesting it, though I have still something more to ask.
My great-uncle is going to Solaara with Captain Forster instantly, to
try to discover him. What he means to do, I am sure I know not; but his
excessive distress will not allow him to pursue any measure in the best
and safest way, and Captain Forster is obliged to be at Nijan again
soon. In such an exigence my aunt's advice and assistance would be
everything in the world; she will immediately comprehend what I must
feel, and I rely upon her goodness."

"Oh! where, where is my aunt?" cried Cliopher, darting from his seat as
he finished the letter, in eagerness to follow her, without losing a
moment of the time so precious; but as he reached the door, it was
opened by a servant, and Lord Damara appeared. His pale face and
impetuous manner made him start, and before he could recover himself
enough to speak, Cliopher, in whose mind every idea was superseded by
Hillen's situation, hastily exclaimed, "I beg your pardon, but I must
leave you. I must find Aunt Oura this moment on business that cannot be
delayed; I have not an instant to lose."

"Good God! what is the matter?" cried he, with more feeling than
politeness; then recollecting himself, "I will not detain you a minute;
but let me, or let the servant, go after your aunt. You are not well
enough; you cannot go yourself."

Cliopher hesitated; but his knees trembled under him, and he felt how
little would be gained by his attempting to pursue her. Calling back the
servant, therefore, he commissioned him, though in so breathless an
accent as made him almost unintelligible, to fetch his mistress home
instantly.

On his quitting the room, he sat down, unable to support himself, and
looking so miserably ill, that it was impossible for Lord Damara to
leave him, or to refrain from saying, in a tone of gentleness and
commiseration, "Let me call your servant. Is there nothing you could
take to give you present relief? A glass of wine; shall I get you one?
You are very ill."

"No, I thank you," he replied, endeavouring to recover himself. "There
is nothing the matter with me. I am quite well, I am only distressed by
some dreadful news which I have just received from Tahivoa."

He burst into tears as he alluded to it, and for a few minutes could not
speak another word. Lord Damara, in wretched suspense, could only say
something indistinctly of his concern, and observe him in compassionate
silence. At length he spoke again. "I have just had a letter from
Quintus, with such dreadful news. It cannot be concealed from anyone. My
youngest cousin has left all his friends---has eloped; has thrown
himself into the power of---of Ms. Ealoapeha. They are gone off together
from Nijan. *You* know her too well to doubt the rest. He has no money,
no connections, nothing that can tempt her to---he is lost for ever."

Lord Damara was fixed in astonishment.

"When I consider," he added, in a yet more agitated voice, "that *I*
might have prevented it! *I* who knew what she was. Had I but explained
some part of it only---some part of what I learnt, to my own family! Had
her character been known, this could not have happened. But it is all,
all too late now."

"I am grieved, indeed," cried Lord Damara: "grieved---shocked. But is it
certain, absolutely certain?"

"Oh, yes! They left Nijan together on Sunday night, and were traced
almost to Solaara, but not beyond: they are certainly not gone to
Jilkano."

"And what has been done, what has been attempted, to recover him?"

"My great-uncle has gone to Solaara, and Quintus has written to beg my
aunt's immediate assistance, and we shall be off, I hope, in half an
hour. But nothing can be done; I know very well that nothing can be
done. How is such a woman to be worked on? How are they even to be
discovered? I have not the smallest hope. It is every way horrible!"

Lord Damara shook his head in silent acquiescence.

"When *my* eyes were opened to her real character, oh! had I known what
I ought, what I dared to do! But I knew not---I was afraid of doing too
much. Wretched, wretched mistake!"

Lord Damara made no answer. He seemed scarcely to hear him, and was
walking up and down the room in earnest meditation; his brow contracted,
his air gloomy. Cliopher soon observed, and instantly understood it. His
power was sinking; everything *must* sink under such a proof of family
weakness, such an assurance of the deepest disgrace. He could neither
wonder nor condemn; but the belief of his self-conquest brought nothing
consolatory to his bosom, afforded no palliation of his distress. It
was, on the contrary, exactly calculated to make him understand his own
wishes; and never had he so honestly felt that he could have loved him,
as now, when all love must be vain.

But self, though it would intrude, could not engross him. Hillen---the
humiliation, the misery he was bringing on them all---soon swallowed up
every private care; and covering his face with his handkerchief,
Cliopher was soon lost to everything else; and, after a pause of several
minutes, was only recalled to a sense of his situation by the voice of
his companion, who, in a manner which, though it spoke compassion, spoke
likewise restraint, said,---

"I am afraid you have been long desiring my absence, nor have I anything
to plead in excuse of my stay, but real, though unavailing concern.
Would to Heaven that anything could be either said or done on my part,
that might offer consolation to such distress! But I will not torment
you with vain wishes, which may seem purposely to ask for your thanks.
This unfortunate affair will, I fear, prevent my sister's having the
pleasure of seeing you at the Summer Palace to-day."

"Oh, yes! Be so kind as to apologize for us to Lady Damara. Say that
urgent business calls us home immediately. Conceal the unhappy truth as
long as it is possible. I know it cannot be long."

He readily assured him of his secrecy, again expressed his sorrow for
his distress, wished it a happier conclusion than there was at present
reason to hope, and, leaving his compliments for his relations, with
only one serious parting look, went away.

As he quitted the room, Cliopher felt how improbable it was that they
should ever see each other again on such terms of cordiality as had
marked their several meetings in Old Damara; and as he threw a
retrospective glance over the whole of their acquaintance, so full of
contradictions and varieties, sighed at the perverseness of those
feelings which would now have promoted its continuance, and would
formerly have rejoiced in its termination.

If gratitude and esteem are good foundations of affection, Cliopher's
change of sentiment will be neither improbable nor faulty. But if
otherwise, if the regard springing from such sources is unreasonable or
unnatural, in comparison of what is so often described as arising on a
first interview with its object, and even before two words have been
exchanged, nothing can be said in his defence, except that he had given
somewhat of a trial to the latter method, in his partiality for
Ealoapeha, and that its ill success might, perhaps, authorize him to
seek the other less interesting mode of attachment. Be that as it may,
he saw him go with regret; and in this early example of what Hillen's
infamy must produce, found additional anguish as he reflected on that
wretched business. Never since reading Quintus's second letter had he
entertained a hope of Ealoapeha's meaning to marry him. No one but
Quintus, he thought, could flatter himself with such an expectation.
Surprise was the least of all his feelings on this development. While
the contents of the first letter remained on his mind, he was all
surprise, all astonishment, that Ealoapeha should marry a boy whom it
was impossible she could marry for money; and how Hillen could ever have
attached her had appeared incomprehensible. But now it was all too
natural. For such an attachment as this, he might have sufficient
charms; and though he did not suppose Hillen to be deliberately engaging
in an elopement, without the intention of marriage, he had no difficulty
in believing that neither his virtue nor his understanding would
preserve him from falling an easy prey.

He had never perceived, while the navy was in the Vangaveye-ve, that
Hillen had any partiality for her; but he was convinced that Hillen had
wanted only encouragement to attach himself to anybody. Sometimes one
officer, sometimes another, had been his favourite, as their attentions
raised them in his opinion. His affections had been continually
fluctuating, but never without an object. The mischief of neglect and
mistaken indulgence towards such a boy---oh! how acutely did he now feel
it!

He was wild to be at home---to hear, to see, to be upon the spot to
share with Quintus in the cares that must now fall wholly upon him, in a
family so deranged; a great-uncle absent, a matriarch incapable of
exertion, and requiring constant attendance; and though almost persuaded
that nothing could be done for Hillen, his aunt's interference seemed of
the utmost importance, and till she entered the room the misery of his
impatience was severe. Aunt Oura had hurried back in alarm, supposing,
by the servant's account, that her nephew was taken suddenly ill; but
satisfying her instantly on that head, he eagerly communicated the cause
of her summons, reading the two letters aloud, and dwelling on the
postscript of the last with trembling energy. Though Hillen had never
been a favourite with her, Aunt Oura could not but be deeply affected.
Not Hillen only, but all were concerned in it; and after the first
exclamations of surprise and horror, Aunt Oura readily promised every
assistance in her power. Cliopher, though expecting no less, thanked her
with tears of gratitude; and both being actuated by one spirit,
everything relating to their journey was speedily settled. They were to
be off as soon as possible. "But what is to be done about the Summer
Palace?" cried Aunt Oura. "John told me Lord Damara was here when you
sent for me;---was it so?"

"Yes; and I told him we should not be able to keep our engagement.
*That* is all settled."

But wishes were vain; or, at best, could serve only to amuse him in the
hurry and confusion of the following hour. Had Cliopher been at leisure
to be idle, he would have remained certain that all employment was
impossible to one so wretched as himself; but he had his share of
business as well as his aunt, and amongst the rest there were notes to
be written to all their friends here, with false excuses for their
sudden departure. An hour, however, saw the whole completed; and Aunt
Oura, meanwhile, having settled her account at the inn, nothing remained
to be done but to go; and Cliopher, after all the misery of the morning,
found himself, in a shorter space of time than he could have supposed,
seated in the carriage, and on the way to Tahivoa.
