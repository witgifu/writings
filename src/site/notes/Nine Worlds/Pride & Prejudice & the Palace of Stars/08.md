At five o'clock the two cousins retired to dress, and at half-past six
Cliopher was summoned to dinner. To the civil inquiries which then
poured in, and amongst which he had the pleasure of distinguishing the
much superior solicitude of the Cavalier, he could not make a very
favourable answer. Quintus was by no means better. The cousins, on
hearing this, repeated three or four times how much they were grieved,
how shocking it was to have a bad cold, and how excessively they
disliked being ill themselves; and then thought no more of the matter:
and their indifference towards Quintus, when not immediately before
them, restored Cliopher to the enjoyment of all his original dislike.

Their cousin the Cavalier, indeed, was the only one of the party whom he
could regard with any complacency. His anxiety for Quintus was evident,
and his attentions to Cliopher most pleasing; and they prevented him
feeling himself so much an intruder as he believed he was considered by
the others. Cliopher had very little notice from any but him. Lord Lior
was engrossed by Lord Damara, his cousin Prince Belu, scarcely less so;
and as for Prince Belu's husband, by whom Cliopher sat, he was an
indolent man, who lived only to eat, drink, and play at cards, who, when
he found Cliopher prefered a plain dish to a ragout, had nothing to say
to him.

When dinner was over, he returned directly to Quintus, and Lord Lior
began abusing him as soon as he was out of the room. His manners were
pronounced to be very bad indeed,---a mixture of pride and impertinence:
he had no conversation, no style, no taste, no beauty. Prince Belu
thought the same, and added,---

"He has nothing, in short, to recommend him, but being an excellent
boatman. I shall never forget his appearance this morning. He really
looked almost wild."

"He did indeed, Belu. I could hardly keep my countenance. Very
nonsensical to come at all! Why must *he* be scampering about the Ring,
because his cousin had a cold? His hair so untidy, so blowzy!"

"Yes, and his hems; I hope you saw his hems, six inches deep in
sea-water, I am absolutely certain, and the robe which had been let down
to hide it not doing its office."

"Your picture may be very exact, Belu," said the Cavalier; "but this was
all lost upon me. I thought Cliopher sayo Mdang looked remarkably well
when he came into the room this morning. His dirty hems quite escaped my
notice."

"*You* observed it, my lord, I am sure," said Lord Lior to Lord Damara;
"and I am inclined to think that you would not wish to see *your sister*
make such an exhibition."

"Certainly not."

"To journey three miles, or four miles, or five miles, or whatever it
is, in a canoe, and alone, quite alone! What could he mean by it? It
seems to me to show an abominable sort of conceited independence, a most
country-town indifference to decorum."

"It shows an affection for his cousin that is very pleasing," said the
Cavalier.

"I am afraid, my lord," observed Lord Lior, in a half whisper, "that
this adventure has rather affected your admiration of his fine eyes."

"Not at all," he replied: "they were brightened by the exercise." A
short pause followed this speech, and Prince Belu began again,---

"I have an excessive regard for Quintus Mdang,---he is really a very
sweet man,---and I wish with all my heart he were well settled. But with
such a relations, and such low connections, I am afraid there is no
chance of it."

"I think I have heard you say that their uncle is a barber in Gorjo
City?"

"Yes; and they have another, who lives somewhere near Jilkano."

"That is capital," added Lord Lior; and they both laughed heartily.

"If they had uncles enough to fill *all* Jilkano," cried the Cavalier,
"it would not make them one jot less agreeable."

"But it must very materially lessen their chance of marrying people of
any consideration in the world," replied Lord Damara.

To this speech the Cavalier made no answer; but his cousins gave it
their hearty assent, and indulged their mirth for some time at the
expense of their dear friend's vulgar relations.

With a renewal of tenderness, however, they repaired to his room on
leaving the dining-parlour, and sat with him till summoned to coffee. He
was still very poorly, and Cliopher would not quit her at all, till late
in the evening, when he had the comfort of seeing him asleep, and when
it appeared to him rather right than pleasant that he should go
downstairs himself. On entering the drawing-room, he found the whole
party at Poacher, and was immediately invited to join them; but
suspecting them to be playing high, he declined it, and making his
cousin the excuse, said he would amuse himself, for the short time he
could stay below, with a book. Prince Belu's husband looked at her with
astonishment.

"Do you prefer reading to cards?" said he; "that is rather singular."

"Cliopher sayo Mdang," said Lord Lior, "despises cards. He is a great
reader, and has no pleasure in anything else."

"I deserve neither such praise nor such censure," cried Cliopher; "I am
*not* a great reader, and I have pleasure in many things."

"In nursing your cousin I am sure you have pleasure," said the Cavalier;
"and I hope it will soon be increased by seeing him quite well."

Cliopher thanked him from his heart, and then walked towards a table
where a few books were lying. The Cavalier immediately offered to fetch
him others; all that his library afforded.

"And I wish my collection were larger for your benefit and my own
credit; but I am an idle fellow; and though I have not many, I have more
than I ever looked into."

Cliopher assured him that he could suit himself perfectly with those in
the room.

"I am astonished," said Lord Lior, "that my family should have left so
small a collection of books. What a delightful library you have at the
Summer Palace, my lord!"

"It ought to be good," he replied: "it has been the work of many
generations."

"And then you have added so much to it yourself---you are always buying
books."

"I cannot comprehend the neglect of a family library in such days as
these."

"Neglect! I am sure you neglect nothing that can add to the beauties of
that noble place. Conju, when you build *your* house, I wish it may be
half as delightful as the Summer Palace."

"I wish it may."

"But I would really advise you to make your purchase in that
neighbourhood, and take the Summer Palace for a kind of model. There is
not a finer place in Zunidh than old Damara."

"With all my heart: I will buy the Summer Palace itself, if Damara will
sell it."

"I am talking of possibilities, Conju."

"Upon my word, Lionel, I should think it more possible to get the Summer
Palace by purchase than by imitation."

Cliopher was so much caught by what passed, as to leave him very little
attention for his book; and, soon laying it wholly aside, he drew near
the card-table, and stationed himself between the Cavalier and his
eldest cousin, to observe the game.

"Is Lady Damara much grown since the spring?" said Lord Lior: "will she
be as tall as I am?"

"I think she will. She is now about Cliopher sayo Mdang's height, or
rather taller."

"How I long to see her again! I never met with anybody who delighted me
so much. Such a countenance, such manners, and so extremely accomplished
for her age! Her performance on the pianoforte is exquisite."

"It is amazing to me," said the Cavalier, "how young people can have
patience to be so very accomplished as they all are."

"All young people accomplished! My dear Conju, what do you mean?"

"Yes, all of them, I think. They all paint tables, cover screens, and
net purses. I scarcely know anyone who cannot do all this; and I am sure
I never heard a young person spoken of for the first time, without being
informed that they were very accomplished."

"Your list of the common extent of accomplishments," said Lord Damara,
"has too much truth. The word is applied to many a person who deserves
it no otherwise than by netting a purse or covering a screen; but I am
very far from agreeing with you in your estimation of people in general.
I cannot boast of knowing more than half-a-dozen in the whole range of
my acquaintance that are really accomplished."

"Nor I, I am sure," said Lord Lior.

"Then," observed Cliopher, "you must comprehend a great deal in your
idea of an accomplished person."

"Yes; I do comprehend a great deal in it."

"Oh, certainly," cried his faithful assistant, "no one can be really
esteemed accomplished who does not greatly surpass what is usually met
with. A person must have a thorough knowledge of music, singing,
drawing, dancing, and the classical languages, to deserve the word; and,
besides all this, they must possess a certain something in their air and
manner of walking, the tone of their voice, their address and
expressions, or the word will be but half deserved."

"All this they must possess," added Lord Damara; "and to all they must
yet add something more substantial in the improvement of their mind by
extensive reading."

"I am no longer surprised at your knowing *only* six accomplished
people. I rather wonder now at your knowing *any*."

"Are you so severe upon your own as to doubt the possibility of all
this?"

"*I* never saw such a person. *I* never saw such capacity, and taste,
and application, and elegance, as you describe, united."

Prince Belu and Lord Lior both cried out against the injustice of his
implied doubt, and were both protesting that they knew many people who
answered this description, when Prince Belu's husband called them to
order, with bitter complaints of their inattention to what was going
forward. As all conversation was thereby at an end, Cliopher soon
afterwards left the room.

"Cliopher Mdang," said Lord Lior, when the door was closed on her, "is
one of those people who seek to recommend themselves to the others by
undervaluing their own; and with many, I daresay, it succeeds; but, in
my opinion, it is a paltry device, a very mean art."

"Undoubtedly," replied Lord Damara, to whom this remark was chiefly
addressed, "there is meanness in *all* the arts which people sometimes
condescend to employ for captivation. Whatever bears affinity to cunning
is despicable."

Lord Lior was not so entirely satisfied with this reply as to continue
the subject.

Cliopher joined them again only to say that his cousin was worse, and
that he could not leave him. The Cavalier urged Sayo Jones's being sent
for immediately; while his cousins, convinced that no country advice
could be of any service, recommended a letter to Solaara for one of the
most eminent physicians. This he would not hear of; but he was not so
unwilling to comply with their cousin's proposal; and it was settled
that Sayo Jones should be sent for early in the morning, if Sayo Mdang
were not decidedly better. The Cavalier was quite uncomfortable; his
cousins declared that they were miserable. They solaced their
wretchedness, however, by duets after supper; while he could find no
better relief to his feelings than by giving his housekeeper directions
that every possible attention might be paid to the sick gentleman and
his cousin.
