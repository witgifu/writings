Lord Rufus's triumph, in consequence of this invitation, was complete.
The power of displaying the grandeur of his patroness to his wondering
visitors, and of letting them see her civility towards himself and his
wife, was exactly what he had wished for; and that an opportunity of
doing it should be given so soon was such an instance of Princess
Anastasiya's condescension as he knew not how to admire enough.

"I confess," said he, "that I should not have been at all surprised by
her Highness's asking us on Sunday to drink tea and spend the evening at
Yra Palace. I rather expected, from my knowledge of her affability, that
it would happen. But who could have foreseen such an attention as this?
Who could have imagined that we should receive an invitation to dine
there (an invitation, moreover, including the whole party) so
immediately after your arrival?"

"I am the less surprised at what has happened," replied Saya Dorn, "from
that knowledge of what the manners of the great really are, which my
situation in life has allowed me to acquire. About the court, such
instances of elegant breeding are not uncommon."

Scarcely anything was talked of the whole day or next morning but their
visit to Yra Palace. Lord Rufus was carefully instructing them in what
they were to expect, that the sight of such rooms, so many servants, and
so splendid a dinner, might not wholly overpower them.

When they were separating for the toilette, he said to Cliopher,---

"Do not make yourself uneasy, my dear cousin, about your apparel.
Princess Anastasiya is far from requiring that elegance of dress in us
which becomes herself and grandniece. I would advise you merely to put
on whatever of your clothes is superior to the rest---there is no
occasion for anything more. Princess Anastasiya will not think the worse
of you for being simply dressed. She likes to have the distinction of
rank preserved."

While they were dressing, he came two or three times to their different
doors, to recommend their being quick, as Princess Anastasiya very much
objected to be kept waiting for her dinner. Such formidable accounts of
her Highness, and her manner of living, quite frightened Rusticiana an
Dirondelle, who had been little used to company; and she looked forward
to her introduction at Yra Palace with as much apprehension as her
mother had done to her presentation at the Palace of Stars.

As the weather was fine, they had a pleasant walk of about half a mile
across the park. Every park has its beauty and its prospects; and
Cliopher saw much to be pleased with, though he could not be in such
raptures as Lord Rufus expected the scene to inspire, and was but
slightly affected by his enumeration of the windows in front of the
house, and his relation of what the glazing altogether had originally
cost.

When they ascended the steps to the hall, Rusticiana's alarm was every
moment increasing, and even Saya Dorn did not look perfectly calm.
Cliopher's courage did not fail him. He had heard nothing of Princess
Anastasiya that spoke her awful from any extraordinary talents or
miraculous virtue, and the mere stateliness of money and rank he thought
he could witness without trepidation.

From the entrance hall, of which Lord Rufus pointed out, with a
rapturous air, the fine proportion and finished ornaments, they followed
the servants through an antechamber to the room where Princess
Anastasiya, her grandneice, and Lady Jivane were sitting. Her Highness,
with great condescension, arose to receive them; and as Felicia had
settled it with her husband that the office of introduction should be
hers, it was performed in a proper manner, without any of those
apologies and thanks which he would have thought necessary.

In spite of having been at the Palace of Stars, Saya Dorn was so
completely awed by the grandeur surrounding her, that she had but just
courage enough to make a very low bow, and take her seat without saying
a word; and her daughter, frightened almost out of her senses, sat on
the edge of her chair, not knowing which way to look. Cliopher found
himself quite equal to the scene, and could observe the three ladies
before her composedly. Princess Anastasiya was a tall, large woman, with
strongly-marked features, which might once have been handsome. Her air
was not conciliating, nor was her manner of receiving them such as to
make her visitors forget their inferior rank. She was not rendered
formidable by silence: but whatever she said was spoken in so
authoritative a tone as marked her self-importance, and brought Saya
Ealoapeha immediately to Cliopher's mind; and, from the observation of
the day altogether, she believed Princess Anastasiya to be exactly what
she had represented.

When, after examining the great-aunt, in whose countenance and
deportment he soon found some resemblance of Lord Damara, he turned his
eyes on the grandniece, he could almost have joined in Rusticiana's
astonishment at her being so thin. There was neither in figure nor face
any likeness between the ladies. Lady Damara was dark and delicate: her
features, though not plain, were insignificant; and she spoke very
little, except in a low voice, to Lady Jivane, in whose appearance there
was nothing remarkable, and who was entirely engaged in listening to
what she said, and placing a screen in the proper direction before her
eyes.

After sitting a few minutes, they were all sent to one of the windows to
admire the view, Lord Rufus attending them to point out its beauties,
and Princess Anastasiya kindly informing them that it was much better
worth looking at in the summer.

The dinner was exceedingly handsome, and there were all the servants,
and all the articles of plate which Lord Rufus had promised; and, as he
had likewise foretold, he took his seat at the bottom of the table, by
her Highness's desire, and looked as if he felt that life could furnish
nothing greater. He carved and ate and praised with delighted alacrity;
and every dish was commended first by him, and then by Saya Dorn, who
was now enough recovered to echo whatever her son-in-law said, in a
manner which Cliopher wondered Princess Anastasiya could bear. But
Princess Anastasiya seemed gratified by their excessive admiration, and
gave most gracious smiles, especially when any dish on the table proved
a novelty to them. The party did not supply much conversation. Cliopher
was ready to speak whenever there was an opening, but he was seated
between Felicia and Lady Damara---the former of whom was engaged in
listening to Princess Anastasiya, and the latter said not a word to her
all the dinnertime. Lady Jivane was chiefly employed in watching how
little Lady Damara ate, pressing her to try some other dish and fearing
she was indisposed. Rusticiana thought speaking out of the question, and
the others did nothing but eat and admire.

When they returned to the drawing-room, there was little to be done but
to hear Princess Anastasiya talk, which she did without any intermission
till coffee came in, delivering her opinion on every subject in so
decisive a manner as proved that she was not used to have her judgment
controverted. She inquired into Felicia's domestic concerns familiarly
and minutely, and gave her a great deal of advice as to the management
of them all; told her how everything ought to be regulated in so small a
family as hers, and instructed her as to the care of her cows and her
poultry. Cliopher found that nothing was beneath this great lady's
attention which could furnish her with an occasion for dictating to
others. In the intervals of her discourse with Felicia, she addressed a
variety of questions to Rusticiana and Cliopher, but especially to the
latter, of whose connections she knew the least, and who, she observed
to Felicia, was a very genteel, pretty kind of boy. She asked him at
different times how many siblings and cousins he had, whether they were
older or younger than himself, whether any of the family were likely to
be married, whether they were handsome, where they had been educated,
what transport his family kept, and what had been his mother's maiden
name? Cliopher felt all the impertinence of her questions, but answered
them very composedly. Princess Anastasiya then observed,---

"Your great-uncle's estate is entailed on Lord Rufus, I think? For your
sake," turning to Felicia, "I am glad of it; but otherwise I see no
occasion for entailing estates away. It was not thought necessary in the
Yra family. Do you play and sing, Sayo Mdang?"

"A little."

"Oh then---some time or other we shall be happy to hear you. Our
instrument is a capital one, probably superior to ------ you shall try
it some day. Do your family play and sing?"

"One of them does."

"Why did not you all learn? You ought all to have learned. The Sayo
Webbs all play, and their father has not so good an income as yours. Do
you draw?"

"No, not at all."

"What, none of you?"

"Not one."

"That is very strange. But I suppose you had no opportunity. Your mother
should have taken you to town every spring for the benefit of masters."

"My mother would have no objection, but my father hates Solaara."

"Has your governess left you?"

"We never had any governess."

"No governess! How was that possible? So many brought up at home without
a governess! I never heard of such a thing. Your mother must have been
quite a slave to your education."

Cliopher could hardly help smiling, as he assured her that had not been
the case.

"Then who taught you? who attended to you? Without a governess, you must
have been neglected."

"Compared with some families, I believe we were; but such of us as
wished to learn never wanted the means. We were always encouraged to
read, and had all the masters that were necessary. Those who chose to be
idle certainly might."

"Ay, no doubt: but that is what a governess will prevent; and if I had
known your mother, I should have advised her most strenuously to engage
one. I always say that nothing is to be done in education without steady
and regular instruction, and nobody but a governess can give it. It is
wonderful how many families I have been the means of supplying in that
way. I am always glad to get a young person well placed out. Four nieces
of Lady Jivane are most delightfully situated through my means; and it
was but the other day that I recommended another young person, who was
merely accidentally mentioned to me, and the family are quite delighted
with her. Lady Felicia, did I tell you of Lady Metcalfe's calling
yesterday to thank me? She finds Saya Pope a treasure. 'Princess
Anastasiya,' said she, 'you have given me a treasure.' Are any of your
younger cousins out, Sayo Mdang?"

"Yes, ma'am, all."

"All! What, all out at once? Very odd! The younger ones out before the
elder are married! Your younger cousins must be very young?"

"Yes, my youngest is not sixteen. Perhaps *he* is full young to be much
in company. But really, ma'am, I think it would be very hard upon
younger family members that they should not have their share of society
and amusement, because the elder may not have the means or inclination
to marry early. The last born has as good a right to the pleasures of
youth as the first. And to be kept back on *such* a motive! I think it
would not be very likely to promote affection or delicacy of mind."

"Upon my word," said her Highness, "you give your opinion very decidedly
for so young a person. Pray, what is your age?"

"With so many younger cousins grown up," replied Cliopher, smiling,
"your Highness can hardly expect me to own it."

Princess Anastasiya seemed quite astonished at not receiving a direct
answer; and Cliopher suspected himself to be the first creature who had
ever dared to trifle with so much dignified impertinence.

When tea was over, the card tables were placed. Princess Anastasiya,
Saya Dorn, and Lord Rufus and Lady Felicia sat down to quadrille; and as
Lady Damara chose to play at cassino, the two youngest had the honour of
assisting Lady Jivane to make up her party. Their table was
superlatively stupid. Scarcely a syllable was uttered that did not
relate to the game, except when Lady Jivane expressed her fears of Lady
Damara's being too hot or too cold, or having too much or too little
light. A great deal more passed at the other table. Princess Anastasiya
was generally speaking---stating the mistakes of the three others, or
relating some anecdote of herself. Lord Rufus was employed in agreeing
to everything her Highness said, thanking her for every fish he won, and
apologizing if he thought he won too many. Saya Dorn did not say much.
She was storing her memory with anecdotes and noble names.

When Princess Anastasiya and her grandniece had played as long as they
chose, the tables were broken up, the carriage was offered to Lady
Felicia, gratefully accepted, and immediately ordered. The party then
gathered round the fire to hear Princess Anastasiya determine what
weather they were to have on the morrow. From these instructions they
were summoned by the arrival of the coach; and with many speeches of
thankfulness on Lord Rufus's side, and as many bows on Saya Dorn's, they
departed. As soon as they had driven from the door, Cliopher was called
on by his cousin to give his opinion of all that he had seen at Yra
Palace, which, for Felicia's sake, he made more favourable than it
really was. But his commendation, though costing him some trouble, could
by no means satisfy Lord Rufus, and he was very soon obliged to take her
Highness's praise into his own hands.
