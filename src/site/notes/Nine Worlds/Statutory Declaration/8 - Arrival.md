They step through the gate, onto a beautiful moonlit field, not a building in sight except for a spire just peeking through some trees on the horizon. Before them is a scattering of boulders, that as Cliopher's eyes adjust to the light, resolve into a rough circle, and a path leading to it. At the end of the path, stands a group of four. Just outside the circle, respecting its sanctity, perhaps.

Cliopher offers his arm to Melissa, who takes it with a small smile. The others arrange themselves in pairs to match. He walks down the path, as if walking to the lower daïs in the Palace of Stars. Maybe this is also a palace of sorts, but of living stars. He looks up, to see if he recognises any. Perhaps, he thinks, but it is hard to be sure. The constellations of Ysthar had names, but they seemed less important than those of his home on Zunidh, so he had only learnt a couple.

The short grass tickles against the hems of their formal robes, but the path is free of stones. Not often used now, but once much cared for. Had it been before the Fall, that this path was made? Likely after. So many generations had passed on Ysthar, it was hard to comprehend.

Yet when they arrive at the four waiting, the Lord of Ysthar greets them in Shaian, “Be welcome, those who come in the name of the Lord of Zunidh.”

Cliopher bows low, a particular bow he has not used frequently. He bows to his Radiancy as Emperor, and all the court now rank below the Lords Magi. They had discussed long and hard who would do the introductions, with some tension as to if Melissa or the Lord of Ysthar had the higher rank. 

“My Lord of Ysthar,” says Melissa, “I am Melissa Damara, Princess Consort to his Radiancy.” She gives the bow of one great lord greeting another, which he returns. She then proceeds to introduce them all by name and rank, starting with Cliopher and ending with the guards.

Receiving the return introductions, Cliopher has to admit he is quite intimidated. A Prince of Fairy, a great lady, and the greatest poet of Ysthar. The poet, Will, rolls his eyes at this introduction, but doesn't dispute it. Cliopher wonders which of the many languages Will writes in, a part of him hoping he doesn't write in Shaian, only so Cliopher doesn't have to compare him to Fitzroy Angursell.

They all smile politely. Cliopher has the sneaking suspicion that the Lord of Ysthar doesn't know what to do next.

Prince Robin sighs, and looks at his lord, “How were you planning on getting back to London, Raphael?”

“Oh, right,” says the Lord of Ysthar, sheepishly, “Probably can't hail enough… transport… out here, and they'd be spooked by the spears. We'll take the shortcut then.” He mutters to himself in what Cliopher thinks is a mix of Shaian and English, clearly unhappy with his choice of words. Then he does something with his hands that Cliopher can't quite see, and they're suddenly in a city, standing in a lane between townhouses.

“Huh,” says Tanaea, “That is a beautiful and handy piece of magic, my lord.”

The Lord of Ysthar smiles, “Thank you. The house I have arranged is just down here.” He indicates what Cliopher could have sworn was a bricked up window before, but is now definitely a short tunnel. At the end, in the yellow electric light, is a courtyard with a willow tree.

The Lord of Ysthar darts through and up the three steps to the glossy red door, and throws it open. “There's not much point with locks, since only you and a few magic folk can even see it, but I left the keys on the hall table if they make you feel more comfortable.”

Cliopher notes that he isn't using titles, and seems to be addressing them all as a group. 

Prince Robin joins his lord, “Now come in out of the cold, and shed your burdens,” he says, with a playful wink. “We know you had to travel a good way to your end of the gate, so we won't pester you anymore tonight.”

“Except,” interjects the Lady Scheherezade, who has a curious accent to her Shaian that Cliopher can't quite place, “That you should know Robin provided the staff, so they'll probably be invisible to most of you, but if you just ask aloud for things they'll hear.”

Cliopher finds this idea even more disturbing than the Tower staff that move silently. However, he maintains his polite court smile, and says, “Thank you very much, my lord, your highness, my lady, for your kind consideration.”

“Oh, and let them know when you're ready to receive guests tomorrow, and they'll let us know,” says Robin, with a bow, “Good evening, all.” Then the four of them, including Will, who had said nothing the entire time, depart back out into the alley. The Zuni contingent bow as the Lord of Ysthar leaves, and as they do so, Cliopher wonders if perhaps Will didn't speak Shaian at all.

The house is not so different from the great apartments in the Palace. The first level holds reception rooms, the level up has the grand bedrooms, and then less grand bedrooms as the stairs go higher. Cliopher enjoys flicking on the switches that work the lights, somehow more exciting than clapping on mage lights, or lighting lamps. After scouting the main floor with Rhodin, Cliopher returns to the others.

In the front parlour, Tanaea declares the glass rectangle, that Cliopher had taken to be some kind of art piece, to be a 'television', and goes hunting for the wand that operates it. Cliopher looks at Melissa, who has slumped onto one of the sofas. Kawa and Aioru have sat down opposite.

“Can I get you anything, Melissa?” Cliopher offers, “A drink? Or would you like to retire for the night?”

“Mmm?” she says, looking around, “It was, what, nearly the sixth bell when we left? What time is it here?”

Kawa and Cliopher look around, but there's no obvious clock in the room. Then Tanaea says “Ah-ha!” and produces a black bar covered in buttons from a drawer under the table. She presses one of the buttons, and the glass frame comes to life. A man in a white coat is very enthusiastically yelling, and waving a number of cooking pans about. It's rather loud, and Melissa cringes at the noise.

Tanaea squints at the bar, muttering in English as she reads the labels. She manages to silence the man, to everyone's relief. Then she brings up some kind of table, and with a satisfied smile says, “It's nearly eleven o'clock. They use a twelve or twenty-four-hour clock here.” She points at the point on the television, which Cliopher realises from his studies does indeed have the time in numbers.

“Well,” says Cliopher, “I suppose a late dinner wouldn't go amiss then?”

Kawa and Tanaea both look like they are listening intently. Cliopher assumes it must be something magical, because the television is now silently showing a sports game.

“Is there anything in particular you'd like, Melissa?” asks Kawa, “The chef says they don't have much information on modern Zuni cuisine, but they do know most of the late Imperial dishes.”

“I'm supposed to eat lots of red meat and milk,” says Melissa, with a small sigh, “But I just want a mango smoothie.” There's a puff of coppery dust, and a tall glass appears on the table next to her, filled with a thick yellow drink, and a straw poking out of it.

“They say they've fortified it?” says Kawa, and then clarifies, “Ah, they've put some kind of medicine in it so it has what you need.”

“Thank you, that's very kind,” says Melissa, and takes a sip, “It's delicious.”

The guards, apparently satisfied with their inspection of the house, join them in the front room. Ser Rhodin salutes Melissa.

“And what do you have to report of the house, Ser Rhodin?” she asks.

“The back door seems to lead to a place where it is daytime. I recommend not exploring it,” he says.

Everyone nods. Getting lost on a strange world where you only speak a tiny bit of one of the languages sounds like a bad idea.

“Could I request some sandwiches?” asks Aioru, “Just whatever you have to hand is fine.”

There is a pause, and then a large platter of sandwiches and wraps appears on the table. Next to it, a stack of small plates. Then some carafes of drinks, and glasses.

“Perfect!” says Cliopher, “I see you've done catering before.” It looks remarkably like what the kitchens would send to a lunch meeting. They all descend on it.

Aioru looks slightly worried. Cliopher shoots his young protégé an inquiring look, but Aioru shakes his head. “I'm just not used to so much _obvious_ magic. And I'm not sure what is magical or just Ystharian technology.”

“Probably assume its technology,” says Tanaea, “There are very few magic workers of any sort here. But don't you take the mage lights in the Palace for granted, or the magic stoves?”

Aioru gives this point genuine consideration. “I suppose I do,” he says, “And the binding of the Fens, and the Lights.”

“The Lights are a far greater work of magic than hiding a house or moving food,” says Kawa, with a smile.

“Rhodin,” says Cliopher, half-way through a really excellent grilled vegetable wrap, “How many bedrooms are there?”

Rhodin smiles, in the way Cliopher is used to from his recommendations for new food carts. “More than I think would fit from the outside,” he says, “The next floor has four bedrooms and a nursery, the floor above has five.”

Oginu blinks at Rhodin, “We get our own bedrooms, sir?”

“There's plenty of space on this floor, so I don't think we'll need them for meeting rooms. So yes, you get your own bedroom,” says Rhodin, “Don't get too excited.”

Oginu is, very obviously, thrilled. Cliopher wasn't sure if the guards were keen to come on this trip, it was a long time to be away. Hopefully the novelty of having a room of their own helps.


---

The next day is Melissa's first appointment at the hospital. It's in easy walking distance, and Lady Scheherezade is coming with them to translate. Also, Cliopher hopes, to offer some support. Doulas are still one of the top bullet points he has to ask the staff at the hospital about.

“The hospital services are all free, Lady Scheherezade?” asks Cliopher, “Is that a recent development?”

“Oh, call me Sherry, both of you,” she says with a smile, “It depends on how you define recent. Within the past hundred years.”

“Free even to non-citizens?” asks Melissa, “Payment shouldn't be a problem.”

Sherry laughs, “Well, if they were to charge you, they'd need a way to send you a bill and a department at the hospital to collect it.  Since they don't, they can't. Now, they _do_ accept donations.”

“Of course,” says Melissa immediately, “Can you help us find out what a suitably generous donation would be? I hope we don't swamp your money-changers.”

“Oh, your currency has been stable for long enough, it shouldn't be a problem,” says Sherry, and Cliopher positively _beams_. Reforming the financial systems had been one of his earlier tasks, and not enough people truly appreciated the benefits of fiat currency and decimalisation.

“Today we will be meeting with a doctor?” asks Cliopher, as they turn the corner.

“A midwife,” says Sherry, and Cliopher sees Melissa relax every-so-slightly, “Unless you end up needing surgery, most pregnancies are handled by midwives.”

They arrive to an arched glass front, the paint on the woodwork slightly worn. Inside is one of the automatic doors, which makes Cliopher smile. Somehow the idea that it is technology rather than magic operating the doors makes it more exciting, even though he notices they worked in almost the same way as the automatic doors in the Palace.

Joining a queue at a reception desk, Cliopher feels much more comfortable than he expected. Hospitals have always made him slightly nervous. Yet somehow the activity feels more like his own offices, and less like the imposing grandeur of the old Imperial hospitals. Perhaps it is the lack of marble columns, and the slightly mismatched posters tacked on the walls. He wonders if any of them are reminding staff to not hold doors open for strangers.

Sherry leads them down a corridor to a waiting room. They sit there, with a lot of other expectant mothers, and one expectant father. A television in the corner is showing some kind of puppet theatre production, with lots of songs. It is hard to know exactly what is happening, with so little English, but Cliopher notices a few words like 'today' and 'thank you', which is pleasing.

Melissa sits in the kind of practiced calm that Cliopher had always thought admirable, until he had realised that it was as much a mask as her court one. Sherry pulls something from her bag, which looks like a tiny television-and-wand in one. When she sees him watching her, she smiles.

“Just letting Robin know not to wait for us for lunch,” she says, “You never know what the queue at the hospital is going to be on any given day. It seems today is busy.”

“Prince Robin and not my Lord of Ysthar?” asks Cliopher, carefully.

“You can just call them Robin and Raphael,” says Sherry, “Only Robin's servants bother with titles, and honestly it's a bit strange. But anyway, no, not Raphael. He never remembers to charge his…” She thinks for a moment, clearly trying to find the right word, and then shrugs, “_Phone_. There's no word in Shaian. And translating it directly as _far sound_ or _portable sound_ feels weird.”

“_Phone_,” repeats Cliopher, “A device for spoken communication over distance, that can be carried around. Requires regular charging to be useful. But also works silently?”

Sherry grins, “Yes, you can send short messages. We should get you one, you're clearly more adept at this than Raphael.”

A portable postal system! Cliopher doesn't want to admit how desperately he wants to know everything about it. Or how embarrassed he is at being told he could be better at _anything_ than the famous Lord of Ysthar. “That would be very welcome, thank you,” he says, “Are they difficult to use? How do they work?”

The wait lasts for the first play on the television, and the next one. Cliopher doesn't notice, he is too busy asking Sherry everything she knows about _phones_.

Eventually a midwife calls out, “Melissa Damara?”

The three of them stand up and walk over to her. She looks between them, smiling, and gestures down the hall. Cliopher is happy he understood “please” of the words she says, and from context guesses they are going to a more private room. 

She leads them to a small office, with a wheeled bed and a few chairs. There is a window onto a courtyard, and more of the same posters.

Washing her hands and nodding at Melissa, the midwife asks a question.

Sherry begins a rapid explanation in English, which is far to fast for Cliopher to catch any of, apart from his own name and Melissa's. The midwife smiles at them all, and nods along, asking some clarifying questions. Eventually, Sherry turns to Melissa.

“Can I confirm your name and date of birth?” translates Sherry, as the midwife looks down at the clipboard she's holding.

“Melissa Damara,” is the easy answer to the first part of the question. After that, Cliopher and Melissa look at each other. Giving a date in the old Imperial calendar isn't going to be much use. Technically, it is something over five thousand years ago, by Ysthar timelines. 

“I'm not sure how old I am, exactly,” says Melissa, eventually, “I think maybe forty-eight.”

This, unsurprisingly, takes rather more time to translate. Melissa, to Cliopher's surprise, is used to having people translate for her. 

“Do you have any medical records with you? I understand you've just arrived? And when was your last menstrual period?” asks Sherry.

She nods at the midwife, and pulls out the folder of notes from August. They'd done their best to translate them ahead of time. “I'm thirty-four weeks pregnant,” she says. Sherry translates back, and the midwife nods again. 

“Would you be the father?” the midwife asks of Cliopher. He is completely blindsided by this question. It is, he concedes, perfectly logical that a pregnant woman would attend an appointment with the baby's father. It just had never occurred to him that someone might draw that conclusion about _him_.

“Cliopher is a close family friend,” says Melissa, “The father, my husband, is unable to travel to London.”

This statement _also_ takes Cliopher by surprise. He gives Melissa a smile that he hopes is thankful and encouraging, rather than confused. _Friend_ seemed like it was out of reach. _Close family friend_… Melissa's words, many months ago now, about how she didn't want to come between Cliopher and his Radiancy come flooding back. He tries to ignore them, it is not the time. The midwife doesn't ask any further questions about their relationship for now, and Cliopher is relieved.

Together, they go through the notes, which cover the pregnancy and Melissa's health. The midwife asks if anything runs in the family, "Twins, heart conditions, diabetes, any cancers?"

Melissa looks slightly frozen, and says in her formal court voice, "None of those, no."

Cliopher offers, "Her uncle and cousin probably had some kind of mental health condition, but it was never diagnosed." The midwife jots a short note, but doesn't clarify the point.

After going through the papers, the midwife directs Melissa to be weighed, takes her temperature, and blood pressure. 

"Now," says the midwife, "We'll also need some blood and urine, just to check you're all healthy. The collection room is down the hall." She turns to Cliopher, "It'll only be five minutes, you can stay here."

Cliopher flicks his eyes to Melissa check if she is okay with that, but she smiles back and nods. So Sherry and Melissa join the midwife, and Cliopher is left alone in the room. He carefully resists the urge to inspect everything in it, he can't read most of the labels anyway.

As promised, they're back very quickly. Melissa says to Cliopher, with a smile, "She asked me if I felt safe with you here."

Cliopher knows why those kinds of questions are asked. He's sat in enough health department reviews to know that pregnant people are at a higher risk of domestic violence. It's sad to know Ysthar has the same problem, but he's hopeful that awareness and screening will help. He doesn't say any of this to Melissa, who probably already knows it from her own provincial reports.

"I'll come to anything you want, Melissa. Or stay out of anything," he says, returning the smile.

The midwife asks Melissa to get up onto the bed. More measurements are taken, and the midwife feels Melissa's stomach. Finally, the midwife uses a device to look at the baby _inside_ her stomach.

“Would you like copies of the pictures?” she asks, “And do you want to know the sex?”

It blows Cliopher's mind that technology can do either of those things. Melissa looks nervous, and Cliopher grabs her hand and squeezes it.

“Yes please, to both,” she says, very quietly.

There's a lot of button pushing, and wiggling the stick around in jelly on Melissa's stomach. “It looks to be a girl,” says the midwife, “But there's something… I just need to call the doctor to confirm. Baby is sitting a little far around, and I want a second pair of eyes. I'll be back in a moment.”

She disappears, still wearing a professional smile. But they all know that needing a second opinion is rarely good news. Cliopher wants to offer reassurance, but he, Melissa, and his Radiancy all know that a _live_ child isn't guaranteed to be a _healthy_ child. He squeezes Melissa's hand.

“My mother had more than twenty confirmed pregnancies,” she says, “Only two lived past the first day.” Her voice sounds flat, but Cliopher knows that's because she's trying to hold in her emotions.

“That might be something the midwife should know,” says Sherry, sounding surprised herself, “Do you know why?”

Melissa looks at Cliopher, he can see she is trembling. “Melissa's parents were too closely related,” says Cliopher. He doesn't mention this is even more true of Melissa and his Radiancy. 

“And,” says Melissa, closing her eyes, “My uncle ordered the killing of any obviously disabled baby.”

She is really shaking now, and Cliopher moves closer to put an arm around her shoulder. It's difficult with the equipment around, but she turns her head into his chest, trying to breathe more evenly. “That won't happen now,” says Cliopher, trying to sound soothing, “Nobody will take your little girl from you like that.”

Melissa takes a deep breath. “Girl?” she asks, looking up at Cliopher.

“At least until they're old enough to tell us otherwise,” he replies, with a smile.

Sherry says, “Females are a bit harder to spot on the pictures than males, because you're trying to confirm something is _missing_. It's not unknown for the baby to pop out and surprise you, so don't go out buying everything pink and throw out all your boy names.”

Cliopher is slightly baffled that colours are gendered on Ysthar (or is it just in London?). He misses Melissa tensing again.

“Did Artorin give you a list of names, Cliopher?” she asks.

Of course, by Imperial Family tradition, the Emperor names all the children. He had forgotten, and they hadn't discussed names at all. It's likely that his Radiancy hasn't either, and will want Melissa to name the child. But it would be unwise to presume.

“No,” says Cliopher, “We should send messages to Zunidh today reporting our safe arrival. Perhaps the Lord of—Raphael would be kind enough to seal a letter to his Radiancy for you.”

“I'm sure that won't be a problem,” says Sherry, “You can write as long a letter as you would like.”

The midwife returns with a doctor, who smiles at Melissa, and picks up the wand.

“They're a… specialist at these kinds of pictures of babies,” says Sherry, “Wow that was hard to translate. Are you okay with them taking a look? And should I tell them what you said about your parents?”

Melissa assents, and Sherry takes a deep breath. Both the midwife and the doctor gives Melissa looks of deep sympathy, as she grips tightly to Cliopher's hand.

"How closely related?" asks the midwife.

"First cousins on both sides," says Cliopher, the answer filed somewhere from his Service examinations, "And the family had a tradition of cousin marriage."

The specialist and the midwife both nod, the midwife scribbling notes down. Then the doctor takes over the baby picture device. 

They narrate what they're seeing as they go. Spine, head, eyes, legs, two tiny hands balled into fists. Finally, they get to the point of concern. “It looks like there is a problem with baby's heart,” translates Sherry, “It's not likely to be fatal, but baby will likely need surgery soon after they're born.”

Cliopher is sure his face looks as anxious as Melissa's. Surgery? Should they have stayed on Zunidh where they could have had magical intervention? But then, they wouldn't have known without these pictures. “Do you know what is wrong? And why?” asks Melissa.

“Ah…” says Sherry, as the doctor explains, “How much do you know about the anatomy of the human heart?”

“There's four chambers, and two sides?” says Melissa, “It's been a long time I'm afraid, I don't think I could name them anymore.”

Sherry nods, “There's a hole between the two sides, and some parts are bigger or smaller than they should be. It can make baby prone to turning blue and fainting, and heart attacks. It's not clear why in every case, but it can be inherited, or from infections during pregnancy.”

“But it can be fixed?” asks Cliopher.

The doctor nods, “Ninety-five percent success. But when baby is born there will need to be a lot of people there, to help and make sure everything is okay.”

It's better to know and be prepared, thinks Cliopher, than be surprised. Still, it's hard news.

They wrap up the picture-taking part of the appointment, and the doctor leaves.

“Did you have any particular preferences for the birth?” asks the midwife.

Melissa, now back to her usual calm and polite court face, says, “I'm sorry, I wasn't aware there were options?”

“Well, sometimes the baby chooses for us,” says the midwife, “But we have facilities for water birth, we also have different kinds of pain relief. You would also be eligible for a planned surgical birth, since you have a high-risk pregnancy. The consultant might recommend it even, you'll find out at the next appointment.” 

She collects some flyers from a little stand on the desk, and hands them to Melissa. Three of them have happy mothers on the front, one has a happy baby.

“I can help translate them,” says Sherry, “They're about the birthing suites and medications and so on.”

The rest of the appointment goes smoothly, and they book in for another appointment in for next week. Melissa clutches the blurry black-and-white pictures of the baby tightly.
