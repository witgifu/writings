You woke slowly.

You felt the same soft sheets, but not quite the same position. Your mouth was dry, and your eyes itchy. You felt slightly sore all over, although you could not yet reason why. You could feel the rocking motion, a ship on the waves.

The wind roared. Under that, the sound of wood creaking, of ropes and sails. Under that, the distant sound of sailors at work. The sound of footsteps, nearby. A voice, but you had no sense of what the words meant. Slightly familiar, but not a friend.

You opened your eyes. You were in the same room, the same bed. And here was the same priest-wizard, looking over you.

"Good to see you awake, my lord," they said, "We'll be making land soon."

How much time had passed since you had taken the drug? It was hard to tell, but you guessed likely a day. You were thirsty, and hungry, and sore.

"Water?" you asked, your voice cracking. It was probably a side-effect of the drug. You hoped they wouldn't give it to you again.

"Of course, my lord," said the priest-wizard, a smile in his voice. He left, closing the door behind him. You could hear the lock click.

I suddenly remembered where I was and why (not that I really knew why). I needed to plan my escape. It was hard though, with my head still so foggy. Would they drug the food? Probably. But I also couldn't escape if I was too weak from thirst and hunger.

*So, Savelin,* you thought, *we meet again.*

*My name is Fitzroy. Where have you been, Chantling?* I asked.

*Watching, waiting, in case you needed me,* you thought.

*I don't need you,* I replied tersely, *My friends will come and get me out, or I'll escape*.

*Maybe,* you thought, and I recoiled in horror, *but I think it's probably best if I handle the Imperials, yes?*

I didn't reply, but I settled back. You had always been better at manners, at keeping us out of trouble back... before. When we were a child.

Sitting up seemed like it would be the next step. You slid your legs out of the bed, and pushed your hands against the mattress. Your body felt like lead. It seemed the greatest weight in the world to pry it up a hand-span.

You scrabbled one hand out for the table. You managed to shuffle over and lean against the wall more than sit up. But it was up. And you weren't wrapped in spells, just sore and stiff.

The priest-wizard opened the door, and let in a sailor carrying a tray. The sailor dumped the tray on the table without a word, and hurried back out. Was he afraid of you? Of the priest-wizard? Or was he just busy, and had been pulled from whatever he was supposed to be doing to carry the tray?

The tray smelt inviting. A bowl of thin cabbage soup and two steamed buns. You must have been starving for that to smell good. And for all the "my lord" you were getting from the priest-wizard, surely even the greenest sailors were getting fed better than this.

You reached out a shaking hand for the spoon. Hoping the soup wasn't too hot, as you might end up with a fair amount spilt on your hands. You pulled the tray towards yourself, and began taking cautious sips of the soup.

The soup was only just warm, and barely salted. But it felt so nice in your dry mouth, and your empty stomach.

The priest-wizard observed you for a few more spoonfuls.

"We'll be docking in an hour or so," he said, "So don't eat too slowly, my lord. We'll need to have you ready to disembark."

Then he also left.

You ate your soup and buns. The buns were room-temperature, and a bit chewy. Neither appeared to be drugged or enchanted, but it would be hard to tell while you were still reeling from the effects of the last dose.

I went to send out a tendril of magic, I reached — but there was nothing there. I had felt like this once before, in Ixsaa, a long time ago. A kind of _slaurigh_. It must have been mixed in with the drug.

How did they know I had magic? Perhaps it was included for some other reason, as a relaxant, or to counter some other side-effect. No matter why, they had disabled my magic. I had no weapon now, no self-defence, very little chance I could escape.

I tried not to panic. I was going to escape however I could as soon as we made land. Maybe even jump into the harbour. Serendipity had always been my ally, and would surely come again. Even without my magic.

---

The priest-wizard returned with clothes, thank heavens. Fine, soft, white linen. You tried not to think about why you were being garbed in all white. They had to help you put it on, as the remnants of the drug were making you unstable. Trews, tunic, under robe, over robe, mantle. Then on top of it all a long hooded cloak that shadowed your face and covered your entire body. All you could see was directly in front of you, like a carriage horse with blinkers. 

When you were led out from the cabin and onto the deck, you expected to see the bustle of the port. The view was blocked by the Emperor's Dogs. Lining the ship's railing, and the wide, railed, gangplank. Onto the pier. Had they sent every battle wizard in Astandalas to fetch you?

I could have bested one, if I had my magic, inside the borders of the Empire. Maybe two. I could perhaps have escaped from as many as five. Here were at least fifty. No wonder we had travelled so fast.

Cowed, you staggered slowly off the ship. You had to lean on the priest-wizard. The battle mages formed up like an honour guard, or a security cordon. You could see nobody past them.

Off the pier, you were hustled into something more closely resembling an omnibus than a coach, except that it was windowless, and horseless. Another decadent and ostentatious use of magic.

Then the door was shut, and you were trapped again. No serendipity, no friends had come for me. It had been over in such a short time.

Inside, the coach (for want of a better term) was lit with soft mage lights. They glinted off gilt edges everywhere. The priest-wizard led you to a couch, and you collapsed onto it. Partially because you were weak even from such a short walk. Partially from fear and despair.

There must have been some signal that you missed. A moment later the coach began to move. Slowly at first, presumably as it navigated the crowded working waterfront. Then the pace increased rapidly, as we reached what must be the Imperial Highway.

Would the magic or wizards that powered the coach need to stop at night? Surely there would be enough mage lights on the outside that the dark itself was not a problem for whoever was driving.

I could only hope they would stop.

From your slumped and shaking position, hood covering part of your face, you looked around. White walls, black wood floor, gilt trim.

The priest-wizard had disappeared, presumably to beyond the white curtain. 

Imperial yellow fabric couch. Deep, and wide. Covered liberally with cushions. White cushions, embroidered with gold bees. Black cushions embroidered with gold sun-in-glory motifs. An imperial yellow cushion with a tapestry cover.

One glance at it and you shoved it on to the floor. If the colour scheme and lack of windows had left you any room for doubt as to who this carriage was for, that tapestry filled it. 

What did your cousin Shallyr, now surely Emperor Shallyr, need you for? Had he murdered Melissa, and now needed you for a public heir?

Between the shaking of the carriage, the lingering effects of the drugs, and the fear, it was unsurprising that you lost the meagre food they had given you. All you managed to do was shuffle yourself to the edge of the couch so you could vomit on the floor rather than on yourself.

It was hard to tell how much time passed, and how much distance. The nausea came in waves, although you had nothing left but stomach bile to purge.

Eventually, the priest-wizard returned. He said nothing, seeing you sprawled across the couch and dry heaving onto the floor. You looked up, but he didn't acknowledge you, just disappeared behind the curtain again.

A maid came, on bare feet, with a bucket and a rag. Her movements reminded you of a frightened woodland creature, a hare, or a bird. You shuffled further onto the couch and out of her way. She knelt to clean your mess, her eyes flicking to your hands regularly.

She was efficient, and thorough. As she stood to leave, you whispered, "Please, Saya, could I have some water?"

Her eyes flicked to your hands again, and she curtsied deeply. Then she stepped back behind the curtain. I lay where I was, breathing heavily.

The priest-wizard returned, some time later. Followed by the maid carting a tray. 

"We are travelling as fast as we may, my lord," he said, as if I wanted to reach the destination. "But hopefully we can ease your discomfort a little. I had hoped to explain things, but it's see if you can keep something down first."

*Ah,* I thought, *will it be drugs or spells this time?*

You tried to nod, but only managed a small movement before the nausea forced you to close your eyes.

Then what felt like a cool sheet fell on you. Tucked around you, from the sweat on your forehead to the tips of your toes. It sank into your skin, and to your relief, pushed the nausea away.

"There you go," said the priest-wizard, and nodded to the maid.

The tray hooked into the wall, and when you managed to roll over it formed something like a table. One hang gripping the edge, you pulled yourself up, the maid shoving cushions behind your back. The cloak hood fell away from your head.

The priest-wizard watched you closely, you tried to ignore him. Dry crackers, water, and a small unlabelled vial. Well, given the state of your stomach, plain crackers was probably wise.

You sipped at the water and nibbled on a cracker. It, unlike the soup, had sufficient salt. It didn't taste drugged, and the priest-wizard didn't seem to mind yet that you were ignoring the vial.

"My lord," began the priest-wizard, "My superiors are planning to tell you themselves, but I think it prudent to give you some time to absorb the information first." He took a deep breath, and then continued. "Emperor Eritanyr died three days ago. As did his son and heir Prince Shallyr." 

He paused, as if he was expecting you might be upset by this news. Or might not know who these people were. I was, in fact, gleeful; you were politely interested. There would be few in the Empire who would morn the loss of those two men, perhaps only some who had enjoyed the opportunities for grift that their appetites had provided.

"So we have had to recall you, the Marwn, and second heir," finished the priest-wizard. As if that made everything perfectly clear and obvious.

Marwn you knew, but perhaps did not fully understand. That was what you had been, or what I was supposed to have been, locked in the tower. The hidden counterweight, balancing the centre. Unknown, unknowable, stolen and perched at the edge of the Empire. 

_Second heir_ was new.

Even with your control and training, you flinched once the words truly reached you. Emperor gone, heir gone, second heir recalled. Not for some secret arcane ritual, for perhaps the most public ritual in the entire Empire.

"Try and get some sleep, my lord," said the priest-wizard, "you will need it. The medicine should help." Then he left, taking the maid with him.

You were left with water, crackers, drugs, and horror. Could they force you onto the throne? Could they crown you against your will? If the past few days were anything to go by, they absolutely could.

If you were not careful, if you were not rescued, you could face decades in a drugged or spelled daze. Surfacing only when the Ouranatha, or your Council, or the military, needed you as a prop in a way that required you to walk and maybe talk.

Should you take the drug now?

I did not want to take it, I wanted my magic back as soon as possible. If I was going to be able to escape, having access to my magic would make everything much easier.

You were more circumspect. If you were seen to be obedient and good, perhaps they would be less careful around you, and you might have more opportunities to escape. More time when you were not closely watched, more time when not enchanted or drugged.

We compromised, and decided to only take the drugs if sleep proved impossible. Thankfully, it came easily.
